# Chapter 7

## Energy

If there is one area of life where people consistently lie to themselves, it's their health. I know because I did it year after year. Very few people wake up one morning and decide to neglect themselves. It happens gradually. One skipped workout. One poor meal. One excuse. One ordinary day at a time. Then one day they look in the mirror and wonder, "How did I get here?"

I know exactly how that feels.

A few years before my lowest point, I had completed an Ironman triathlon: a 2.4-mile swim, a 112-mile bike ride, and then a marathon. At one point in my life, I had the discipline to finish one of the most physically demanding endurance events in the world. Then life happened. Excuses happened. Alcohol happened. Convenience happened. Before I understood what was going on, I had gained nearly fifty pounds. The athlete disappeared. The energy disappeared. The confidence disappeared. When I stood in front of the mirror, I didn't just dislike how I looked. I disliked who I was becoming.

There's an important distinction there. Energy isn't about appearance. It's about capacity — the capacity to fully show up for the people who matter most. During that season, I wasn't doing that. I had become a liability. That's a difficult word to write. It's also the truth. If I didn't have the energy to be fully present for my wife, I was a liability. If I didn't have the energy to engage with my daughters, I was a liability. If my health limited my ability to serve the people around me, I was a liability.

Everything begins with energy. Not because it's more important than the other pillars, but because it supports every one of them. You only get one body. One. There isn't another one waiting. There isn't a replacement. That reality deserves our attention. Most people don't think much about their health until it begins disappearing. Then comes the diagnosis. The injury. The wake-up call. I don't say that to shame anyone. I've lived it. I've stood in front of the mirror making excuses. I've convinced myself tomorrow would somehow be different. I've believed convenience would eventually produce the same results as discipline. It never did. Eventually, I stopped lying to myself, and that's when everything began changing.

One of the greatest lies I used to tell myself was, "I don't have time." Today, I don't believe that anymore. We all have the same twenty-four hours. The real question isn't whether we have time. The question is whether we've made our health a priority. That's uncomfortable. It's also true.

People often think they need expensive equipment. The perfect gym. The newest workout. The latest diet. The truth is much simpler than that. You don't need perfection. You need consistency. I haven't had a gym membership in a long while, and I've discovered that remarkable results usually come from remarkably common habits repeated consistently. That's the difficult part. Not knowledge. Consistency. Most people already know what healthy looks like. Eat better. Move more. Sleep more. Drink water. Repeat. The basics aren't exciting, but they're effective. And the basics always work.

One of the biggest shifts in my thinking came when I stopped training to look better and started training to become more capable. That may sound like a small change. For me, it changed everything. I stopped asking, "How do I look?" and started asking, "What am I capable of?" Could I carry someone to safety if I had to? Could I sprint toward danger instead of away from it? Could I physically protect the people I love? Those questions mattered far more to me than visible abs ever would.

Capability creates confidence. Not arrogance — real confidence, the kind that comes from preparation rather than appearance. That understanding completely changed my relationship with fitness. I stopped chasing mirrors and started building capacity. Because when your energy improves, everything else has an opportunity to improve with it. Your patience. Your relationships. Your work. Your leadership. Your attitude. Everything. That's why Energy became the first pillar. It isn't the most important one; it's the multiplier. The stronger your energy becomes, the greater your capacity to strengthen every other part of your life.

There was another lesson running quietly taught me: your mind usually quits long before your body does. Every runner knows that voice. "Slow down." "Skip today." "Nobody will know." "You've done enough." Sometimes those thoughts are wise. Most of the time, they're just uncomfortable. Learning the difference changed my life. The body is remarkably resilient, and so is the human spirit. Both deserve far more credit than we usually give them. That doesn't mean ignoring pain. It means refusing to surrender every time discomfort shows up.

Growth almost always introduces itself as discomfort. That's true physically. It's true emotionally. It's true spiritually. Avoiding discomfort doesn't protect your future. It weakens it. That's why I've learned to welcome certain kinds of discomfort — less because I enjoy suffering than because I know who usually waits on the other side of it. A stronger husband. A stronger father. A stronger friend. A stronger leader. A stronger man.

One of the biggest changes in my own life happened before I ever became faster. It happened when I finally became teachable. I met Paul Duncan while I was building my running streak. Paul is a former professional triathlete, and when I first met him, he was already more than seven hundred days into his own running streak. That immediately caught my attention — not the number itself, but the reason behind it. He wasn't chasing miles. He was setting an example. His son was watching. That resonated deeply with me. Mine were watching too.

The more time I spent learning from Paul, the more I understood that I didn't really know how to run. I knew how to work hard. Those aren't the same thing. Paul became my coach as I prepared for my first one-hundred-kilometer ultramarathon, and the very first thing he started teaching me wasn't speed. It was patience. Heart-rate training. At first, I honestly thought something was wrong. My training pace became painfully slow — slow enough that I felt like people walking their dogs might pass me. Everything inside me wanted to run faster. Paul kept saying the same thing: "Trust the process." The goal wasn't running fast. The goal was building the engine. The foundation. He taught me that if my heart rate climbed too high, I wasn't building endurance anymore. I was only teaching my body to survive.

That lesson fascinated me. Some of the hardest runs I've ever completed weren't hill repeats. They weren't sprint workouts. They weren't races. They were slow runs — runs that required tremendous discipline because every instinct told me to speed up. Instead, I had to intentionally slow down. I had to pay attention. I had to remain patient. I had to trust that doing the fundamentals well would eventually produce the results I wanted. It required concentration. Humility. Discipline.

Now I can see that Paul wasn't merely teaching me how to become a better runner. He was teaching me how to build a better life. Foundations before speed. Basics before breakthroughs. Patience before performance. Those lessons extended far beyond running. They changed the way I approached everything else.

One of the greatest surprises during that season was discovering that slower eventually became faster. At first, that made absolutely no sense to me. Everything inside me believed progress meant pushing harder. Running faster. Working harder. Doing more. Paul kept reminding me that endurance isn't built through constant intensity. It's built through consistency. The better my aerobic base became, the more efficiently my body learned to move. Little by little, the pace naturally improved. I hadn't forced it; the foundation had become stronger.

That lesson has stayed with me far beyond running. How often do we chase speed before building the foundation? We want a stronger marriage, but we skip the small conversations. We want financial freedom, but ignore the daily habits that create it. We want deeper faith, but neglect the quiet minutes that strengthen it. We want confidence, but avoid the everyday promises that slowly build trust. Life keeps teaching the same lesson. The basics matter. They always have. They always will.

Running also stripped away distractions in a way nothing else ever had. There were no emails. No meetings. No television. No notifications. Just me, the road, my thoughts, and God. Some mornings the road was flat. Other mornings it climbed relentlessly. Sometimes I ran on smooth pavement, sometimes on rocky trails, sometimes on gravel roads, sometimes into a brutal West Texas headwind. Some mornings the sun felt incredible. Other afternoons the heat seemed unbearable. Every run looked different.

Just like life.

Some seasons ask you to sprint. Some ask you to climb. Some ask nothing more than that you keep putting one foot in front of the other. No two runs are identical, and no two lives are either. That's one of the reasons comparison slowly disappeared from my thinking. I wasn't running someone else's course. I was running mine.

One morning another important truth landed. At some point, your one hundred percent may only look like forty percent compared to yesterday. That's okay. If forty percent is honestly everything you have today, then forty percent becomes one hundred percent. Progress doesn't always look impressive. Sometimes it just looks consistent. Seeing that removed a tremendous amount of pressure from my life. Perfection creates anxiety. Consistency creates hope. And hope isn't wishful thinking. Hope is confidence built one promise at a time.

As my confidence quietly grew, another opportunity appeared. Someone asked if I wanted to run a one-hundred-kilometer ultramarathon. Sixty-two miles. People immediately asked the obvious question: "Why?" The answer surprised even me. I wasn't trying to prove anything. I wasn't chasing another medal. I wanted another opportunity to become.

Training for sixty-two miles required everything I had been learning. Patience. Discipline. Humility. Preparation. Trusting the process instead of chasing immediate results. Race day finally arrived. Unlike a triathlon, there weren't massive crowds. No loud music. No grand production. Just runners, trails, rugged terrain, and miles of quiet. Oddly enough, that was exactly what I needed. The race wasn't about excitement. It was about endurance. One step. Then another. Hour after hour. Everything I had practiced during those slow heart-rate runs suddenly made sense. The foundation carried me. Not speed. Not adrenaline. The basics.

When I finally crossed the finish line after sixty-two miles, I wasn't thinking about the medal. I wasn't thinking about the time. I was thinking about my family. A few minutes later, my daughters walked over and placed the finisher's medal around my neck. That meant more to me than finishing the race itself. Months earlier they had watched Dad leave the house for another routine run. Now they were standing at the finish line. Not celebrating a race — celebrating the man I was becoming. That's a moment I'll never forget. Not because of the medal. Because of who put it there.

The ultramarathon didn't convince me I could run sixty-two miles. It reminded me that ordinary disciplines, practiced consistently over time, quietly prepare you for extraordinary moments. Life works exactly the same way. The finish line only reveals what the quiet days have already built.

As I thought about those sixty-two miles, another truth quietly settled into my heart. The ultramarathon hadn't changed me.

It revealed me.

People often ask where confidence comes from. For years I would have answered, "Winning." Today, I answer very differently. Confidence comes from keeping promises. One promise. Then another. Then another. Eventually, you gather enough evidence that you stop questioning whether you'll show up. You already know you will. That kind of confidence can't be purchased. It can't be borrowed. It can't be faked. It has to be earned.

One ordinary day at a time.

One of the greatest gifts running ever gave me wasn't endurance. It was self-trust. For longer than I'd like to admit, I had taught myself that my promises were negotiable. I'll start Monday. I'll begin next month. I'll get serious after this trip. Every broken promise quietly taught me another lesson: "You don't really mean what you say." The running streak began teaching me something completely different: "You can trust yourself again." That may be one of the greatest transformations I've experienced. It wasn't that I became stronger physically. It was that I became dependable — to myself, to my family, to the people around me.

Dependability is a quiet kind of strength. It doesn't usually make headlines. It just keeps showing up. The older I get, the more I admire dependable people — people who quietly do what they said they would do. Not when it's exciting. Not when it's convenient. Every day. Rain. Wind. Heat. Travel. Busy schedules. They keep showing up anyway. That's exactly what my father modeled. That's exactly what my grandfather modeled. Now I finally understood what I had been watching my entire childhood. They weren't extraordinary because they were talented. They were extraordinary because they were faithful. Faithful people quietly build extraordinary lives. Understanding that changed the way I looked at success. Success stopped being something I chased. It became someone I was becoming.

The more I reflected on my own road, the more something else came into focus. Running had never been about running. Not really. It had become a daily reminder that excuses no longer controlled my life. Every morning I laced up my shoes, I was making the same statement: "Excuses don't get a vote today." That's powerful. Not because of the miles. Because of the identity. One ordinary decision repeated often enough eventually becomes who you are.

The remarkable thing is that nobody else can make that decision for you. Not your spouse. Not your coach. Not your pastor. Not your parents. Eventually, every one of us stands alone in front of the mirror. No audience. No applause. No excuses. Just one question.

Who am I becoming?

That question has become one of the greatest gifts God has ever given me — not because it always has an easy answer, but because it always has an honest one. The answer changes. The question never does. Every season asks something different. The man I needed to become to survive the restaurant isn't the same man I needed to become to finish an ultramarathon. The man I needed to become to quit drinking isn't the same man I need to become to remain present for my family today. Growth changes the answer. That's healthy, because becoming never ends.

I don't ever want to arrive. I don't ever want to believe I've figured life out. The day I stop learning, I'll probably stop growing. That's why I still ask the question every morning: Who do I have to become today? Not next year. Not after another accomplishment. Today.

That question eventually organized every part of my life. I wasn't searching for another self-improvement system; I was searching for a life built on truth instead of excuses. Little did I know that simple question would eventually grow into the framework that now guides almost every decision I make.
