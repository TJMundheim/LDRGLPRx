# Chapter 4
## Becoming

People often ask me which day was the hardest. Day 7? Day 30? Day 100? Day 365? The truth surprises them: none of those days. The hardest day was Day 1. It wasn't the run itself that made it hard. Day 1 was hard because Day 1 required a decision. Every day after that only required keeping it. There's a huge difference between the two.

People spend a lot of time chasing motivation. I did too. I kept waiting to feel inspired, and I kept believing that if I could just find enough motivation, consistency would naturally follow. It never did. Motivation is emotional. Discipline is a decision. Motivation comes and goes; discipline quietly stays. Running every day taught me that lesson better than anything else ever had.

There were mornings when I couldn't wait to get outside, and there were other mornings when I stared at my shoes hoping I'd think of a good excuse. Cold mornings. Windy mornings. Rain, travel, long workdays, family vacations, sickness, busy schedules. Life never stopped happening, and the decision remained the same: run today.

From where I stand today, I don't think I was building a running streak at all. I was rebuilding trust. Not other people's trust. My own.

For most of my adult life I had made promises to myself that I never kept. I'll start Monday. I'll quit next month. I'll get back in shape. I'll drink less. I'll spend more time with my family. Every promise felt sincere when I made it, and every broken promise quietly taught me something: "You don't really follow through." I never said those words out loud. I didn't have to. My actions had already taught them to me.

Running interrupted that story. Every morning I finished another run, I quietly gathered another piece of evidence — evidence that I could keep my word, evidence that I could finish what I started, evidence that I wasn't the same man who had stood in front of the mirror believing he couldn't change. That's why I tell people the streak isn't what matters. Integrity matters. The streak just became visible evidence of an invisible transformation. One promise. Kept. Then another. Then another.

Eventually, something remarkable happened. I stopped wondering whether I was going to run, because I already knew the answer. The negotiation disappeared. That may be one of the greatest gifts discipline has ever given me: it removes unnecessary negotiations. Once you've decided who you're becoming, many decisions have already been made for you. I no longer asked, "Am I going to run today?" The only question left was, "When?"

That same principle slowly spilled into every other area of my life — marriage, parenting, health, work, faith. The more promises I kept, the more I trusted myself. The more I trusted myself, the less I depended on motivation. In hindsight, I can see that confidence wasn't something I found. It was something I built. One ordinary morning at a time. One quiet run at a time. One small promise at a time.

Most people see extraordinary lives. Very few people ever see the unremarkable days that quietly built them. That's where becoming actually happens. Not on race day. Not at the finish line. Not when everyone is watching. It happens early in the morning when no one would ever know if you chose the easier path. That's where character is built. That's where identity changes. That's where excuses quietly lose their voice.

But I don't want to give the impression that one decision smoothed the road ahead, because it didn't. One of the biggest misconceptions people have about growth is that once you make the decision to change, everything gets easier. That wasn't my experience. The decision is only the beginning. The real work starts the next morning. And the morning after that. And the one after that. The beautiful thing about momentum is that it compounds. So does complacency. The direction depends entirely on what you choose to repeat.

By the time my running streak had reached several months, people began asking questions. "How do you keep going every day?" "Don't you ever take a day off?" "What happens if you're traveling?" "What if you're tired?" Those questions made me smile, because they revealed something I hadn't even noticed. People thought the difficult part was the running. It wasn't. The difficult part had been making the decision that running was no longer negotiable. Once that decision was made, running became what I did. There's a huge difference between making a decision every day and living according to one you've already made. That's what standards began teaching me.

For a long stretch, my life had been controlled by negotiation. "I'll start Monday." "I'll skip today." "I deserve a break." "One day won't matter." Every excuse sounded reasonable in the instant I made it, and reasonable excuses still produce the same results. Eventually the truth landed. Every negotiation weakened my integrity. Every promise I broke taught me not to trust myself. Every promise I kept taught me the opposite. That's why I say I wasn't building a running streak. I was rebuilding self-trust.

People often ask me if I was trying to become a runner. Not really. I was trying to become a man who kept his word. Running just happened to become the vehicle. One small promise, repeated every day. That's all it was.

One morning during a run I caught myself smiling. It wasn't that the run felt easy — it didn't. I smiled because I noticed I hadn't argued with myself before leaving the house. There was no debate. No internal negotiation. No "Should I?" I had already answered that question months earlier. That's freedom. Not the kind that comes from discipline removing difficulty, because it doesn't. The kind that comes from discipline removing unnecessary decisions.

The more I thought about it, the more I saw that almost every successful person I admired had something in common. They weren't making fewer difficult decisions. They were making fewer unnecessary ones. They had already decided who they wanted to become, and everything else aligned with that identity.

Seeing that changed the way I looked at every area of my life. I stopped asking, "What do I feel like doing today?" and started asking, "What would the man I'm becoming do today?" Those are two completely different questions. One is driven by emotion. The other is driven by identity. And identity almost always wins. If I believe I'm someone who keeps promises, I naturally keep looking for promises to keep. If I believe I'm someone who quits when life gets hard, my actions eventually prove me right. Identity quietly shapes behavior. Behavior quietly shapes your life.

That's why I became so fascinated by one simple question: Who do I have to become? It didn't magically solve my problems. What it did was change where I looked for the answers. Year after year I had searched for better strategies, better plans, better motivation. The better answer was becoming a better man. Everything else began growing from there.

The remarkable thing is that nobody else noticed this transformation at first. There wasn't some dramatic announcement. No celebration. No applause. Just another regular morning. Then another. Then another. Transformation almost always happens quietly, long before anyone else notices. That's okay. Some of the most important work you'll ever do happens where no one else is watching.

The longer the streak continued, the more I understood something else: people were paying attention. Not because of me. Because consistency stands out in a world full of starts and stops. Friends would occasionally ask, "How many days are you at now?" Others would say, "I saw you were running in the snow," or, "Didn't you just get back from a trip?" The truth was, none of those days felt particularly extraordinary while I was living them. They were just Tuesday. Wednesday. Saturday. Uneventful days.

That's one of the greatest lessons running has ever taught me: extraordinary lives are almost always built through ordinary days. People celebrate the milestone. Very few people celebrate the hundreds of mornings that made the milestone possible. I began thinking about that during my runs. Why do we admire the finish line so much while overlooking the thousands of small decisions that made it possible? The answer, I think, is that unremarkable work isn't exciting. It's repetitive. Quiet. Unnoticed. But that's exactly where character is built.

One morning another thought came to me: the race isn't preparing you for race day. The race is preparing you for life. That sentence stayed with me, because I could see it was true. Running every day wasn't teaching me how to become a better runner. It was teaching me how to become a better husband, a better father, a better employee, a better friend, a better leader. Every difficult run became another opportunity to practice keeping my word when I didn't feel like it.

That lesson carried into every other part of my life. Marriage doesn't always feel easy. Parenting doesn't always feel easy. Business certainly doesn't always feel easy. Neither does faith. Neither does becoming. There are days when you just don't feel like doing the work, and those are the days that matter most. Anyone can stay committed when everything feels good. Character is revealed when it doesn't. That truth removed another excuse from my life. I stopped waiting to feel inspired. I stopped waiting to feel motivated. I kept showing up. Again. And again.

Eventually, another opportunity presented itself. Someone asked if I wanted to run an ultramarathon. Sixty-two miles. One hundred kilometers. My first thought wasn't, "Can I do it?" My first thought was, "Who do I have to become to finish it?" That question had quietly become the filter for every major decision in my life. Not "Can I?" but "Who do I need to become?" Those are very different questions. One focuses on ability. The other focuses on identity. Identity changes everything.

Training for that race wasn't really about sixty-two miles. It was about becoming the kind of man who could prepare for sixty-two miles. The race was months away, but the man I needed to become started changing that very day. The workouts changed. The nutrition changed. The sleep changed. The priorities changed. None of it happened because race day had arrived. It happened because identity had. Goals have a remarkable way of changing tomorrow. Identity changes today. That distinction has stayed with me ever since. One of the greatest mistakes I made earlier in life was chasing accomplishments. Now I chase becoming. Accomplishments eventually end. Becoming never does.

The ultramarathon wasn't another finish line. It became another classroom — another opportunity to learn, another opportunity to discover where my limits actually were. More importantly, it became another opportunity to discover that many of the limits I believed in weren't physical at all. They were stories. Stories I had believed for most of my life. Stories that sounded remarkably similar to the ones I first heard as a little boy. "You're not enough." "You can't do this." "You should quit." The amazing thing about discipline is that it slowly begins answering those stories without saying a word. Every promise kept becomes another piece of evidence. Every ordinary day becomes another vote. Eventually, the evidence becomes louder than the lies. And when that happens, you don't just accomplish difficult things.

You become someone different.

That's what I had been searching for all along.

One morning, during a run, I found myself thinking about the little boy who believed he wasn't enough. It wasn't planned; the thought just appeared. Maybe that's one of the greatest gifts running has given me. When the distractions disappear, the truth has room to speak. As the miles passed, something hit me that stopped me in my tracks. That little boy wasn't weak. He never was. He survived things no child should ever have to survive. He endured years of abuse. He carried lies that weren't his. He learned how to smile while hurting. He learned how to survive.

For a long time, I looked back at that little boy with sadness. Now I look at him with compassion. Even gratitude. Because if he hadn't refused to quit, the man writing these words wouldn't exist.

That understanding changed the way I viewed my entire life. I stopped seeing myself as someone who had been broken and started seeing myself as someone God had been preparing. Those are two completely different perspectives. Preparation often feels like punishment while you're living through it. Only later do you recognize the purpose. That doesn't mean I would ever choose those experiences again. I wouldn't. It just means I no longer believe God wasted them. Nothing was wasted. Not the abuse. Not the restaurant. Not the lawsuits. Not the depression. Not the alcohol. Not the rocking chair. Every painful chapter became preparation for another one.

The older I get, the more convinced I become that God rarely wastes pain. He redeems it. Sometimes not immediately. Sometimes years later. Sometimes through helping another person who's walking through something remarkably similar. That truth slowly changed the reason I shared my story. At first, I talked about it because I was trying to understand it. Now I talk about it because someone else might need hope. Maybe that's the purpose of healing. Not merely feeling better, but helping someone else believe they can too.

Around this same time, another understanding quietly settled into my heart. I wasn't chasing happiness anymore. I was creating it. For years I had believed happiness waited somewhere in the future. After the next promotion. After the next race. After the next business opportunity. After life finally settled down. I couldn't have been more wrong. Happiness isn't something we discover. It's something we build. One ordinary day at a time. One promise at a time. One conversation at a time. One decision at a time.

Everything compounds. Excuses compound. Discipline compounds. Kindness compounds. Truth compounds. Hope compounds. The question is, what are you compounding today?

That thought stayed with me for weeks, because I could see that I had spent years compounding excuses. Now I wanted to compound integrity. I wanted plain days that quietly pointed me toward the man I believed God had created me to become. Not because I thought I'd ever arrive — becoming never ends. There's always another lesson. Another opportunity. Another promise. Another chance to grow. Understanding that removed an enormous amount of pressure from my life. I stopped trying to arrive. I kept becoming.

The funny thing is, once I stopped chasing a destination, I started enjoying the road itself. I smiled more. Laughed more. Spent more time truly present with my family. I wasn't thinking about tomorrow nearly as much anymore. Today deserved my attention. My daughters deserved my attention. My wife deserved my attention. Life deserved my attention. For longer than I'd like to admit, I had been physically present while mentally somewhere else. Now I was finally beginning to live where my feet actually were. That may sound simple. It isn't. Presence is one of the greatest gifts you can ever give another human being. I learned that the hard way, through all the seasons when I wasn't giving it.

Running taught me many things. Discipline. Consistency. Self-trust. But maybe the greatest lesson it taught me was this.

Life isn't won in extraordinary moments. It's won in ordinary ones.

One sunrise. One difficult conversation. One workout. One apology. One promise. One day. Repeated often enough, those ordinary days quietly become an extraordinary life.

I didn't know it then, but those everyday runs were laying the foundation for something much bigger than I could imagine. Not another race. A framework. A way of living. A question that would eventually organize every area of my life. Not, "What do I want?" But, "Who do I have to become?" That question would eventually lead me to four simple pillars that transformed the way I looked at health, learning, relationships, and stewardship. They weren't created overnight. They were discovered.

One ordinary day at a time.
