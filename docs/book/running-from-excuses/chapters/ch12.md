# Chapter 12

## Shine Your Light

One of the unexpected things that happened as my life began changing was that people started asking questions. It wasn't that I had all the answers — it was that they noticed something was different. Some asked about the running streak. Others asked about the weight I'd lost. Some wanted to know why I looked happier. Others just asked, "What's changed?"

At first, those questions made me uncomfortable. It wasn't that I didn't want to answer them; I wasn't sure my story was worth telling. Who was I to encourage someone else? I wasn't a millionaire. I wasn't famous. I wasn't a pastor. I wasn't a nutritionist. I wasn't someone who had life completely figured out. I was nothing more than a man who had stopped lying to himself.

The more I thought about it, though, the more something dawned on me. Maybe that's exactly who people need to hear from — not someone pretending to have a perfect life, but someone willing to honestly talk about an imperfect one.

That truth changed everything.

For years I had hidden behind a mask. Now I felt God gently asking me to take it off — not for attention, but for freedom. One conversation at a time, I began sharing pieces of my story. The abuse. The restaurant. The alcohol. The depression. The rocking chair. The running streak. The becoming.

I expected judgment. Instead, something remarkable happened. People began sharing their stories with me — stories they had never told anyone else. Stories of addiction, depression, broken marriages, financial struggles, anxiety, childhood trauma. The more vulnerable I became, the more permission other people seemed to feel to become vulnerable too.

That taught me something I will never forget. Vulnerability is contagious. So is courage. When one person finally tells the truth, it often gives another person permission to do the same. That's one of the reasons I believe silence can be so dangerous. Silence convinces us we're alone. Truth reminds us we're not. Through a long stretch of my life I believed I was the only one fighting the battles inside my own head. I wasn't. I just couldn't see everyone else carrying theirs.

The more conversations I had, the more one thing became clear to me: people are desperate for authenticity. Not perfection. Authenticity. The world doesn't need another polished image. It needs more honest people — people willing to say, "I'm struggling." "I need help." "I don't have everything figured out." Ironically, those conversations almost always create deeper strength than pretending ever could.

That's why I believe your story matters. Its value has nothing to do with whether every detail is unique; it matters because someone else is living a chapter you've already survived. You may become the proof they desperately need that another ending is possible — not because you're extraordinary, but because you're willing to be honest.

That's where light begins. Not through perfection. Through truth.

One of the greatest fears people carry is the fear of being seen — not physically, emotionally. We spend an incredible amount of energy trying to convince the world we're okay, and social media has made that even easier. We post the vacation. The promotion. The race medal. The smiling family picture. Very few people post the panic attack, the marriage that's struggling, the loneliness, the sleepless nights, the fear. I'm not criticizing social media. I'm only saying it rarely tells the whole story.

For years, my smile told one story while my heart told another. Nobody knew I was sitting in a rocking chair wondering if my family would somehow be better off without me. Nobody knew I was using alcohol to quiet anxiety I didn't know how to face. Nobody knew how exhausted I had become pretending everything was fine. That's one of the reasons I believe masks are so dangerous. The longer we wear them, the harder they become to remove. Eventually, we forget they're even there.

One conversation changed that for me. I was sharing a small part of my story with another man, and when I finished, he looked at me and quietly said, "I thought I was the only one."

I'll never forget those words, and not for their uniqueness — I've heard them over and over again ever since. "I thought I was the only one." People aren't nearly as alone as they believe they are. They're suffering in silence. Silence gives shame room to grow. Truth takes that room away.

That doesn't mean we tell everyone everything. Wisdom still matters. Discernment still matters. Healthy boundaries still matter. But every one of us needs someone with whom we can remove the mask — someone who knows the real story, someone who loves us enough to tell us the truth, someone who reminds us who we are when we've temporarily forgotten.

One of the greatest gifts God has ever given me has been people willing to have those conversations. My wife. My coaches. Close friends. Men who challenged me, men who encouraged me, men who refused to let me settle. Those conversations didn't always feel comfortable — growth rarely does. But they felt honest, and honesty became one of the greatest expressions of love I've ever experienced.

That's one of the reasons I believe your light grows brighter every time you choose truth over image. Vulnerability doesn't earn admiration; it builds connection. People don't connect with perfection. They connect with honesty. The more I shared my struggles, the more I noticed something remarkable. People weren't inspired by my success. They were encouraged by my willingness to admit where I had failed. That surprised me. I thought people wanted polished. They wanted real.

Understanding that changed the reason I began telling my story. I stopped trying to impress people. I simply wanted to encourage them. Because if God could rebuild my life, maybe someone else could begin believing He could rebuild theirs too.

As those conversations continued, I began noticing something I had completely overlooked for most of my life. Pain has a remarkable way of convincing us that our story ends with us. Healing does the exact opposite. Healing reminds us that our story was never only about us.

Every difficult season I'd walked through suddenly had a different purpose. The abuse. The restaurant. The lawsuits. The depression. The alcohol. The running. None of it had been wasted. That isn't to say those experiences were good — they weren't. But God has a remarkable ability to redeem things that were never good to begin with. That's one of the greatest miracles I've witnessed in my own life. He didn't erase my past. He repurposed it. There's a tremendous difference.

If someone asked me today whether I would relive those painful years, the answer would still be no. I wouldn't wish those experiences on anyone. But if they asked whether I would remove what God has done through them, the answer would also be no. Because those painful chapters created opportunities to reach people I never could have reached otherwise.

I've sat across from men battling alcohol. I've talked with fathers who quietly believed they had failed their families. I've listened to people convinced they were beyond hope. Again and again, I've watched something remarkable happen. The instant they realize someone else understands, their shoulders relax. It isn't that all of their problems disappear — it's that they finally stop carrying them alone.

I think that's one of the reasons God rarely wastes pain. Someone else is eventually going to need what you've learned. Not your perfection. Your testimony. The older I become, the less interested I am in protecting my image. I'd much rather protect someone else's hope. If sharing one difficult chapter from my life gives another person the courage to begin rewriting theirs, then every uncomfortable conversation becomes worth it.

That's why I believe your story has value — not because it's dramatic, but because it's honest. People don't need another superhero. They need another human being, someone willing to admit, "I've been there too." That simple sentence has opened more meaningful conversations than anything else I've ever said. It doesn't remove another person's pain; it removes isolation. Isolation convinces us we're uniquely broken. Community reminds us we're not.

One of the greatest lies I believed for far too long was that asking for help somehow meant I was weak. Now I believe the opposite. Asking for help often requires more courage than pretending you're okay. Strength isn't hiding your wounds. Strength is refusing to let those wounds define the rest of your life. That's a completely different way to live.

The more I thought about shining your light, the more one truth kept surfacing. Light never argues with darkness. It just shows up. Darkness doesn't have to be chased away; it disappears the instant light arrives. Truth works exactly the same way. Hope works exactly the same way. Love works exactly the same way. That's why I no longer feel responsible for changing people. I only want to faithfully shine the light God has placed inside me. He'll take care of the rest.

One image has stayed with me throughout this entire road. A candle. If you light another candle from it, the first candle loses nothing. It doesn't become dimmer. It doesn't become weaker. The room just becomes brighter. I think our lives work much the same way.

Some people spend their entire lives protecting their light — afraid someone else might receive the credit, afraid someone else might become more successful, afraid someone else's success somehow diminishes their own. I've learned the opposite is true. Helping someone else shine never makes your own light smaller. It multiplies it.

That's become one of the greatest joys of my life. Watching someone else begin believing again. Watching someone else quit making excuses. Watching someone else become present with their family. Watching someone else begin asking, "Who do I have to become?" Those experiences have become far more meaningful to me than personal accomplishments ever were. Because accomplishments eventually become memories. People become legacies.

I've thought about legacy quite a bit over the years. When I was younger, I thought legacy meant leaving behind something impressive — a successful business, financial security, recognition. Today I think legacy is much simpler. Legacy is who continues becoming because you chose to become first.

My daughters are part of my legacy, and it isn't anything they'll accomplish that makes them so. It's the example they watched every single day. They're watching how I speak to their mother. They're watching whether I keep my word. They're watching whether I apologize when I'm wrong. They're watching whether my faith is something I merely talk about or something I actually live. Children are incredible observers. They notice consistency long before they understand principles.

That thought has challenged me more than almost anything else, because it reminds me that leadership always begins at home. If my daughters never remember how many miles I ran, I hope they remember that their dad kept his word. If they never remember the ultramarathon, I hope they remember that I showed up. If they never remember another accomplishment, I hope they remember feeling loved. Everything else is secondary.

One day, long after this book has been forgotten, long after someone else has broken my running streak, long after another person has run farther or faster, the people closest to me will still remember how I lived. That thought keeps me grounded. It reminds me that significance is usually found in ordinary faithfulness, not extraordinary moments. One ordinary day lived with integrity, repeated often enough, eventually becomes a life that quietly points other people toward hope.

That's what I want my life to do. Not point people toward me. Point them toward the God who patiently rebuilt me. Because if there's anything worthwhile about my story, it's not that I finally figured life out. It's that God never gave up on me while I was still trying to.

That's the light I hope people see. Not mine. His.

And if my story encourages even one person to believe that another chapter is still possible, then every difficult chapter I lived through becomes worth telling.
