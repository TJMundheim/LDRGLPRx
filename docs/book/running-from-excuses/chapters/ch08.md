# Chapter 8
## Education

When most people hear the word education, they immediately think about school. Grades. Diplomas. Degrees. That's not what this chapter is about. Education, at least the way I define it today, has very little to do with a classroom. It's about becoming. It's about remaining curious. It's about refusing to believe you've arrived — every single day, for the rest of your life.

For a long stretch I assumed education ended the day I graduated from college. I couldn't have been more wrong. The most valuable education I've ever received happened after I walked across that stage. It came through books, mentors, failure, conversations, experience, and reflection. Life itself became my classroom.

That understanding changed everything. Suddenly every mistake carried value, every setback carried a lesson, and every difficult conversation became an opportunity to grow. Education stopped being about information. It became about transformation.

When I struggled in sales, I studied sales. When I wanted to better serve my customers, I studied people. When I wanted stronger relationships, I studied communication. When I wanted better health, I studied health. When I wanted a deeper faith, I spent more time in Scripture. When I wanted a stronger mindset, I intentionally sought people who had already walked roads I hoped to travel. Nobody assigned those lessons. Curiosity did. That's the difference. No one can force another person to grow. Growth becomes powerful when learning becomes personal.

The more I learned, the more I recognized how little I actually knew. When I was younger, I thought confidence came from having answers. Today I believe confidence comes from remaining teachable. Those are two completely different people. The first eventually stops growing. The second never has to. That's why I still seek coaches, still read, still ask questions, and still spend far more time listening than talking. I hope I never stop being a student, because the day I believe I've arrived is probably the day I'll stop becoming.

One thought kept returning to me during that season. A sword that never gets sharpened eventually becomes dull. The answer isn't swinging it harder. The answer is stepping away long enough to sharpen the edge. That's exactly how I now view education — not collecting more information, but sharpening the sword, preparing the instrument before the battle arrives. Life will eventually test every one of us. The question is whether we've been preparing long before the test begins.

That's when something else hit me. Not everything deserves access to your mind. That sentence stopped me, because I had spent so long becoming intentional about what entered my body while remaining almost completely unintentional about what entered my mind. Imagine allowing someone to walk into your house every day carrying bags of garbage. Eventually your house becomes unlivable. Our minds work exactly the same way. Every conversation, every headline, every social media post, every television show, every opinion, and every repeated thought — everything leaves something behind. The question isn't whether information influences you. It does. The question is whether you're intentionally deciding what gets through the gate.

One of the greatest investments you'll ever make isn't in the stock market. It isn't in real estate. It isn't even in your career. It's in your mind. Everything in your life eventually passes through it — every decision, every relationship, every opportunity, every response. The quality of your thinking eventually determines the quality of your life.

Through most of my early adulthood, I thought education was something you finished. Graduate high school. Graduate college. Get the degree. Start working. In hindsight, I had completely misunderstood what education actually was. School teaches you subjects. Life teaches you wisdom. The two aren't always the same. I've met people with advanced degrees who continually make poor decisions. I've also met people who never attended college who possess incredible wisdom. Knowledge and wisdom aren't identical. Knowledge tells you that something is true. Wisdom teaches you how to live it. That's the education I became interested in. Not information. Transformation.

One of the greatest changes in my life happened when I stopped asking, "What do I have to learn?" and began asking instead, "Who can teach me?" That subtle shift changed everything. Books became conversations. Podcasts became mentorship. Failure became tuition. Every person I met knew something I didn't, and if I stayed curious, life constantly offered another lesson.

Coming to see that removed something I had quietly carried for a long time. Pride. Pride says, "I already know." Humility asks, "What am I missing?" One question stops growth. The other fuels it.

I've discovered that the wisest people I know all seem to have something in common. They're incredibly curious. They ask questions. They listen. They aren't trying to prove how much they know; they're trying to learn one more thing. That's become one of my favorite ways to approach life: walk into every room believing someone knows something you don't. Because they probably do. That perspective has made me a better husband, a better father, a better salesman, a better runner, and a better leader. Most importantly, it has made me a better student. I hope I never stop being one. The day I believe I've learned everything I need to know, I'll probably stop becoming.

And I never want becoming to end.

One of the greatest lies I ever believed was that learning automatically produces change. It doesn't. I've read books that changed absolutely nothing about my life — and it wasn't that the books weren't good. It was that I never applied what they taught. I've also read a single sentence that completely changed the direction of my thinking. The difference wasn't the amount of information. It was what I chose to do with it. Knowledge by itself has very little value. Applied knowledge changes everything.

That's why I eventually stopped measuring how many books I finished each year. I became much more interested in one question: what am I doing differently because of what I just learned? If the answer was nothing, then I hadn't really learned anything. I had merely been entertained.

That truth changed the way I approached reading. Instead of trying to finish another chapter, I stopped whenever something challenged me. Sometimes I'd reread the same page several times — not because I didn't understand it, but because I wanted to live it. The goal wasn't another completed book. The goal was another transformed habit.

I think that's one of the biggest mistakes our culture makes. We celebrate consuming information. We rarely celebrate applying it. The world doesn't need more people who know what to do. The world needs more people willing to actually do it. One applied lesson is worth more than one hundred forgotten quotes. One difficult conversation is worth more than another notebook full of highlights. One healthy decision repeated consistently is worth more than another inspirational podcast. Application changes identity. Information simply fills notebooks.

I learned that lesson the hard way. Year after year I collected ideas. I attended seminars. I listened to podcasts. I highlighted books. I took pages of notes. Yet my life often looked exactly the same. The problem wasn't what I knew. The problem was what I refused to practice.

Eventually I began asking myself another question: what's one thing I can live today? Not ten things. One. If I learned about gratitude, practice gratitude today. If I learned about forgiveness, make the phone call today. If I learned about discipline, keep today's promise. That approach simplified growth tremendously. Small changes became sustainable. Sustainable changes became habits. Habits eventually became identity. Identity quietly changed my life.

One lesson, applied consistently, can change everything.

That's why I no longer read just to finish books. I read to become someone different. The same is true for this book. If you finish every chapter but never apply a single lesson, nothing changes. If one idea changes the way you live tomorrow, everything changes. That's why I included questions throughout this book — not so you'll agree with me, but so you'll wrestle with your own answers. My answers built my life. Yours will build yours. That's exactly the way it should be.

Education isn't complete when you've learned something. Education becomes complete when your life begins reflecting what you've learned. That's when knowledge finally becomes wisdom.

One lesson I learned through medical sales has stayed with me far beyond my career: people don't care how much you know until they're convinced you genuinely care about them. That lesson changed the way I approached almost every conversation. When I first entered sales, I thought success depended on having all the answers — knowing every product, every study, every statistic, every objection. The longer I worked with physicians, nurses, and hospital staff, the more I understood something. They weren't looking for another salesperson. They were looking for someone they could trust.

Trust is built long before someone signs an agreement. It's built through consistency, through listening, through following through — through doing exactly what you said you were going to do. Education taught me something important there too. The more I learned, the less I felt the need to prove how much I knew. I became much more interested in asking better questions. Curiosity became one of my greatest professional advantages. Instead of walking into an office thinking, "What do I want to say?" I started thinking, "What do I need to understand?" Those are completely different conversations. People feel the difference. They know when they're being talked to. They also know when they're genuinely being heard.

That lesson carried into my marriage, into parenting, into friendships, and into leadership. Listening became more valuable than speaking. Understanding became more valuable than impressing. One question eventually became part of almost every meaningful conversation I had: "Help me understand." Those three words changed relationships. It wasn't that they solved every disagreement; it was that they communicated humility. Humility creates space for learning. Pride usually shuts the conversation down before it ever begins.

I've discovered that education isn't really about accumulating more answers. It's about asking better questions. The quality of your questions often determines the quality of your life. Ask shallow questions, and you receive shallow answers. Ask honest questions, and life usually responds honestly. That's one of the reasons I've become so fascinated with questions throughout this book. Questions don't tell people what to think. They invite people to think.

No one ever changed my life by forcing answers on me. The people who changed my life asked questions I couldn't ignore — questions I carried home, questions that followed me onto runs, questions that stayed with me long after the conversation ended. Questions like, "Who do I have to become?" That single question has probably shaped my life more than any answer ever has. The right answer may inspire you for a moment. The right question can shape you for a lifetime.

Education eventually taught me that the greatest classroom isn't a building. It's life itself. Every conversation teaches something. Every failure teaches something. Every success teaches something. Every difficult season teaches something. The only real question is whether we're paying attention. For too long, I wasn't. Today, I hope I never stop learning, because I hope I never stop becoming.

As I continued learning, I noticed something else. The people I admired most all shared one characteristic: they were readers. It wasn't that reading somehow made them smarter. Reading kept them teachable. Every book became another opportunity to borrow someone else's lifetime of experience.

I've often thought about how incredible that really is. A person spends thirty, forty, sometimes fifty years learning difficult lessons, then condenses those lessons into a few hundred pages. For the price of a book and a few hours of your time, you gain access to decades of wisdom. That's an incredible return on investment. The problem isn't access. The problem is application. I've met people with bookshelves full of incredible books whose lives never changed. I've also met people who built extraordinary lives from faithfully applying just a handful of timeless principles. The difference wasn't intelligence. It was action.

Seeing that completely changed the way I approached personal growth. Instead of constantly searching for the next great idea, I started asking, "Am I living the ideas I already know?" That question exposed me. I didn't need another book telling me to become more disciplined; I needed another disciplined day. I didn't need another podcast on marriage; I needed another intentional conversation with my wife. I didn't need another financial seminar; I needed to steward today's money wisely.

Growth became much simpler. Not easier. Simpler. Learn. Apply. Repeat. The same pattern kept showing up everywhere I looked. Read, practice, reflect, repeat. Listen, apply, repeat. Fall down, get back up, repeat. Life isn't nearly as complicated as we often make it. Consistency has a remarkable way of making complicated things simple.

One of the greatest educational experiences of my life didn't happen because I learned something new. It happened because I finally understood something I had heard many times before: there is a tremendous difference between information and wisdom. Information answers questions. Wisdom asks better ones. Information fills your mind. Wisdom shapes your life. Information can impress people. Wisdom quietly serves them. That's the kind of education I wanted — not education that merely made me more knowledgeable, but education that made me more useful. More dependable. More compassionate. More prepared. More intentional.

The more I grew, the more I noticed another interesting pattern. The wisest people I knew weren't constantly talking. They were constantly observing — listening, asking questions, learning. That changed my approach to leadership. I stopped feeling like I needed to have every answer. I simply needed to remain curious enough to keep asking better questions.

Curiosity has become one of the greatest gifts God has given me. Curiosity keeps pride from taking over. Curiosity keeps growth alive. Curiosity reminds me that every person I meet knows something I don't. That's incredibly freeing. It means every conversation becomes an opportunity, every relationship becomes another classroom, and every day becomes another lesson. The day we stop learning, we quietly begin declining. I don't ever want that day to come.

I hope that five years from now I'm embarrassed by some of the things I believe today — not because they were wrong, but because I continued growing. If I'm exactly the same man ten years from now, then somewhere along the way I stopped becoming. I don't want that. I hope I'm always becoming. Always learning. Always refining. Always growing. Not for my sake alone, but for my family, for the people I lead, for the people I serve, and for the readers who trusted me enough to spend time with this book. If I'm asking you to keep becoming, I owe you the same commitment myself.

That may be the greatest lesson education has ever taught me. The student never graduates. He just keeps showing up, ready to learn again tomorrow.

From where I stand today, I don't believe education's greatest purpose is helping us make a better living. I believe its greatest purpose is helping us become better people. There's a difference. A better living can provide comfort. A better person creates impact.

For most of my adult life I measured success by accomplishments — promotions, income, business opportunities, athletic goals. One by one, I accomplished many of the things I once believed would finally make me feel successful. Oddly enough, the feeling never lasted very long. Another goal always appeared. Another mountain. Another finish line. Another opportunity. Eventually it sank in that I had been measuring the wrong things. The older I become, the less interested I am in asking, "What have I accomplished?" I'm much more interested in asking, "Who have I helped?" That question changed my definition of success.

One of the greatest blessings of my career in medical sales has been the opportunity to serve physicians, nurses, and hospital staff who dedicate their lives to helping others. Being around those people for so many years taught me something I don't think I'll ever forget. The most respected people in the room usually aren't the ones trying to prove they're the smartest. They're the ones constantly learning. Medicine changes. Technology changes. Research changes. The best physicians never stop studying — and no one makes them. They study because lives depend on it.

That principle extends far beyond medicine. Our marriages deserve that kind of curiosity. Our children deserve that kind of curiosity. Our faith deserves that kind of curiosity. Our leadership deserves that kind of curiosity. The instant we believe we've figured everything out, growth quietly stops. I hope I never become that person. I hope I remain curious enough to ask questions that make me uncomfortable. I hope I remain humble enough to admit when I'm wrong. I hope I remain teachable enough to continue changing. Because if this road has taught me anything, it's this: growth is never something we graduate from. It's a lifelong invitation — an invitation to become a little wiser, a little more patient, a little more compassionate, a little more intentional, every single day.

When I think about education today, I no longer picture classrooms. I picture conversations with my daughters. Long walks with my wife. Early morning runs. Books filled with highlighted pages. Coaches who challenged me. Friends who told me the truth. Failures that humbled me. Successes that reminded me to stay grateful. Life has become my classroom. Every day offers another lesson. The only question is whether I'm paying attention.

Education isn't about filling your mind. It's about shaping your character. Character is what remains when the information is forgotten. Character is what your family experiences every day. Character is what your coworkers trust. Character is what your children imitate. Character is what quietly introduces you long before you ever speak. Everything we consistently learn eventually becomes someone. The question is, who are we becoming?

That question naturally leads us to another pillar — one that, for many years, I misunderstood almost as much as education. I thought relationships happened naturally. I eventually learned they require just as much intention as every other area of life. Maybe more.

That's where our next pillar begins.
