# Chapter 9

## Empathy

For a long time, I misunderstood empathy. I thought empathy meant agreeing with someone, avoiding difficult conversations, trying to make sure everyone felt comfortable. I couldn't have been more wrong. Empathy isn't lowering the standard. Empathy is caring enough to understand someone's story before you judge their behavior. That lesson changed my relationships more than almost anything else I've learned — maybe because I needed it so desperately myself.

Growing up, there were many times I wished someone would just ask, "Randall... what's really going on?" It wasn't sympathy I was after. I wanted someone to understand. Sometimes the greatest gift you can give another person isn't advice. It's your attention. Through much of my life I believed people needed better answers. Today I believe most people first need someone willing to truly listen. Listening has become one of the greatest demonstrations of love I know — not waiting for your turn to speak, not trying to solve every problem, but listening. Really listening.

The older I become, the more convinced I am that everyone is carrying something. Some burdens are visible; most aren't. The person who seems angry may actually be exhausted. The person who appears distant may be grieving. The person who seems impatient may be carrying fear nobody else knows about. I've learned to become much slower when judging people — not because everyone is right, but because everyone has a story.

My own story taught me that. There were years when people saw a quiet teenager; they didn't see the abuse. Years later, people saw a businessman; they didn't see the anxiety. They saw someone who smiled; they didn't see the depression. They saw a husband and father; they didn't see the man sitting in a rocking chair wondering if his family would somehow be better off without him.

Appearances rarely tell the whole story.

That's why empathy begins with curiosity. Instead of asking, "What's wrong with that person?" I try asking, "I wonder what they've been through?" That question has changed countless conversations. It reminds me that behavior often has roots we can't see. Understanding those roots doesn't excuse unhealthy behavior; it only helps explain it. That distinction matters, because empathy doesn't ignore truth. Empathy delivers truth with compassion.

Jesus modeled that perfectly. He never compromised truth, and He also never forgot the person standing in front of Him. I've tried to remember that in my own life — especially when someone disappoints me, especially when someone frustrates me, especially when someone doesn't meet my expectations. Because I've been that person too. I've disappointed people. I've needed grace. I've needed second chances. Remembering my own story has made it much easier to extend grace to someone else's.

One of the greatest gifts my wife has given me isn't encouragement. It's honesty. Real honesty — the kind that loves you enough to tell you the truth. There have been many conversations over the years where she saw something in me long before I saw it myself. Sometimes she reminded me I was capable of more. Other times she lovingly pointed out where I was falling short. Neither conversation was always easy. Both were necessary. Love isn't merely celebrating someone's strengths; sometimes love quietly refuses to let someone settle. I've become incredibly grateful for that. Empathy isn't avoiding difficult conversations. It's caring enough to have them. That's a lesson I continue learning.

For a long stretch of my career, I believed leadership meant having the right answers. Today I think leadership begins with asking better questions. "How are you... really?" "What can I do to help?" "What are you carrying that nobody else knows about?" Those questions have opened doors that advice never could. People rarely need another lecture. Most people already know where they're struggling. What they often need is someone willing to sit beside them while they find the courage to face it.

I've learned that fixing people isn't my responsibility. Loving them is. That's a tremendous relief, because it means I don't have to carry the weight of changing another person's life. All I have to do is faithfully love the person standing in front of me. God handles the transformation.

One lesson that changed the way I parent came from a truth so plain I had somehow missed it: my daughters don't need a perfect father. They need a present one. Presence has become one of the greatest expressions of empathy in my life. When I'm truly listening, I'm present. When I put the phone away, I'm present. When I stop thinking about work while someone else is talking, I'm present. Presence communicates value. It quietly tells another person, "You matter enough for me to give you my full attention."

For years I thought providing for my family was the greatest gift I could give them. Providing matters. But provision without presence leaves something important missing. Children spell love in ways adults often overlook. They spell it T-I-M-E. Not expensive vacations. Not elaborate gifts. Time. Conversations. Shared experiences. Quiet evenings around the dinner table. Long car rides. Throwing a ball in the backyard. Ski trips. Fishing trips. Running errands together. These are the things that don't seem particularly significant while they're happening — until years later, when they become the memories your children carry into adulthood.

As I've reflected on my own childhood, it has hit me that the times I treasure most weren't expensive. They were ordinary. Watching football with my grandparents. Working alongside my dad. Traveling with my grandmother. Sitting quietly in a deer blind. Those experiences shaped me far more than possessions ever could, and that's one of the reasons experiences have become so valuable to me today. Experiences are the world's most valuable currency — not for what they cost, but for the way they become part of who we are. Money comes and goes. Experiences become stories. Stories become memories. Memories quietly shape generations.

The more I thought about empathy, the more I saw that it always begins with one decision: slow down. People are difficult to love when we're constantly in a hurry. People are difficult to understand when we're always thinking about the next meeting, the next email, the next task, the next obligation. Empathy requires margin. Margin requires intention. Intention requires priorities. Everything is connected. The quality of our relationships rarely depends on how much we know. It depends on how well we love.

One of the greatest changes empathy brought into my life was forgiveness. It wasn't that forgiveness suddenly became easy; it was that I finally understood who was carrying the weight. Year after year, I believed holding on somehow protected me. I thought if I stayed angry, if I stayed bitter, if I remembered every painful detail, then somehow justice would eventually be served. Instead, I discovered I was the one carrying the burden.

Forgiveness doesn't erase the past. It doesn't pretend something painful never happened. It doesn't excuse abuse. It doesn't remove accountability. Forgiveness refuses to allow yesterday to continue controlling today. That truth took me years to accept. There are still days when old memories surface — still times when I remember words spoken over a little boy who didn't deserve them. The difference is that those memories no longer determine who I become. They remind me where I came from, not where I'm going.

I've often been asked whether I ever forgave my stepmother. The answer surprises some people. Yes. She never asked for it, and nothing was suddenly resolved. I forgave her because I needed to. Forgiveness wasn't setting her free.

It was setting me free.

That doesn't mean trust automatically returns. Trust and forgiveness are not the same thing. Trust is rebuilt through consistent behavior. Forgiveness is a decision. Healthy boundaries still matter. Wisdom still matters. Discernment still matters. One doesn't cancel the other.

That's another lesson empathy taught me. You can love someone and still establish healthy boundaries. You can forgive someone and still recognize that certain relationships require distance. Those ideas once seemed contradictory to me. Today they make perfect sense, because love doesn't always mean unlimited access. Sometimes love says, "I genuinely wish you well," while also recognizing that wisdom requires separation. That's difficult. It's also healthy.

The more I grew, the more I understood that empathy isn't only something we extend to other people. Sometimes it's something we must learn to extend to ourselves. That may have been one of the hardest lessons of all. For longer than I'd like to admit, I spoke to myself in ways I would never speak to another human being. I replayed mistakes. I magnified failures. I ignored progress. I demanded perfection. If someone else had spoken to me the way I spoke to myself, I would have called it abuse. Yet I accepted it because the voice sounded familiar.

Eventually I recognized whose voice it really was. It wasn't God's. It wasn't truth. It was an old recording that had been playing since childhood. Once I recognized that, I finally had a choice. Would I continue repeating those lies, or would I begin replacing them with truth? That process didn't happen overnight. It happened one thought at a time. One prayer at a time.

One ordinary day at a time.

The lies grew quieter. They never disappeared entirely; truth finally became louder than they were. That's what empathy ultimately taught me. Every person you meet is carrying a story — including you. Treat both with grace. Tell both the truth. Then keep moving forward, not carrying yesterday but learning from it. That's where healing begins. And healing has a remarkable way of changing not only the person receiving it, but every person whose life they eventually touch.

As I continued thinking about empathy, something else came into focus. The people who impacted my life the most all had one thing in common: they made me feel seen. It wasn't that they solved all my problems. They took the time to understand them.

I think back to my grandmother. She probably never realized how much she was shaping my life. She wasn't giving motivational speeches. She wasn't teaching leadership seminars. She simply loved me. Consistently. Quietly. Faithfully.

My father did the same thing. He wasn't perfect — neither am I — but he kept showing up. He coached my baseball teams. He took me hunting. He taught me to ski. He let me ride beside him while checking cattle, fixing fences, or spending an afternoon at the bank or accountant's office. At the time, I thought we were just running errands. Today I understand something much deeper. He was giving me his time. Time became trust. Trust became memories. Those memories eventually became part of the foundation I would stand on years later.

That's one of the reasons I believe empathy is expressed far more through actions than words. People may forget what we say. They rarely forget how we made them feel. Understanding that changed the way I approached leadership. Whether I was working with physicians, leading a team, coaching someone, or just talking with a friend, I stopped asking, "How do I impress this person?" I started asking, "How can I leave this person better than I found them?" That question became incredibly freeing. It shifted the focus away from me and toward them.

The funny thing about empathy is that it usually comes back to us. We're not looking for something in return; people naturally trust those who genuinely care. Trust isn't demanded. It's earned — one conversation at a time, one promise kept at a time, one relationship at a time.

I've come to believe that leadership is really stewardship. God places people in our lives for a reason. Our spouse. Our children. Our coworkers. Our friends. Sometimes complete strangers. Each interaction gives us an opportunity: to leave someone encouraged, to leave someone heard, to leave someone with a little more hope than they had before we met. That may be one of the greatest responsibilities we'll ever carry.

The older I become, the less interested I am in being remembered for accomplishments. I hope I'm remembered for how I treated people, because accomplishments eventually fade. Influence doesn't. The conversations we have today, the encouragement we give today, the grace we extend today — all of it may become part of someone else's story for years to come. We'll probably never know. That's okay. Most seeds are planted long before anyone sees the harvest. That's how my grandmother lived. That's how my father lived. That's how I hope to live.

Empathy isn't weakness. It's strength under control. It's choosing compassion when judgment would be easier. It's choosing curiosity before criticism. It's remembering that every person you meet is fighting a battle you know nothing about — including the person looking back at you in the mirror.

The world has enough critics. It has enough outrage. It has enough people determined to win every argument. What it desperately needs are more people willing to understand before they're understood. More people willing to listen before speaking. More people willing to love without keeping score. Those people change families. They change workplaces. They change communities. Sometimes they change generations.

When I trace my own life back, I can clearly see the people who changed mine. None of them changed it through perfection. They changed it through presence. Through consistency. Through love. I hope, by God's grace, that someone can someday say the same about me. Because if becoming has taught me anything, it's that success without relationships is empty. The people we love will always matter more than the things we achieve.

That's why empathy became the third pillar. Not because relationships are easy — because they're worth everything.
