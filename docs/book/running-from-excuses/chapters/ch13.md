# Chapter 13
## The Ripple Effect

One of the most surprising things about becoming is that it rarely stays with you. It spreads. At first, I thought this road was only about changing my own life. Quitting drinking. Becoming healthier. Being more present. Keeping promises. Learning to steward what God had entrusted to me. I thought those changes were personal. Then I began watching something happen that I never expected.

Other people started changing too.

It wasn't that I convinced them of anything. They watched consistency. That's one of the remarkable things about integrity: you never have to announce it. People notice. Not immediately, but over time. The same way excuses quietly compound, integrity quietly compounds too. One conversation becomes another. One example becomes another. One decision quietly influences another person to make one of their own. I don't think we appreciate how powerful that really is.

Every one of us is influencing someone. The question isn't whether we're leading. The question is where we're leading them. That thought challenged me deeply, because leadership isn't a title. Leadership is influence. Parents influence. Teachers influence. Coaches influence. Managers influence. Friends influence. Even strangers influence. Every interaction quietly leaves something behind — hope, discouragement, truth, fear, encouragement, or excuses. We're constantly planting seeds, whether we realize it or not.

One morning during a run, I found myself thinking about a single domino. By itself, it doesn't seem very significant. Small. Unremarkable. Easy to overlook. But tip it over and it hits the next one. Then the next. Eventually, something that began with one small movement creates momentum far beyond itself. Life works the same way. One difficult conversation may save a marriage. One encouraging word may keep someone from giving up. One honest story may convince another person they're not alone. One decision to become healthier may completely change the future your children inherit. The ripple almost always becomes larger than the stone that created it.

That's one of the reasons I stopped asking, "How will this affect me?" and started asking, "Who else might this affect?" Those are very different questions. The first focuses on personal benefit. The second focuses on legacy.

Legacy begins much earlier than most people think. It doesn't begin after we're gone. It begins every morning we choose how we're going to live. Every promise we keep. Every apology we make. Every difficult conversation we refuse to avoid. Every act of kindness no one else ever notices. Those small moments become someone else's example, whether we intended them to or not.

Understanding that filled me with both humility and hope. Humility, because I recognized how often people had quietly influenced me. Hope, because maybe my own ordinary decisions could eventually encourage someone else — not because they're extraordinary, but because they're honest. Honesty gives people permission to stop pretending.

And pretending has stolen enough lives already.

---

One truth kept returning to me as I watched those ripples spread: you never know who's watching. For a long stretch, I assumed nobody noticed the everyday choices I was making. The early morning runs. The books. The conversations. The quiet decisions to keep my word. I couldn't have been more wrong. People notice consistency. They may not say anything. They may never mention it. But they notice.

I think about my daughters often when I consider that truth. Children are always watching — not just when we think they're paying attention. Always. They're watching how we speak to our spouse and how we respond when life doesn't go our way. They're watching whether we tell the truth, whether we apologize, whether we keep our promises, whether our actions match our words. That's both a tremendous responsibility and an incredible opportunity, because children rarely become what we tell them. They usually become what we consistently show them.

That truth challenged me more than almost anything else. There were plenty of days when I didn't feel like running. There were mornings when the weather was miserable. There were business trips, vacations, long workdays. Excuses never completely disappeared. They just stopped making decisions for me.

Some mornings, before I walked out the door, one of my daughters would ask, "Dad, are you going running?" I always smiled, because eventually the answer became obvious. Of course I am. Not because I had to — because that's who I had become. They weren't merely watching a running streak. They were watching integrity, and that matters much more. One day they'll forget how many consecutive days I ran. I hope they never forget what consistency looked like.

The same is true in every area of life. People may not remember our speeches, but they remember our example. Examples quietly outlive words. That's why leadership has become so important to me — not leadership through position, but leadership through example. Anybody can tell another person what they should do. Living it? That's different. The older I become, the less interested I am in convincing people. I'd rather quietly show them. Not because I do it perfectly, but because consistency has a way of speaking for itself.

I've discovered something beautiful about becoming. When you're focused on faithfully living your own life, you rarely realize how many other people are quietly gathering courage from yours. That isn't pressure. It's purpose. Every small decision has the potential to become someone else's permission. Permission to begin. Permission to hope. Permission to tell the truth. Permission to stop making excuses. You may never know whose life your consistency is encouraging, and that's okay. Seeds are planted long before harvest ever appears.

That's how my grandmother lived. That's how my father lived. That's how every mentor who's impacted my life has lived. Quietly. Faithfully. Consistently. One ordinary day after another. Now I can see that none of those people were trying to become famous. They were only trying to become faithful. Ironically, that's exactly why their influence continues long after the day itself has passed.

---

As I continued thinking about influence, another question quietly found its way into my morning runs. What if the greatest impact of my life isn't something I'll ever personally see? That thought humbled me. We're naturally drawn toward immediate results. We like finishing projects, crossing finish lines, checking boxes, seeing progress. Influence rarely works that way. Influence usually unfolds slowly — sometimes over years, sometimes over generations. Think about the people who have influenced your own life.

My grandmother probably never imagined I'd still be talking about her rose garden decades later. My father probably never realized that a little boy riding beside him while checking cattle was learning far more than ranching. He was learning consistency. Responsibility. Faithfulness. He wasn't trying to create life lessons; he was living them. That's what made them so powerful. Authenticity always carries more influence than performance.

The same became true with my daughters. There were mornings when they saw me leave before sunrise for another run. Other mornings they saw me come home tired after a long day of work and still lace up my shoes because I hadn't run yet. They weren't listening to another lecture about commitment. They were watching it.

That understanding changed the way I looked at parenting. Children don't need parents who constantly tell them what matters. They need parents willing to show them. The older I become, the more convinced I am that our children are studying us far more than they're listening to us. They're studying how we respond to disappointment and how we handle conflict. How we treat waiters. How we speak about people who aren't in the room. How we love our spouse. How we handle stress. How we keep promises. That's why becoming matters so much. Whether we realize it or not, someone is always learning from the way we live.

One day I asked myself another question. If my daughters become exactly like me, would I be proud of that?

That question stopped me — not because I immediately loved the answer, but because it forced me to become brutally honest. There was a season of my life when I wouldn't have wanted that. I wouldn't have wanted them carrying anxiety the way I carried it. I wouldn't have wanted them believing lies the way I believed them. I wouldn't have wanted them using alcohol the way I did. That truth hurt. It also became one of the greatest motivations for change. I wasn't just rebuilding my life anymore. I was changing what my daughters would one day believe was normal.

That's legacy. Not leaving your children more money — leaving them a better example. Money eventually gets spent. Character continues growing long after we're gone. That's the inheritance I hope to leave behind. Not perfection, but faithfulness. Not extraordinary accomplishments, but ordinary integrity. Not a father who had life figured out, but a father who never stopped becoming. If that's what my daughters remember, I'll consider my life incredibly successful.

Because the ripple doesn't stop with them. One day, it may quietly continue through their children. And theirs. That's how one small decision, one everyday promise, one ordinary life can eventually influence generations.

---

The more I reflected on legacy, the more something became clear to me. Most people overestimate what they can accomplish in a year — and underestimate what God can do through a faithful life over decades. I know I certainly did. Through much of my life I wanted dramatic breakthroughs. Big moments. Life-changing opportunities. Now I find myself appreciating average Tuesdays. Regular dinners around the table. Everyday conversations with my wife. Routine runs before the sun comes up. Because I've learned something: ordinary moments are where life actually happens. The milestones exist to remind us to appreciate them.

One of the greatest compliments anyone has ever given me wasn't about work. It wasn't about running. It wasn't about finishing an ultramarathon. Someone once told me, "You're the same person in private that you are in public." I don't remember exactly where I was when they said it, but I remember exactly how it made me feel. Grateful. Because years earlier, that wouldn't have been true. I had become very good at wearing masks. Now I wanted nothing more than for my public life to match my private one. Not perfectly. Honestly.

Integrity has a remarkable way of simplifying life. When you're no longer pretending, you no longer have to remember which version of yourself you presented to different people. You just become yourself. That freedom is difficult to describe until you've experienced it.

I don't want my daughters to remember me as the man who ran every day; I hope they remember me as the man who kept showing up. I don't want them to remember me because I finished an ultramarathon; I hope they remember that I finished what I started. I don't want them to remember another accomplishment. I hope they remember the unremarkable evenings. The conversations. The laughter. The ski trips. The vacations. The times when Dad was fully present. Because presence has become one of the greatest demonstrations of love I know.

The more I think about influence, the less interested I become in building something people remember. I'd much rather build people. Businesses eventually close. Awards gather dust. Trophies eventually end up in storage boxes. But people — people carry your influence long after you're gone. That's why I no longer believe success is measured by what you leave behind. I believe it's measured by who continues becoming because you chose to become first.

If one father becomes more present because he read this book, that's success. If one husband chooses honesty over another excuse, that's success. If one young person asks, "Who do I have to become?" before making another decision, that's success. If one person decides their story isn't over yet, then every difficult chapter I lived becomes worthwhile.

I don't know who will eventually read these words. I don't know what chapter of life they'll be in. I don't know what burdens they're carrying. But I know this: hope has a remarkable way of multiplying. One honest conversation can change a family. One family can influence a community. One community can influence generations. Every ripple begins exactly the same way. One person decides to become. Then another. Then another.

That's why I believe shining your light has very little to do with being noticed. It has everything to do with faithfully living the life God has entrusted to you. The light isn't ours to create. It's only ours to carry. And the beautiful thing about light is this: it never runs out by being shared. It just makes the world a little brighter for the next person still trying to find their way.

---

One question has stayed with me ever since I began sharing my story. What if the greatest impact of your life is something you'll never personally see? I think about that often. My grandmother never lived to see me write this book. She never knew how many times I'd think about her rose garden while out on a run. She probably never imagined that decades later I'd still be talking about the quiet way she loved people. She wasn't trying to leave a legacy. She was living faithfully, nothing more.

My father probably never realized that a little boy riding beside him while checking cattle, stopping at the bank, repairing fences, or spending an afternoon at the accountant's office wasn't just learning about work. He was learning integrity. Responsibility. Consistency. Presence. He wasn't giving speeches. He was giving an example.

That's the thing about influence: the strongest examples are usually the quietest. Very few people intentionally set out to change generations. They choose to become faithful in the small moments instead. One conversation. One promise. One act of kindness. One difficult truth spoken with love. Then they repeat it tomorrow. I've come to believe that's how God usually works — not only through dramatic breakthroughs, but through everyday people who faithfully keep showing up.

That understanding has changed the way I define significance. I no longer ask, "Will people remember my name?" I find myself asking, "Will someone love their family better because our paths crossed?" "Will someone tell the truth because I finally told mine?" "Will someone decide not to quit because they learned I almost did?" Those questions matter much more to me. Because names are eventually forgotten. Lives changed continue changing other lives. That's a legacy worth pursuing.

I don't know where this book will eventually go. I don't know who will read it, or what chapter of life they'll be living when they do. Maybe someone will be sitting in a hotel room wondering if anything can change. Maybe someone will be standing in front of a mirror avoiding their own reflection. Maybe someone will be quietly carrying lies they've believed since childhood. Maybe someone will just need hope. If that's you, I want you to hear something I wish someone had told me long ago.

Your story is not over.

The chapter you're living today is exactly that — a chapter. Not the entire book. Don't confuse your current season with your final destination. I did that longer than I'd like to admit. I believed the restaurant defined me. It didn't. I believed alcohol defined me. It didn't. I believed childhood trauma defined me. It didn't. They became chapters. Important chapters. Painful chapters. Necessary chapters. But they were never the ending. The ending is still being written.

So is yours.

That's why I believe shining your light is much simpler than most people think. Tell the truth. Love people well. Keep your promises. Remain teachable. Point people toward hope whenever you have the opportunity. You don't have to change the entire world. You may only need to change one person's day. Then trust God with the ripple. Because He has always been much better at multiplying quiet faithfulness than we could ever imagine.
