# Chapter 3
## Momentum

One of the biggest misconceptions people have about transformation is that it happens all at once. It doesn't. From where I stand today, I can tell you there wasn't one morning when I woke up and suddenly became a different man. There wasn't one prayer, one workout, one conversation, or one breakthrough. There were hundreds of small decisions that slowly began moving my life in a different direction.

The rocking chair awakened me. The hotel room gave me hope. The coaching group gave me direction. Now came the hard part: living it.

Quitting drinking wasn't the finish line. It was the starting line. Alcohol had spent year after year covering up problems I refused to face, and the instant I removed it, every one of those problems was still waiting for me. The lawsuits. The debt. The anxiety. The embarrassment. None of them disappeared. For the first time in years, though, I had a clear mind to face them.

That changed everything.

Instead of spending another day avoiding difficult conversations, I started making them. Phone calls I had been putting off for months suddenly became today's responsibility. Financial decisions I had avoided finally got made. Paperwork that had been sitting untouched got touched. The remarkable thing about facing your problems is that most of them become smaller the second you stop running from them. Not easier. Smaller. Because uncertainty almost always grows larger in our imagination than it does in reality.

Every conversation I completed gave me a little more confidence. Every responsibility I handled gave me a little more peace. It wasn't that life had suddenly become easy; it was that I was finally moving instead of hiding. I often tell people today that action creates clarity. I didn't know those words back then. I only knew that every difficult thing I stopped avoiding became one less thing controlling my life.

Little by little, hope returned. I started sleeping better. My thinking became clearer. The constant fog that had followed me for so long slowly began lifting. My family noticed the difference before I fully did. I was more present, more engaged, more optimistic. Not perfect. Just different. For the first time in a very long time, I believed I might actually become the man I had started envisioning in that hotel room.

Momentum is an incredible feeling. Once you've spent years standing still, even small progress feels enormous. That's exactly where I found myself. I wasn't celebrating because everything had been fixed. I was celebrating because I was finally moving in the right direction.

Then something happened that I never saw coming. One day my coach made a statement that immediately stopped me. He said, "You can't do this without me."

I don't know exactly what everyone else in the room thought. I only know what happened inside me. Everything resisted those words. I remember sitting there thinking, "Watch me."

Now I can see that wasn't confidence. That was pride. There's a difference. At the time, though, I couldn't see it.

Part of my personality has always been incredibly stubborn. Sometimes that stubbornness has served me well. Other times it has gotten in my own way, and this became one of those times. Instead of hearing the value of community, I heard dependency. Instead of hearing someone encouraging continued accountability, I heard someone telling me I wasn't capable on my own. Those are two completely different messages. Unfortunately, I heard the wrong one.

So I left.

I honestly believed I was making the right decision. I believed I had learned everything I needed to learn. I believed I could continue growing without anyone else's help. I believed I had proven enough to myself that I could handle the rest alone. In hindsight, I wasn't leaving because I had become strong. I was leaving because my ego had quietly convinced me I no longer needed anyone. That's a dangerous place to live. I just didn't know it yet.

For a while, it looked like I had made the right decision. Life continued improving. I stayed away from alcohol. I kept working, kept paying down responsibilities, and kept believing I had everything under control. If someone had looked at my life from the outside, they probably would have said, "See? He didn't need the coaching group after all." Honestly, I probably would have agreed with them.

That's the dangerous thing about slow drift. It doesn't announce itself. It whispers. I didn't wake up one morning and decide to stop growing. I didn't consciously choose to become complacent. I just became a little less intentional.

One ordinary day at a time.

The daily conversations became less frequent. The accountability disappeared. The uncomfortable questions disappeared. The challenge slowly disappeared. At first, nothing looked different. Then life reminded me of something I had forgotten: growth is much easier to begin than it is to sustain. Those are two completely different skills. Starting requires courage. Sustaining requires humility. I had plenty of courage. Humility I was still learning.

Only later could I see exactly what had happened. I confused progress with transformation. Because life had improved, I assumed I had changed. Those aren't the same thing. Improvement can happen because your circumstances change. Transformation happens because you change. I was improving, but I hadn't finished transforming. The old man was still there. Quieter. Less obvious. But still there. Waiting.

One thing I now understand about discipline is this: discipline doesn't disappear. It gets replaced. If you stop intentionally building good habits, unhealthy ones quietly begin filling the empty space. Nature hates a vacuum. So does character. Without realizing it, I slowly relaxed the very disciplines that had helped me begin rebuilding my life. I wasn't drinking, but I also wasn't growing. Those aren't the same thing either. Growth requires intention, every day — not out of fear of slipping, but because becoming never stops. I didn't understand that yet.

At the time, I honestly thought I had arrived. Not completely, just enough that I stopped feeling the urgency I had once felt. Life has a remarkable way of exposing that kind of thinking. It doesn't usually happen immediately. It waits. Then it tests the very areas where you've quietly become comfortable. That's exactly what happened to me.

The world changed, almost overnight. The pandemic arrived. Hospitals closed their doors. Travel stopped. Face-to-face meetings disappeared. Medical sales no longer looked anything like medical sales. The relationships that had energized me for so many years suddenly became emails, phone calls, and virtual meetings. The part of my career I loved most wasn't selling products; it was sitting across the table from another human being. Listening. Learning. Helping solve problems. Now I found myself sitting alone behind a computer screen.

Every day looked almost identical. Wake up. Open the laptop. Answer emails. Make phone calls. Document everything. Close the laptop. Repeat. The work wasn't necessarily harder. It just wasn't fulfilling. I was technically busy all day, yet I felt disconnected from the part of my career that had always given me energy.

As if that wasn't enough, life added another unexpected challenge. Within days of each other, I underwent reconstructive wrist surgery followed by hip surgery. Almost overnight, the physical outlets that had always helped me manage stress disappeared. I couldn't lift. I couldn't train. I couldn't run. The very things that had helped quiet my mind were temporarily taken away.

Today I understand something important about that season. The pandemic didn't create my weaknesses. The surgeries didn't create my weaknesses. Just like the restaurant, they exposed them.

The boredom became familiar. The isolation became familiar. Old habits started whispering again, not because they were good, but because they were familiar. That's what unhealthy habits do. They don't usually return when life is easy. They wait until you're tired, until you're isolated, until the structure that once protected you quietly disappears.

At first, it wasn't alcohol. It was comfort. Comfort in small decisions. Comfort in telling myself, "I'll get back to it tomorrow." One skipped workout became another. One unhealthy meal became another. One excuse quietly invited the next. The frightening part wasn't that I was slipping. It was that I almost didn't notice.

That's how almost every decline begins. Not with one terrible decision. With a hundred small ones. The same way growth compounds, so does complacency. Little compromises eventually become new standards. Before long, what once would have bothered you becomes normal.

I don't think I was intentionally quitting. I was drifting. There's a huge difference. Nobody drifts toward becoming healthier. Nobody drifts toward a stronger marriage. Nobody drifts toward deeper faith. We drift toward comfort, toward convenience, toward whatever requires the least amount of intentionality. That's exactly where I found myself. The old excuses I thought I had defeated weren't dead. They had merely been waiting. Waiting for isolation. Waiting for fatigue. Waiting for me to believe I no longer needed the disciplines that had started changing my life.

That truth humbled me. It also taught me one of the most valuable lessons I've ever learned: growth isn't something you achieve. It's something you protect. Every day. Not out of fear of failing, but because life never stops testing your foundation.

The pandemic exposed mine. It showed me that I had improved, but that there were still parts of me that hadn't fully transformed. At first, that discovery discouraged me. Today I'm grateful for it. Because if those weaknesses hadn't been exposed then, they would have remained hidden, and hidden weaknesses have a way of becoming much bigger problems later.

One of the greatest gifts that difficult season gave me was honesty. It forced me to admit I wasn't as strong as I thought I was. It wasn't that I lacked potential; it was that I had quietly stopped doing the small things that made me strong in the first place.

That season reminded me of something I had watched my father and grandfather do my entire life. The work doesn't stop just because life becomes inconvenient. The cattle still need feeding. The fences still need repairing. The windmills still break. The work remains, every single day. No excuses. Watching those men growing up, I thought they were nothing more than hardworking farmers and ranchers. Now I can see they were living something much deeper.

Consistency.

They weren't motivated every day. They were committed. There's a difference. Motivation comes and goes. Commitment stays. That understanding became one of the greatest gifts my father ever gave me — not through a speech, but through an example. For years I had admired his work ethic. Now I finally understood it.

The older I get, the more convinced I become that consistency is one of the greatest demonstrations of love. Showing up. Again. And again. And again. That's what my father did. That's what my grandfather did. Somewhere along the way, I had drifted from that example.

The pandemic didn't only expose my weaknesses. It reminded me where I came from. It reminded me who I wanted to become. I knew I had two choices. I could continue drifting, or I could intentionally begin rebuilding again. This time I wasn't after another breakthrough; I wanted a foundation that could withstand whatever life brought next.

By the spring of 2021, I knew something had to change. Again. At first, that truth frustrated me. Part of me thought, "I already learned these lessons." I had. The problem wasn't the lessons. It was that I had stopped living them. That was a difficult truth to accept. Nobody had pulled me off course. I had just stopped steering. One ordinary decision at a time.

This time, I approached things differently. I wasn't looking for another emotional breakthrough. I wasn't looking for another motivational speech. I wasn't looking for another coach to rescue me. I was looking for consistency. Plain consistency. The kind that doesn't attract attention. The kind that quietly changes everything.

I began rebuilding the same way I had the first time. One decision. Then another. Then another. No grand announcement. No dramatic social media post. Just work, every day.

Slowly, momentum returned. I started training again, and my body began responding. More importantly, my mind began responding. The fog that had settled over me during the pandemic slowly began lifting. Hope returned. It wasn't that life had suddenly become easy; I was moving again. Movement creates hope. Standing still rarely does.

One goal found its way onto my calendar: another triathlon. I didn't need another medal. I needed another mountain. Training gave my days purpose again. It gave me structure. It gave me something to prepare for instead of merely reacting to whatever happened next.

I realize now that the race itself was never the point. The training was. Every workout became another promise kept. Every early morning became another vote for the man I wanted to become. The race gave those routine days a direction, nothing more.

When race day finally arrived in the fall of 2021, I crossed the finish line with a medal around my neck. People saw the finish. I saw something completely different. I saw hundreds of uneventful mornings. Hundreds of ordinary decisions. Hundreds of opportunities where I could have chosen comfort, and didn't. That's what I was most proud of. Not the race. The consistency.

Crossing that finish line reminded me of something I had forgotten: I was capable of far more than I had recently believed. Not because I had become extraordinary, but because I had become intentional again. That distinction matters. The race wasn't changing me. It was revealing me. It showed me that the discipline was still there. It had only been buried beneath comfort and complacency.

The question became: how do I keep this momentum? Not for another race. For life.

That question stayed with me for months. I thought about it while driving, while working, while training. I knew I needed something that couldn't be negotiated. Not another goal, because goals eventually end. I needed something that became part of my identity. I didn't know exactly what that looked like yet. I only knew I was tired of rebuilding the same life over and over again. I wanted to build something that would last. I wanted to become the kind of man who no longer negotiated with himself.

Now I believe God had already been preparing the answer. I just hadn't reached the day yet.

That day arrived on an average Monday. October 17, 2022. At first glance, there was nothing remarkable about it. It looked like every other Monday. Only later did I understand that it became one of the most important days of my life.

That morning, I laced up my shoes and went for a run. There was nothing extraordinary about the run itself. I had run thousands of miles before. I had completed marathons. I had completed an Ironman. I had trained for triathlons. Running wasn't new. The commitment was.

When I finished that run, a simple thought entered my mind. Tomorrow...I'm going to run again.

The next morning, I did. Then the next. Then the next. No announcement. No challenge. No social media post. No audience. Just a promise. A promise to myself.

I understand now that I wasn't creating a goal. I was creating a standard. Goals eventually end. Standards become part of your identity. At the time, I didn't know how many days I would run. I never said thirty. I never said one hundred. I never said one year. I decided one thing: I wasn't going to miss. That small decision quietly changed everything. Every morning I had one question to answer. Will I keep my word today? The run was never the point. Integrity was.

Around that same time, I grabbed three Post-it notes and stuck them to my bathroom mirror. Nothing fancy. Nothing motivational. Just three reminders I desperately needed to see every single day.

Who do I have to become?

Right Now.

Your integrity is more important than what you sell.

Those three notes became my daily conversation. Some mornings they encouraged me. Other mornings they exposed me. Every morning they reminded me that becoming wasn't about another finish line. It was about another regular day.

The first question eventually became the foundation of everything that followed. Who do I have to become? Not "How do I make more money?" Not "How do I lose weight?" Not "How do I fix my marriage?" Those are outcome questions. This became an identity question. Who do I have to become to build a stronger marriage? Who do I have to become to earn trust? Who do I have to become to become the father my daughters deserve? Who do I have to become to become the man God created me to be? Every meaningful change in my life eventually pointed back to that single question.

The second note said only two words. Right Now. No excuses. No tomorrow. No Monday. No waiting until life settled down. Right now. If a difficult conversation needed to happen, right now. If an apology needed to be made, right now. If something important needed my attention, right now. Procrastination had already stolen enough years from my life. I wasn't giving it another day.

The third note became deeply personal. Your integrity is more important than what you sell. At first, I wrote it because of my career. Eventually it dawned on me that it had almost nothing to do with sales. Integrity matters when nobody else is watching. Integrity matters when nobody would know if you skipped the run. Integrity matters because you know. That became enough.

As the days turned into weeks, something remarkable happened. I stopped asking myself whether I felt like running. I simply ran. Feelings became less important. Commitment became more important. Little by little, the excuses became quieter. They hadn't disappeared; my discipline had finally become louder.

I don't think the running streak changed my life because of the miles. It changed my life because every single morning I reminded myself of one simple truth.

I am the kind of man who keeps his word.

That identity became stronger than any excuse I could create. And once that happened, everything else slowly began changing with it. I had no idea where those quiet morning runs would eventually lead. I only knew one thing. For the first time in a very long time, I wasn't running from my life anymore.

I was finally running toward it.
