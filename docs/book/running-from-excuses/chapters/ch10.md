# Chapter 10
## Economics

For most of my life, I thought money was the goal. It wasn't greed driving that belief. I genuinely thought money would solve my problems. If I made more, life would become easier. If I built a successful business, I'd finally feel successful. If I could just get ahead financially, everything else would eventually fall into place. I know a lot of people who believe that. I used to be one of them.

The restaurant taught me something I never expected: money is a terrible foundation for your identity. Businesses succeed, and businesses fail. Markets rise, and markets fall. Jobs change, and economies change right along with them. If your identity rises and falls with those things, you'll spend your entire life on an emotional roller coaster. I know, because I lived it. When the restaurant failed, I didn't just lose a business. I believed I had lost myself.

That was one of the greatest financial lessons I've ever learned. Your net worth and your self-worth are not the same thing. They never have been, and they never will be. When that truth finally landed, it freed me, because for a long stretch I had unknowingly tied my value to my performance. A good sales month, and I felt valuable. A bad month, and I questioned myself. A successful business made me feel confident, and a failed business made me feel like a failure. Nothing about that way of living is sustainable. Eventually it sank in that money makes a wonderful tool and a terrible master.

The more I reflected on it, the more I understood something my father had quietly modeled my entire life. Money was never the goal. Stewardship was. Growing up on a farm and ranch, I watched my father take care of things — equipment, land, animals, people. Nothing was wasted. That wasn't because we had unlimited resources; it was because every resource mattered.

That lesson followed me into adulthood. Everything we own is eventually something we've been entrusted to manage: our money, our time, our opportunities, our influence, even our talents. None of them truly belong to us. They're gifts. So the question isn't, "How much do I have?" The better question is, "How well am I managing what I've already been given?"

That one question completely changed the way I looked at finances. Instead of constantly chasing more, I started becoming grateful for what I already had. Gratitude has a remarkable way of changing financial decisions. Grateful people usually make better stewards, while entitled people usually make bigger consumers. I've been both. Stewardship creates peace; consumption never seems satisfied. The more I chased things, the less fulfilled I became. The more I focused on stewardship, the more freedom I experienced. That surprised me, because freedom isn't found in owning everything.

Freedom is found in needing less.

The restaurant taught me that. The lawsuits reinforced it, and the rebuilding years confirmed it. Money matters — I'm not pretending it doesn't. It provides opportunities, it creates choices, and it allows generosity. But money has never been the destination. It's one more area where integrity matters, one more area where becoming matters, one more area where small decisions quietly compound over time.

One of the greatest financial mistakes I ever made had nothing to do with investing. It had everything to do with believing that more would finally be enough. I kept chasing the next opportunity, the next promotion, the next business, the next accomplishment. The finish line kept moving. Every time I reached what I thought would finally satisfy me, another target appeared. That cycle is exhausting — and ambition isn't the problem. The problem is that fulfillment was never designed to come from accomplishment alone. The restaurant exposed that lie. Medical sales challenged it. Running eventually confirmed it.

Happiness doesn't come after success. For me, happiness had to come before it.

That statement probably would have frustrated a younger version of me. At one point in my life, I honestly believed happiness was something you earned. Earn enough money, build enough success, accomplish enough goals — then you'd finally be happy. Life taught me the opposite. Happy people usually build better businesses. Happy people usually become better spouses, and happy people usually become better parents. It isn't that life is easier for them. It's that they aren't asking accomplishments to provide something accomplishments were never designed to give: peace.

Understanding that changed the way I looked at work. I still work hard — probably harder than ever. The difference is that work no longer defines me. It gives me an opportunity to serve, to provide, to create value, to steward the abilities God has given me. That's very different from asking work to determine my worth.

One lesson from medical sales has stayed with me ever since I learned it. The best sales representatives aren't focused on selling; they're focused on solving problems. That shift completely changed my career. Every morning I stopped asking, "How do I make another sale?" Instead I asked, "How can I create more value today?" The amazing thing is that income eventually followed value. Not immediately, and not perfectly, but consistently. That's how stewardship usually works: value first, reward second.

The same principle applies almost everywhere in life. Healthy marriages create value. Great parents create value. Honest leaders create value, and faithful employees create value. Money usually follows people who consistently create value for others. That doesn't mean every faithful person becomes wealthy. It just means value has a remarkable way of opening doors.

The more I thought about stewardship, the more something became clear to me: money amplifies character. If someone is generous with very little, they're usually generous with much. If someone lacks integrity with very little, more money rarely fixes that problem — it usually magnifies it. That's why stewardship starts long before wealth ever arrives. It's learned through everyday decisions: paying bills on time, keeping promises, living below your means, giving generously, being grateful. Those habits quietly become character, and character eventually shapes every financial decision you'll ever make.

The more I understood that, the less interested I became in appearing successful. I became much more interested in becoming faithful. Faithfulness has become one of my favorite words. Faithful husband. Faithful father. Faithful steward. Faithful friend. Faithful employee. Faithful leader. Those titles matter much more to me than successful businessman ever will, because success can disappear. Faithfulness stays with you.

One question eventually became a filter for almost every financial decision I made: Does this move me toward freedom, or farther away from it? Years ago, I would have answered that question very differently. Back then, I thought freedom meant having more money. Today, I think freedom means needing less. There's an incredible difference between the two. The more I chased things, the more I discovered there was always something else to chase — a newer vehicle, a larger home, a different title, a bigger account, another accomplishment. The finish line kept moving, and contentment never seemed to arrive.

Then something unexpected happened. As my priorities began changing, so did my definition of wealth. Wealth became having dinner with my family without thinking about work. Wealth became taking my daughters on a ski trip. Wealth became watching my wife laugh while we explored another part of the world together. Wealth became having the health to climb a mountain, to run another trail, to throw a football in the backyard, to be fully present.

Experiences slowly became far more valuable than possessions. There's nothing wrong with possessions; the difference is that experiences become part of us. Nobody can repossess a memory. Nobody can foreclose on time spent with your family, and nobody can take away the conversations that changed your life. Those become part of who you are forever.

One of the greatest gifts my parents and grandparents gave me wasn't money. It was experiences. Hunting trips with my dad. Washington, D.C. with my grandmother. Watching football with my grandparents, working cattle, driving fence lines, skiing together. Those memories have appreciated in value every single year of my life. That's a remarkable investment. The older I become, the more convinced I am that experiences are the world's most valuable currency. They're one of the few investments that continue increasing in value long after they're over.

Seeing that changed the way my wife and I began making decisions. Instead of asking, "What can we buy?" we found ourselves asking, "What will our daughters remember?" That's a completely different conversation. Children rarely remember the price of Christmas. They remember how it felt. They remember traditions, vacations, road trips, Saturday mornings, conversations around the dinner table — unremarkable moments. Those moments quietly become their childhood, and one day they'll become their stories. That thought carries tremendous responsibility, because whether we realize it or not, we're writing our children's memories every single day.

Money can certainly create opportunities. But it can never replace presence. I learned that lesson from my own father. He worked incredibly hard, yet somehow he still found time. Time to coach. Time to hunt. Time to ski. Time to let a little boy ride beside him while checking cattle or stopping by the bank. At the time, I thought we were just spending the day together. Today I understand he was giving me something money could never buy: his attention, his example, his presence. That may have been the greatest investment he ever made in me. It's certainly one of the greatest investments I hope to make in my own daughters.

The more I thought about stewardship, the more I saw that money is only one resource among many. Time, energy, attention, relationships, influence — they're all forms of wealth, and they're all entrusted to us. The question has never been, "How much do I have?" The question is, "What am I doing with what I've already been given?" That question has a way of simplifying life, because stewardship isn't measured by comparison. It's measured by faithfulness.

And faithfulness always begins today.

One lesson the restaurant taught me that I never could have learned from a textbook was the difference between ownership and stewardship. Ownership says, "This is mine." Stewardship says, "This has been entrusted to me." That shift in thinking changed almost every part of my life. My house isn't really mine; it's something God has entrusted to my family. My career isn't really mine; it's an opportunity to create value for others. Even my daughters aren't my possessions. They're gifts, and I'm trusted to raise them well. That perspective removed an enormous amount of pressure. I no longer felt like I had to control everything. I only needed to faithfully manage what had already been placed in front of me.

That applies to money too. One of the greatest financial habits my wife and I developed wasn't making more. It was becoming intentional. Money has a way of disappearing when it doesn't have a purpose. Every dollar eventually goes somewhere; the question is whether you decided where, or whether it decided for you. Intentional living changed that for us. We began talking about our priorities before making purchases. We weren't trying to eliminate fun. We wanted our spending to reflect our values.

That's a very revealing exercise. If someone looked at your calendar and your bank statement, would they be able to identify what matters most to you? That's an uncomfortable question. It was for me too, because money tells the truth — not about how much we have, but about what we value. I discovered there were seasons where I claimed family was my highest priority while my calendar quietly disagreed. I claimed health mattered while my habits disagreed. I claimed faith mattered while my schedule disagreed. Results have a remarkable way of exposing reality. That isn't condemnation; it's clarity. And clarity gives us an opportunity to change, one decision at a time.

I've also learned something else about stewardship: generosity changes the giver as much as the receiver. When we hold everything tightly, fear usually grows. When we learn to give, trust grows. That doesn't mean giving irresponsibly. It means recognizing that everything we have is temporary. We arrive with nothing, and we leave with nothing. Somewhere in between, we become managers. The older I get, the more freedom I find in open hands. Open hands receive, and open hands give. Closed fists usually do neither.

That's one of the reasons gratitude has become such an important part of my life. Grateful people tend to become generous people, and generous people tend to become joyful people. Giving doesn't always change their circumstances, but it does change their perspective. I've discovered that contentment isn't found when we finally have enough. Contentment begins the instant we become grateful for what's already enough. That's a lesson I'm still learning, every single day.

One of my favorite definitions of success has become remarkably simple. Success is faithfully stewarding everything God has entrusted to you. Not someone else's life — yours. Not someone else's opportunities — yours. Not someone else's gifts — yours. Comparison quietly disappears when stewardship becomes the goal, because God has never asked me to manage your life. He's asked me to faithfully manage mine. Understanding that brought an incredible amount of peace.

It also reminded me that every pillar we've talked about so far shares something in common. They're all ultimately about stewardship. Stewarding your body. Stewarding your mind. Stewarding your relationships. Stewarding your resources. Everything points back to becoming the kind of person God can trust with whatever He chooses to place in your hands. That became my new definition of wealth — not what I owned, but who I was becoming.

As I've reflected on this pillar over the years, I've come to see that stewardship isn't really about money. Money is only one expression of it. Stewardship is ultimately about trust. Can God trust me with what He's already given me? That question reaches much farther than finances. Can He trust me with my marriage? Can He trust me with my children? Can He trust me with influence? Can He trust me with success? Can He trust me with adversity? Can He trust me with another opportunity? Those questions have become much more important to me than, "How do I get more?"

I've discovered that people who constantly chase more rarely enjoy what they already have. I know, because I lived there. There was always another goal, another account, another race, another mountain, another milestone. Eventually the truth caught up with me. If I couldn't be grateful where I was, nothing waiting in the future was going to permanently change that. Gratitude isn't created by abundance. Abundance is often recognized through gratitude.

That one lesson quietly changed my entire perspective. Instead of asking, "What do I still need?" I began asking, "What have I already been given that I'm overlooking?" That question has a remarkable way of slowing life down. It reminds me of conversations around my grandparents' table, of hunting trips with my dad, of Washington, D.C., with my grandmother, of my daughters placing an ultramarathon medal around my neck. None of those experiences showed up on a financial statement, yet they represent some of the greatest wealth I've ever known. Money has certainly made some of those experiences possible, but it never created their value. Love did. Presence did. Time did.

I've often thought about the end of life. I don't believe anyone sits on their deathbed wishing they had answered one more email, closed one more sale, or accumulated one more possession. I think they remember people. Conversations. Experiences. Laughter. Faithfulness. The ordinary moments they almost overlooked while they were busy chasing extraordinary ones.

That's one of the reasons my definition of success has changed so dramatically. Today, success looks remarkably plain. Being present with my wife. Laughing with my daughters. Serving customers with integrity. Keeping my word. Taking care of the body God entrusted to me. Growing a little wiser than I was yesterday. Helping someone else believe their life can change. That's success. Everything else is a bonus.

I don't say that because money doesn't matter. It does. It allows generosity, it creates opportunities, and it provides security. But money has never been the destination. It's another tool — a tool that can accomplish tremendous good when placed in the hands of someone who understands stewardship.

The beautiful thing about stewardship is that it keeps us grounded. It reminds us that everything is temporary — every opportunity, every possession, every accomplishment. One day we'll hand all of it back. The only thing that will remain is who we became while we were entrusted with it. That thought has become one of the greatest motivations in my life. Not to accumulate more, but to become more faithful. Because faithfulness compounds, just like excuses once did. One everyday decision. One quiet conversation. One small act of integrity. Day after day, year after year, until one day you look back and see that you didn't just build a better financial life.

You built a better life.

One that isn't measured by what you own, but by what you faithfully steward. The four pillars all lead me back to the same conclusion. Everything I have, everything I am, and everything I hope to become has been entrusted to me for a season. My responsibility isn't to compare my season with someone else's. It's to be faithful in my own. That understanding quietly brought the first four pillars together — not as separate parts of life, but as one life. One foundation. One question. One daily decision.

Who do I have to become today?
