# Chapter 5
## Who Do I Have to Become?

There are certain questions that quietly change the direction of your life. It isn't that they're complicated. It's that they're honest.

For most of my life, I asked the same questions everyone else asks. How do I make more money? How do I lose weight? How do I grow my business? How do I save my marriage? How do I become successful? None of those are bad questions, and I've asked every one of them. The problem wasn't the questions themselves. The problem was where they began. Every one of them started with how.

Year after year I chased better strategies, better systems, better routines, better plans. I honestly believed that if I could just discover the right method, my life would eventually fall into place. It never did.

The running streak quietly exposed why. Every morning I laced up my shoes, I wasn't getting healthier because I had found a better training plan. I was getting healthier because I had become someone who kept promises. Identity came first. Everything else followed. That truth changed the way I looked at every area of my life.

One morning, standing in front of my bathroom mirror, I caught myself staring at the same three Post-it notes I had looked at every day for months. Suddenly, one question seemed to stand above all the others.

Who do I have to become?

I stood there for a long time thinking about those six words. The more I sat with them, the more I understood they were completely different from every goal I had ever written down. Goals ask what you want. This question asked who you must become before those things naturally follow. That completely changed the direction of my thinking.

Instead of asking, "How do I build a better marriage?" I asked, "Who does my wife deserve?" Instead of asking, "How do I become healthier?" I asked, "Who naturally lives the life I'm trying to build?" Instead of asking, "How do I become successful?" I asked, "Who creates enough value that success eventually becomes the byproduct?"

Those are very different questions. One chases outcomes. The other transforms identity.

The more I lived with that question, the more something else became clear: the answer never stayed the same. The man I needed to become to quit drinking wasn't the same man I needed to become to complete an ultramarathon. The man I needed to become to earn trust wasn't the same man I needed to become to keep trust. The man I needed to become at forty wasn't the same man I needed to become at forty-five. Growth changes the answer, and that's exactly how it should be, because becoming never ends. Every season asks something different from us.

That's one of the reasons that Post-it note has never come off my mirror. The question is still alive. Every morning it asks me the same thing: Who are you becoming today? Not next year. Not after another promotion. Not after another race. Today.

That one question quietly eliminated hundreds of smaller ones, because once I knew who I wanted to become, many of my daily decisions became much easier. Would the man I'm becoming have another drink? No. Would the man I'm becoming skip another promise? No. Would the man I'm becoming avoid that difficult conversation? No.

Identity began answering questions before discipline ever had to. That's when something remarkable sank in: the strongest discipline usually grows out of a clear identity, not the other way around.

The mirror eventually became much more than a place where I brushed my teeth each morning. It became a conversation — not with my reflection, but with myself. Every morning those three handwritten notes quietly asked me the same question. Who are you becoming? Not, what are you accomplishing? Not, how much money are you making? Not, how many miles have you run?

Who are you becoming?

The longer I lived with that question, the clearer one thing became. Results never lie. I could tell myself I valued my health; my habits answered honestly. I could tell myself I valued my marriage; my presence answered honestly. I could tell myself I valued integrity; my daily choices answered honestly. Results don't judge us. They reveal us, nothing more. Oddly enough, I found that freeing, because once the truth is exposed, you finally know where to begin.

For a long stretch of my life I had spent enormous amounts of energy trying to change outcomes. Lose weight. Make more money. Build a better business. Become more successful. The problem wasn't the goals. The problem was that I kept trying to change the fruit without ever examining the roots. Healthy roots naturally produce healthy fruit, and the same is true in life. If I became healthier, better habits would naturally follow. If I became more disciplined, consistency would naturally follow. If I became a better husband, my marriage would naturally grow stronger. Everything kept pointing back to identity.

That understanding followed me everywhere — during sales calls, during workouts, while traveling, even while sitting in airports. I found myself asking, "Who does this situation require me to become?" Sometimes the answer was patience. Sometimes courage. Sometimes humility. Sometimes discipline. Sometimes it was nothing more than listening instead of talking. The answer changed. The question never did.

One morning another thought found its way onto my bathroom mirror.

You versus you.

At first, those three words didn't seem particularly profound, but the longer I looked at them, the more freedom they created. For as long as I could remember, I had quietly compared myself to everyone else. Other sales representatives. Other runners. Other fathers. Other husbands. Other people on social media who always seemed to have life figured out. Comparison became exhausting, because no matter how much progress I made, someone always appeared farther ahead.

Eventually the truth landed. I wasn't competing against anyone else. I was competing against yesterday's version of myself. That was the only comparison that actually mattered. Could I become a little more patient today? Could I become a little more disciplined today? Could I become a little more present today? Those became races worth running, because those races never required someone else to lose for me to win.

Another Post-it eventually appeared.

Always grateful. Never satisfied.

At first glance, those ideas seem to contradict one another. They don't. Gratitude reminds me to appreciate where I am. Never being satisfied reminds me that growth never stops. I can be deeply thankful for today while still believing God isn't finished with me tomorrow. Both can exist at the same time. One keeps me humble. The other keeps me moving.

Over time, another thought found its place on the mirror: You are the CEO of your life. Hire. Fire. Promote. Accordingly. The first time I wrote it, I laughed. Then I thought about it. Every habit is an employee. Every relationship has influence. Every repeated thought eventually produces behavior. Some habits deserve promotion; others need to be fired. Some relationships deserve greater access; others require healthy boundaries. Some thoughts deserve to be believed; others should never have been hired in the first place.

The more time I spent standing in front of that mirror, the more I understood those notes weren't motivational. They were diagnostic. Every morning they exposed where my life wasn't matching the man I claimed I wanted to become. That isn't always comfortable — growth rarely is. But honesty has a remarkable way of simplifying life, because once you stop lying to yourself, you finally know where the work belongs.

One quote challenged me more than almost anything else during that season. It wasn't a quote I immediately liked. In fact, the first time I heard it, I wanted to argue with it. It said, plainly, "The truth only offends those who live outside of it."

That sentence stayed with me — not because it made me feel good, but because it exposed me. For most of my adult life I had surrounded myself with ideas that protected my comfort. Now I was intentionally surrounding myself with ideas that challenged it. Those are two very different ways to live. Comfort rarely transforms us. Truth does.

The more honest I became with myself, the less interested I became in collecting information. I became much more interested in applying it. For years I believed another book would change my life. Then I believed it would be another coach. Another seminar. Another podcast. Another breakthrough. Eventually something became obvious: information had never been my problem. Application was.

I already knew I should exercise. I already knew alcohol wasn't helping me. I already knew my marriage deserved more of me. I already knew excuses were costing me years. Knowledge wasn't missing. Execution was. That truth humbled me. I had spent so much of my life collecting ideas that never became habits.

In hindsight, I think that's where many people become frustrated. They confuse learning with changing, and they're not the same thing. Knowledge informs. Application transforms. The world is full of informed people; transformation belongs to the people willing to live what they've learned. One page applied is worth more than an entire bookshelf ignored. One difficult conversation is worth more than another motivational speech. One promise consistently kept is worth more than another notebook full of goals.

That understanding completely changed the way I approached growth. I stopped asking, "What else do I need to know?" Instead, I started asking, "What do I already know that I'm not living?" That question exposed me — and it wasn't a lack of information it revealed. It was a lack of consistency. The answer wasn't another lesson. The answer was another normal day lived with intention.

Little by little, I stopped chasing dramatic breakthroughs and started honoring plain repetition. The same habits. The same disciplines. The same commitments. Again. And again. And again. Most people underestimate repetition because repetition isn't exciting. But repetition quietly creates mastery. The basics, repeated consistently, eventually become extraordinary.

I think about my father often when I consider that idea. Every day he woke up and handled the basics. The cattle. The fences. The equipment. The land. Nothing glamorous. Nothing flashy. Just the work that mattered. Life works exactly the same way. The basics never stopped working. We just stopped valuing them.

That's when I began understanding something that would eventually organize every area of my life. I wasn't trying to become healthier, or wealthier, or more successful. I was trying to become more intentional. Because intentional people don't merely react to life. They prepare for it.

That thought quietly became the bridge to everything that followed. For so long I had been reacting. Now I wanted to begin living proactively — not because I had life figured out, but because I was finally becoming the kind of man who understood that ordinary preparation changes extraordinary moments. I just didn't know how to organize that way of thinking yet. That answer would come one everyday run, one small thought, and one simple framework that eventually became the foundation for everything else.

One morning during a run, a simple picture came into my mind. A table. Nothing elaborate — just a table with four legs. If one leg weakens, the table doesn't immediately collapse. It begins to wobble.

The longer I thought about it, the more I saw that's exactly what had happened in my own life. For as long as I could remember, I had treated every area of my life as though it existed independently. If my health suffered, that was one problem. If my marriage struggled, that was another. If my finances became stressful, that was something completely separate. The truth was much different. Everything was connected. When my energy declined, my patience declined. When my patience declined, my relationships suffered. When my relationships suffered, my mindset followed. When my mindset suffered, everything else eventually felt the effects.

For too long I had been trying to repair individual problems while ignoring the foundation holding everything together. No wonder life always seemed out of balance.

The more I thought about that table, the more obvious it became. I didn't need another complicated system. I needed something simple enough to remember every day. Something practical. Something honest. Something I could return to regardless of what life looked like.

I filled notebook after notebook during that season — pages of ideas, thoughts from morning runs, lessons from books, questions I kept asking myself. As I read back through those pages, I noticed something. Almost everything I had written fit into one of four areas. My body. My mind. My relationships. My stewardship. The categories were different, but the purpose was the same. Each one represented an area where I needed to become more intentional.

Little by little, those four areas became clearer. I wasn't trying to invent another self-improvement program; I was trying to simplify my own life. Whenever life felt overwhelming, I needed a place to return. Not another motivational speech. Not another complicated plan. Just the fundamentals — the same way my father approached the ranch. Every day he returned to the basics. The fences. The cattle. The equipment. The land. Nothing glamorous. Just faithful stewardship of what had already been entrusted to him.

That picture stayed with me. Maybe life wasn't asking me to become more complicated. Maybe it was inviting me back to the fundamentals.

That understanding brought an incredible sense of peace. For the first time in years, I wasn't asking, "How do I fix my life?" I was asking, "Which part of my foundation needs attention today?" That question felt manageable. Honest. Actionable. Instead of trying to rebuild everything at once, I could strengthen one leg of the table. Then another. Then another. Little by little, the wobble disappeared — not because life became easier, but because the foundation became stronger.

I didn't know it yet, but those four simple areas would eventually become the framework I now call E4. Creating a brand had nothing to do with it. I was trying to build a life that wouldn't collapse every time another storm arrived. The more I lived it, the more convinced I became of one simple truth. Strong lives are rarely built on extraordinary ideas. They're built on ordinary fundamentals, lived consistently.

That's where I finally stopped reacting to life and began preparing for it.

The framework didn't appear all at once. It wasn't something I sat down and designed in a single afternoon. It emerged through years of failure, years of rebuilding, and years of asking one question over and over again.

Who do I have to become?

The answer kept changing. The categories didn't. No matter what season I was in, every challenge seemed to point back to one of four areas. If my body was neglected, everything else became more difficult. If my mind wasn't growing, my decisions suffered. If my relationships weakened, success felt empty. If I wasn't faithfully managing what God had entrusted to me, peace disappeared. Everything was connected.

That truth simplified my life in a way I never expected. Instead of trying to fix everything at once, I could just ask, which pillar needs my attention today? Some days the answer was my health. Some days it was my attitude. Some days it was spending uninterrupted time with my wife or my daughters. Some days it was making a phone call I had been avoiding or taking responsibility for a financial decision. The answer changed. The process stayed the same. Tell yourself the truth. Take the next step. Keep your promise. Repeat tomorrow.

The older I get, the less interested I become in complicated systems. Complicated systems usually impress people. Simple systems change people. I wanted something my daughters could understand. Something a high school student could use. Something a young couple could build their marriage around. Something a business owner could apply on Monday morning. Something I could still be living twenty years from now. That's what this framework became. Not another program. A way of living.

From where I stand today, I can see how each pillar quietly rescued another part of my life. Taking care of my body gave me the energy to become present again. Sharpening my mind changed the way I responded to problems. Strengthening relationships reminded me that success means very little if you arrive alone. Learning to steward my time, money, opportunities, and influence brought a kind of freedom I had spent years chasing in all the wrong places.

None of those pillars stood alone. Each one supported the others, just like the legs of a table. Remove one, and everything becomes unstable. Strengthen all four, and life becomes remarkably resilient.

I wish someone had handed me that framework when I was twenty years old. It wouldn't have prevented every mistake. It wouldn't have removed every hardship. But I believe it would have helped me respond to those hardships very differently. Then again, maybe I had to live through everything first. Maybe the restaurant, the lawsuits, the depression, the rocking chair, the hotel room, the pandemic, the routine morning runs — maybe all of those seasons were preparing me to understand something I couldn't have appreciated any other way.

Today, I don't see E4 as four categories. I see it as stewardship. Stewardship of the body God gave me. Stewardship of the mind He's continuing to shape. Stewardship of the people He's placed in my life. Stewardship of every opportunity He's entrusted to me.

When I finally began looking at life through that lens, everything changed. Not overnight. Not dramatically. One small decision, one quiet promise, one ordinary day.

At a time.

Those four pillars would eventually become the foundation for the rest of this book — not because I believe they're the only framework that works, but because they're the framework that rebuilt my life. And before we explore each pillar individually, there's one more distinction that completely changed the way I think. For so long I had lived reactively. The future I wanted required something entirely different. It required learning how to live proactively.
