# Chapter 2

## The Cost of Excuses

There are few lies more dangerous than the ones we tell ourselves. Not because they're convincing, but because we want them to be convincing.

For a long stretch of my life, if someone asked me how I was doing, I gave the exact same answer: "I'm fine." Friends asked, and I said it. Coworkers asked, and I said it. Family asked, and I said it. Customers asked, "How's everything going?" and the answer came back the same every single time, word for word. "I'm fine." The strange thing is that eventually I started believing my own answer — or at least I wanted to believe it. The truth was very different from the answer I kept giving. I wasn't fine, and I hadn't been fine for a very long time.

By this point in my life, I had become very good at wearing a mask. The little boy who had learned to hide eventually became a man who knew how to pretend. From the outside, life looked successful enough. I had graduated from college. I had built a career in medical sales. I had gotten married, and I had become a father. Anyone looking in from the outside probably would have assumed everything was going according to plan. What they couldn't see was what was happening underneath the surface. Neither could I.

One of the dreams I had carried since I was young was opening my own restaurant. Growing up around my dad's catering business, I had fallen in love with the idea of bringing people together over great food. For me, it was never just about owning a business. It was about creating something. It was about building something. It was about serving people. I poured everything I had into that dream — my time, my energy, my money, my heart. Like many entrepreneurs, I believed that hard work alone would be enough. Sometimes it is. Sometimes it isn't. The restaurant struggled, and then it failed.

People often summarize that season of my life by saying, "The restaurant closed." That's true. It's also a massive understatement. Closing the doors wasn't the hardest part of it; the hardest part came afterward. Saturday mornings became something I dreaded, because every knock at the door made my stomach tighten. More than once, I opened the front door to find someone standing there waiting to serve me with another lawsuit. Every single week seemed to bring another reminder of everything that had gone wrong.

Before I ever dealt with the lawsuits, though, there was something far more important to me: my employees. Those people had trusted me, and many of them depended on those paychecks to get by. Before I started worrying about myself, I made sure every one of them received their final paycheck. I made sure payroll was handled and the taxes were addressed. The responsibilities that belonged to me stayed with me. Only after every one of those obligations had been taken care of did I finally begin facing what remained.

And there was a lot remaining. There was debt. There were lawsuits. There was embarrassment, and there was failure. The pressure seemed endless. What made it even more difficult was that I genuinely believed I had failed everyone around me — my family, my employees, my investors, and myself. The restaurant wasn't just a business to me. It had become part of my identity, and when it failed, I quietly believed I had failed right along with it. Now I can see something I couldn't see then. Businesses fail. People don't. At the time, though, I couldn't separate the two. Failure felt deeply personal.

The financial pressure continued growing. The lawsuits continued coming. The anxiety became a constant companion, and the depression quietly settled in. And somewhere during that season, alcohol stopped being something I occasionally enjoyed and became something I depended on. I didn't start drinking because I was celebrating. I started drinking because I wanted to feel different. From where I stand today, I can see that's an important distinction.

Most people assume alcohol was the problem. For me, alcohol wasn't the problem — at least not initially. It was the solution I chose. The real problem was what I was trying to solve. There were the wounds from childhood. There was the pressure from the business. There was the constant anxiety, the depression, the fear, the embarrassment, and the feeling that I was letting everyone down. Alcohol temporarily quieted all of it. At least I thought it did. For a little while, the noise stopped. For a little while, I felt lighter. For a little while, I didn't have to think at all. That's what made it so dangerous. It seemed to work. The problem was that every morning, everything returned — and it usually came back stronger than before.

The cycle became painfully predictable. I would wake up. I would carry the weight. I would go to work, come home, have a drink, and feel a little better. Then I would wake up the next morning and carry even more weight than I had carried the day before. It went on like that, over and over again.

At the time, I would have told you I was a functional alcoholic, and I actually believed those words when I said them. After all, I could still get up every morning. I could still provide for my family. I could still perform at work, and I could still smile. From the outside, nothing looked completely out of control. That's why the word functional became so dangerous. It gave me permission to stay exactly where I was. These days I don't even like using that phrase, because the truth is I wasn't functioning. I was surviving. There's a difference.

Every morning I woke up carrying anxiety before my feet ever touched the floor. It was already there waiting for me — the pressure, the dread, the heaviness, the constant feeling that something was wrong. I just became accustomed to it. That's one of the frightening things about slow decline. You adapt to it. You normalize it. You convince yourself that everyone must feel this way. And then you stop questioning it altogether.

Today I understand something I couldn't see back then. The hangovers never actually disappeared; I only stopped recognizing them for what they were. Feeling terrible became normal. Feeling anxious became normal. Feeling depressed became normal, and living exhausted became normal too. That's what happens when the decline is gradual. The extraordinary slowly becomes ordinary.

Through all that time, I believed alcohol was helping me manage stress. The truth was very different. It wasn't helping me manage anything; it was helping me postpone everything. There's a huge difference between the two. Every drink pushed tomorrow's problems into tomorrow. Unfortunately, tomorrow always came. And when it did, the problems were still there waiting, usually heavier than they had been before.

I often think about physical pain now. If you break your arm, the pain serves a purpose. Pain tells you something needs attention. Now imagine taking a medication that completely removes the pain while never treating the broken bone underneath. You might feel better for a while, but the injury would still be there. That's exactly what I was doing emotionally. Alcohol wasn't healing the wound; it was numbing the symptoms, and the wound remained. My childhood wounds remained. The shame remained. The anxiety remained. The depression remained. The embarrassment over the restaurant remained, and the lawsuits remained. Nothing had actually changed. I had just delayed dealing with all of it one more day. Then another. Then another.

One of the hardest truths I've ever had to accept is this: you can't heal pain by numbing it. You can't solve anxiety by avoiding it. And you can't rebuild your life while you're constantly escaping from it.

Eventually, something else sank in, and it was harder to face than anything before it. I wasn't just hurting myself. I was hurting the people I loved most.

One of the biggest lies I told myself during that season was that everything I was doing was for my family. The long hours were for them. The stress was for them. The pressure, the drinking, the chasing, the striving — I convinced myself that every bit of it was for them. In hindsight, it wasn't. My wife didn't need a better provider nearly as much as she needed her husband. My daughters didn't need another paycheck; they needed their dad. And although I was physically sitting right there in the room, mentally I was somewhere else entirely.

Even when I was home, I wasn't fully present. I was carrying tomorrow's problems into today's time with the people I loved. I was always thinking, always worrying, always trying to solve another crisis somewhere else. I was sitting right beside my family while living almost entirely inside my own head. My body occupied the room. My mind rarely did.

The painful part is that I didn't recognize any of it. The people who loved me did — especially my wife. She watched the distance grow long before I ever noticed it. She saw me changing. She saw pieces of me disappearing, and she watched the version of me she had married slowly becoming someone else. And honestly, I didn't like that man either. I just wasn't ready to admit it yet.

The fire that had once driven me seemed to be disappearing. Life became routine. Work, stress, drink, sleep, repeat. The scary part wasn't that it happened at all. The scary part was how gradually it happened.

One ordinary day at a time.

It happened exactly the same way childhood lies had quietly become childhood beliefs. Everything compounds. Good habits compound. Bad habits compound. Truth compounds, and so do lies. Every ordinary decision I made was becoming another vote for the man I was becoming. Unfortunately, I didn't like where those votes were leading me.

* * *

The first person who noticed what was happening wasn't me. It was my wife. She didn't need a dramatic speech, and she didn't need a list of everything that was wrong. She was living right beside it every single day. She watched the distance grow — and it wasn't that I had stopped loving my family. It was that I had slowly stopped bringing the best version of myself home to them.

Even now, I can only imagine how difficult those years must have been for her. She saw me carrying burdens I refused to talk about. She watched me smile in public while quietly disappearing in private. She prayed for me long before I was ever willing to pray for myself, and she believed there was still something more inside of me long before I could see it myself.

One day she said something that I've never forgotten. "You're going to have to decide." That was it. There was no lecture, no argument, no long explanation. Just one sentence. She didn't have to explain what she meant, because I already knew. I was standing at a fork in the road. One path continued exactly where I was already headed. The other path required becoming someone completely different. Deep down, I already knew which path I wanted to take. I just wasn't sure I was capable of walking it.

Around that same time, another battle was quietly taking place. Every morning I walked into the bathroom and caught a glimpse of myself in the mirror. At first, I tried not to look at all. Eventually, I couldn't avoid it anymore.

Most people assume the mirror became significant because I had gained weight, and the weight certainly bothered me. A few years earlier I had completed an Ironman triathlon. I knew what discipline looked like, and I knew what my body was capable of doing. Now I barely recognized the person staring back at me. But the extra weight wasn't what hurt the most. What hurt the most was what I saw behind my own eyes.

The fire was gone.

That's still the best way I know how to describe it. The drive was gone. The excitement was gone. The belief, the confidence, the energy — everything that once made me feel alive seemed buried beneath years of excuses, disappointment, and compromise. I remember standing there one morning, looking at my reflection, and feeling something I had never admitted to myself before. I didn't like the man staring back at me. It had nothing to do with how he looked. It had everything to do with who he had become. That truth landed harder than any lawsuit, harder than any business failure, and harder than any criticism I'd ever received.

For so long I had blamed circumstances. I blamed the economy. I blamed the restaurant. I blamed the lawsuits, my childhood, other people. I always had someone or something to point toward. Standing in front of that mirror, there was nowhere left to point. The mirror doesn't care about excuses. It doesn't negotiate with you, and it doesn't soften the truth. It simply reflects what's there.

What I saw wasn't just a heavier man. I saw a husband who wasn't fully present. I saw a father who wasn't bringing his best home. I saw a businessman hiding behind stress, and I saw a man trying to numb his pain instead of healing it. For the first time in a very long time, I stopped seeing a victim. I saw responsibility. That was painful. It was also the beginning of freedom, because as long as someone else owned the problem, someone else owned the solution.

The day I accepted that I was responsible for the direction of my own life, something incredibly hopeful arrived along with the pain. If I had become this man, then I could just as surely become someone else. I didn't need another excuse. I didn't need another reason. I needed another identity. I just didn't know what that identity looked like yet.

What I did know was this: I couldn't keep living the way I was living. Something had to change. I just had no idea where to begin looking for it.

I didn't know it then, but life was about to answer that question in the most unexpected place imaginable. It wouldn't happen in a courtroom. It wouldn't happen at the restaurant, and it wouldn't happen in front of that bathroom mirror. It would happen late one night, in a dark nursery, with a sleeping little girl resting in my arms.

### The Rocking Chair

People often assume the hardest day of your life is the day everything falls apart. For me, it wasn't. By then the restaurant had already closed. The lawsuits were already underway. The debt had already piled up. The anxiety had already become part of my daily life, and the drinking had already become my escape. None of those things were the hardest part. The hardest part came after the noise settled. It came after the phone stopped ringing, after there was nothing left to fix. That's when I was finally left alone with myself.

I think that's one of the reasons so many of us stay busy. Busyness becomes another distraction. As long as we're answering emails, solving problems, chasing the next opportunity, or putting out another fire, we rarely have to sit quietly with the questions we've spent years avoiding. I certainly never did.

For a long time I blamed the restaurant for where I was. Then I blamed the lawsuits. Then I blamed the economy. Then I blamed my circumstances. Eventually something uncomfortable became clear to me. None of those things had created what I was feeling. They had only exposed it. The restaurant didn't create my insecurity. The lawsuits didn't create my anxiety. Alcohol didn't create the emptiness. Those things simply uncovered wounds that had been living beneath the surface most of my life. The pit didn't create the pain. It revealed it.

I believe that's one of the reasons God sometimes allows us to reach the bottom. It isn't to punish us. It's to remove every distraction that keeps us from facing the truth. That's exactly where I found myself. There was no more pretending, no more blaming, and no more hiding. It was just me and the man I had become.

It wasn't a man I respected. That's still difficult to admit, even now. It isn't that it hurts my pride today; it's that it reminds me how lost I had become. The version of me living through that season wasn't the husband I wanted to be. He wasn't the father I wanted to be. He wasn't the leader I wanted to be. And most painfully of all, he wasn't the man I believed God had created me to become.

Every night I would carry my youngest daughter, Isla, into her room after her bottle. I'd sit down in an old rocking chair, hold her against my chest, and gently rock her until she fell asleep. That became our nightly routine together. The room was almost completely dark. A small nightlight cast just enough light across the nursery for me to see her face, and soft music played quietly through the baby monitor. Everything about that room felt peaceful. Everything except me.

Some nights my eyes filled with tears. Other nights I cried without even understanding why I was crying. At first I blamed stress. Then I blamed the restaurant. Then I blamed the lawsuits. Then I blamed exhaustion. Eventually I ran out of things to blame, and the truth was much deeper than any of those explanations. I was broken. Not physically — emotionally, mentally, and spiritually.

People often misunderstand what depression looks like. They imagine someone who never smiles, someone who never leaves the house, someone who can't function at all. That wasn't me. I smiled. I worked. I laughed when I was supposed to laugh, and I showed up wherever I was expected to show up. From the outside, I looked perfectly fine. On the inside, I was drowning.

I've thought about that a lot over the years. Nobody wears a hat that says, "I'm hurting." Nobody wears one that says, "I'm depressed," or one that says, "I'm barely hanging on." Most people just smile, and then they quietly carry burdens that almost nobody else ever sees. I understand that now, because I became one of those people myself.

Night after night I sat in that rocking chair holding my little girl while quietly believing the exact same lies that had been following me since childhood. "You're a failure." "You're not enough." "Everyone would be better off without you." When you've listened to lies for long enough, they stop sounding like lies. They begin sounding like your own thoughts instead. And that frightened me more than anything else did.

I wasn't making plans to end my life. I wasn't thinking about dramatic exits. I just didn't want to wake up. Even writing those words today is difficult — not because I'm ashamed of them, but because I know someone reading this book may understand exactly what I mean by them. I wanted the pressure to stop. I wanted the anxiety to stop. I wanted the constant feeling of failure to stop, and I wanted the noise inside my head to finally become quiet. Somewhere along the way, I convinced myself that my family deserved someone better than me. I know now how distorted that thinking had become. The answer was never my absence. The answer was always my transformation. I just couldn't see it yet from where I was sitting.

* * *

I don't know how long I sat there that night. Maybe fifteen minutes. Maybe an hour. Time has a strange way of disappearing when you're carrying that much weight. I just kept rocking. Forward, then back. Forward, then back. The room was completely silent except for the soft music playing in the background and the quiet rhythm of the chair moving beneath me.

Then something happened. It didn't happen outside of me — it happened somewhere inside of me. For the first time that night, I stopped staring into the darkness. I looked down at her. Really looked. There she was, completely asleep. Her tiny hand rested against my chest, and her breathing was slow and steady. She had absolutely no idea that her father was sitting there fighting the biggest battle of his life.

She trusted me completely.

That thought hit me harder than every lawsuit, harder than every business failure, and harder than every lie I had been believing about myself. This little girl believed I could protect her. She believed I would always be there for her. She believed her dad could fix anything in the world.

Sitting there, something surfaced that brought me to tears. I didn't want to put her down. It wasn't that she hadn't fallen asleep yet — it was that putting her down meant returning to reality. As long as she was in my arms, I could pretend the world outside that nursery didn't exist at all. So I rocked her a little longer. Then I rocked her a little longer still. I wanted to freeze that hour and keep it forever. Just one more minute. One more prayer. One more chance to hold her.

Even now, all these years later, I can still see her face. I can still hear that music, and I can still feel that rocking chair moving beneath me. Some memories never leave you. I'm grateful this is one of them.

People sometimes ask me why I still call Isla my angel. This is why. It isn't that she saved me — God did that. But I believe God chose to use a sleeping little girl to wake up her father. She became the first crack in the darkness. She became the first glimpse of hope, and the first point where the truth finally became louder than the lies.

As I sat there holding her, one thought slowly replaced another. For months — maybe even years — I had been thinking about what life had done to me. That night, I started thinking about what I was doing to the people I loved most. That single shift in perspective changed everything.

The restaurant was still gone. The lawsuits still existed. The debt hadn't disappeared. The anxiety was still there, and so was the depression. Nothing about my circumstances had changed that night. But something inside me had. For the first time in years, I could see that my daughters didn't need a perfect father. They needed their father. My wife didn't need another provider. She needed her husband. The answer wasn't my absence. The answer was my transformation. It had been all along.

That understanding broke me — in the best possible way. I cried harder that night than I ever had before. It wasn't that I had finally given up. It was that I had finally stopped pretending. For so long I had worn a mask so convincing that even I believed it. That night, it finally came off. Not all at once, but just enough for the truth to get in.

That's the thing about truth. It doesn't have to knock the whole wall down at once. It only needs one small crack. Light has a way of finding even the smallest opening, and hope works exactly the same way.

That night didn't solve my problems. It didn't eliminate the lawsuits. It didn't erase the debt. It didn't magically heal my depression, and it didn't remove my anxiety. But it did something much more important than any of that would have been. It gave me a reason to fight. Not for success. Not for money. Not for another business. For my family. And maybe, for the first time in a very long time, for myself.

From where I stand today, I can honestly say that God answered every single prayer I prayed in that rocking chair — just not in the way I expected Him to answer them. I asked Him to take away my pain; instead, He showed me my purpose. I asked Him to remove me from the struggle; instead, He walked with me through it. I asked Him for an ending, and He gave me a brand-new beginning instead. At the time, I didn't understand any of it. Today, I thank Him for it.

There isn't a single prayer that goes unanswered. Sometimes the answer is yes. Sometimes the answer is wait. Sometimes the answer is no. I often wonder about it now. What if God wasn't failing to meet my expectations? What if He was patiently preparing to exceed them instead? I didn't know it then, but while I was asking God to end my pain, He was quietly preparing to transform my entire life.

The next step wouldn't begin in the rocking chair, though. It would begin a short time later. It would begin late one night, alone in a hotel room, with a phone in my hand and one decision that would change the trajectory of my life forever.

### The Hotel Room

The rocking chair changed my heart. It didn't immediately change my habits. I wish I could tell you that I walked out of that nursery and never struggled again, but that isn't my story. The anxiety didn't disappear the next morning. The depression didn't magically lift. The temptation to drink didn't vanish, and life didn't suddenly become any easier. If anything, my circumstances looked exactly the same as they had the day before. The difference was that, for the first time in years, I no longer wanted to stay the same. That desire became the beginning of everything that followed.

I can see now that I had spent year after year trying to change my life without changing myself. That never works. You can't build a different future while remaining the same person who created the present. Something deep inside me finally understood that truth. I didn't need another opportunity. What I needed was another identity.

Not long after that season, I found myself in yet another hotel room while traveling for work. Like most people in medical sales, I spent a lot of nights on the road. Hotels became routine for me. Different city. Different room. Same schedule every time. One evening, after another long day, I was lying in bed doing what so many of us do. I was scrolling, mindlessly moving from one post to the next. Then something caught my attention. It was an advertisement for a men's coaching group.

To this day, I don't remember exactly what the advertisement said. I don't remember the headline, and I don't remember the sales pitch. What I remember is the feeling it gave me. It was as though that message had found me at the exact instant I was finally ready to hear it.

Over the years, plenty of people had offered me advice. Some of it was good. Some of it wasn't. None of it had ever really landed — and it wasn't that the advice itself was wrong. I just wasn't ready to hear it yet. This time was different. For the first time in a long time, I felt something I hadn't felt in years.

I felt possibility.

I didn't know where it would lead. I didn't know if it would work, and I didn't know whether it would actually change anything at all. I only knew one thing for certain. Doing nothing was no longer an option. So I clicked. Immediately. There was no debate, no overthinking, no waiting until Monday, no telling myself I'd come back to it later. I just clicked.

That may have been one of the most important clicks of my life. It didn't solve a single one of my problems, but it represented something I hadn't done in years. I bet on myself.

One of the first challenges my coach gave me was straightforward: quit drinking. At first, it almost sounded too easy to matter. How could one single decision possibly change everything? In hindsight, I can see that it didn't change everything. It changed me.

All that time, I had convinced myself alcohol helped me relax. I told myself it helped me sleep. I told myself it helped me deal with stress. The truth was much different. Alcohol wasn't helping me deal with anything. It was helping me avoid everything. Avoidance disguises itself as relief. For a little while the pressure feels lighter. For a little while the anxiety gets quieter and the noise fades away. Then morning comes, and everything you postponed is still sitting there waiting for you. Only now, it's heavier than it was.

One of the greatest discoveries of my life came during that season. Alcohol wasn't the root; it was the symptom. If I quit drinking without changing the man who depended on it, something else would eventually come along and take its place. I couldn't merely become a man who didn't drink anymore. I had to become a man who no longer needed alcohol to escape his own life. That's a completely different kind of transformation altogether.

The first few weeks weren't glamorous, and nothing magical happened. The lawsuits didn't disappear. The debt didn't disappear, and the pressure certainly didn't disappear. What disappeared was the fog. For the first time in years, I could actually think clearly. For the first time in years, I wasn't waking up carrying the consequences of whatever I had done the night before. For the first time in years, I was finally facing reality exactly as it was. Reality can be uncomfortable to face, but reality is also honest. And honesty — honesty is where the rebuilding truly begins.

Little by little, I stopped asking, "How do I escape this?" and started asking a completely different question: "What's the next right step?" Not the next ten steps. Not next year. Just the next single step. I didn't know it then, but that single shift in thinking would eventually change every area of my life. Because becoming someone new doesn't happen all at once. It happens one next step at a time.

* * *

The clarity that came from quitting drinking gave me something I hadn't experienced in years. It gave me the ability to think honestly. For the first time in a long while, I wasn't trying to solve today's problems while standing in yesterday's fog. The restaurant was still gone. The lawsuits were still there. The debt was still very real. Nothing about my circumstances had improved overnight. The difference was that I was finally seeing all of them for what they actually were.

They were problems. They were not identities.

I had been confusing the two for as long as I could remember. The restaurant failed, and therefore I was a failure. I struggled with alcohol, and therefore I was weak. I was battling anxiety, and therefore something must be wrong with me. Those statements sound ridiculous to me now. At the time, though, I believed every single one of them. That's what lies do. They quietly become your identity.

The coaching group challenged much more than just my drinking. It challenged the stories I had been telling myself. It challenged the excuses, and it challenged the blame. Most importantly of all, it challenged my sense of ownership.

One conversation in particular still stands out to me. My coach asked me a simple question. "Randall... what part of this is your responsibility?" I remember immediately thinking about everything that wasn't my responsibility. The economy wasn't. The restaurant wasn't. Neither were the lawsuits, my childhood, the pressure, or the stress. I had a whole list ready to go. Then I stopped, because for the first time, it dawned on me that I was answering a question he hadn't even asked. He didn't ask me what had happened to me. He asked me what part of it was my responsibility.

That question stayed with me. I couldn't stop thinking about it. Little by little, something began changing inside me. Instead of asking, "Why did this happen?" I started asking, "What am I going to do now?" That's a completely different question. One question searches for blame. The other searches for responsibility. Responsibility is heavy. It's also incredibly freeing at the same time. Because if I'm responsible for my response, then I still have choices. I still have agency, and I still have hope.

One of the greatest lies I had believed through all those years was that someone else was eventually coming to rescue me. Maybe another opportunity would come along. Maybe another business would save me. Maybe more money would fix it. Maybe someone would finally understand what I was carrying. The truth is, nobody was coming. And strangely enough, accepting that fact wasn't depressing. It was liberating. Because if nobody was coming to save me, then maybe God had already given me everything I needed to begin saving myself. Not by myself, but through Him — and through choosing differently. It would happen one decision at a time. One promise at a time.

One ordinary day at a time.

That's when I finally understood something my father, my grandfather, and my great-grandfather had been quietly teaching me my entire life. The work still has to be done. The cattle don't care how you feel. The fences still need repairing. The windmills still break, and the crops still need attention. Life doesn't stop just because we're feeling discouraged. The work always remains. Somewhere along the way, I had drifted away from that example. Now I was slowly finding my way back to it.

The remarkable thing about that season is that nothing looked particularly dramatic from the outside. There wasn't one giant breakthrough. There was no overnight transformation, and there was no movie scene where everything suddenly became clear. It was much quieter than that. It was one honest conversation. It was one difficult decision. It was one promise kept. Then another, and another after that. The changes were almost invisible — until one day I looked back and saw that I wasn't the same man who had walked into that hotel room. I still had a long way to go. But for the first time in years, I was finally moving in the right direction, and I could feel it.

I didn't know it then, but one conversation was about to permanently change the way I looked at accountability, discipline, and excuses. It wasn't gentle, and it wasn't polished. In fact, it made me angry. Years later, I'm incredibly grateful that it did.

* * *

One day, during that first coaching program, I received a message that hit me harder than almost anything I had heard up to that point in my life. It wasn't encouraging. It wasn't gentle, and it wasn't designed to make me feel better. It was designed to wake me up. The message read:

"You don't need accountability, you need f'ing discipline! You're the one putting food and booze in your mouth, not anyone else! Don't like it? Not what you want to hear? Stop f'ing doing it!! Take a little pride in strengthening your weaknesses and start becoming an all-around asset! If you want to quit drinking, you literally will not take another drink. If not, you'll keep lying to yourself and proving what takes precedence!"

I hated reading it. Every part of me wanted to defend myself. I wanted to explain the restaurant. I wanted to explain the lawsuits, the stress, the childhood trauma, the anxiety, and the depression. I had an answer ready for everything. Then something occurred to me. He wasn't asking for my explanation. He was confronting my excuses, and those are two very different things.

That message stayed with me because every single sentence in it forced me to look in the mirror instead of looking at everyone else. It stripped away every place I had been hiding. Was the restaurant difficult? Absolutely it was. Were the lawsuits real? Without question they were. Did childhood trauma affect me? More than I understood at the time. But none of those things were physically picking up a bottle.

I was.

That truth hurt. It also became one of the most freeing turning points of my life. I certainly didn't enjoy hearing it, but it left me with nowhere else to hide, and that turned out to be exactly what I needed. For so long I had been looking for accountability. As it turned out, accountability wasn't what I was missing at all. Discipline was. Accountability can remind you. Accountability can encourage you. Accountability can ask hard questions. But accountability can't make your decisions for you. At some point, every one of us has to decide who we're going to become when no one else is watching. That's what discipline is.

For the first time, I began understanding the difference between knowing what to do and actually doing it. I had spent so much of my life collecting information. I knew alcohol wasn't helping me. I knew I needed to lose the weight. I knew I needed to become more present with my family, and I knew I needed to stop making excuses. Knowledge had never been my problem. Execution was the problem.

Seeing that changed everything for me. I stopped searching for another motivational speech. I stopped waiting until I felt inspired, and I stopped telling myself that tomorrow would somehow be different. Tomorrow had become my favorite excuse. Eventually it became clear to me that tomorrow never changes anything. Only today does. One decision, made today — that's where change actually begins.

The coach's words were hard to hear. Today, I'm grateful for every one of them, because sometimes the most loving thing someone can do is refuse to participate in your excuses. They cared more about the man I could become than about the comfort of the man I was. I needed that. They weren't trying to tear me down. They were refusing to let me settle, and there's a world of difference between the two.

Honestly, I don't think that message changed my life all by itself. What it did was confirm something I had already begun discovering in the rocking chair and the hotel room. Nobody was coming to save me. God had been inviting me to become someone different all along. The decision had always been mine to make.

That became the turning point for me. I stopped asking, "How do I fix my life?" and I started asking the one question that would eventually end up written on a Post-it note stuck to my bathroom mirror.

Who do I have to become?

I had no idea how much that one question was about to change the direction of my life. The answer wouldn't come all at once. It would begin with one unremarkable decision — the simple decision to lace up a pair of running shoes.
