# Chapter 6

## Reactive vs. Proactive

One of the most expensive habits I've ever had wasn't financial. It was reactive living. Through most of my adult life, that's exactly how I operated. I reacted to problems. I reacted to stress. I reacted to conflict. I reacted to financial pressure. I reacted to disappointment. I reacted to emergencies. And every single time I reacted, I paid interest.

At the time, I didn't know I was doing it. I thought I was adapting. I thought I was handling whatever life threw at me. Today, I see it very differently. I was constantly paying interest on problems that could have been addressed months—or even years—earlier. That sentence has stayed with me because it explains so much of my life.

Ignore your health long enough, and eventually you pay interest. Ignore your marriage long enough, and eventually you pay interest. Ignore your finances long enough, and eventually you pay interest. Ignore your mindset long enough, and eventually you pay interest. Life always collects. The bill always comes due. The only question is whether you'll pay now or pay much more later. For a long stretch of my life, I chose later.

Later always cost more.

When I look back over my own story, I can see it clearly. The restaurant didn't fail overnight. My drinking didn't become destructive overnight. My health didn't decline overnight. My marriage didn't become strained overnight. Everything compounded. One decision. One excuse. One ordinary day at a time.

The encouraging part is that the opposite is also true. Truth compounds. Discipline compounds. Kindness compounds. Integrity compounds. One small promise kept today makes tomorrow's promise a little easier to keep. That's exactly what I had experienced through running. Nothing dramatic happened on Day 14, or Day 37, or Day 83, but every day quietly built on the one before it. Eventually, the ordinary became extraordinary. That's how life works, whether we're moving toward the life we want or away from it.

The problem isn't reacting once in a while; life demands that from all of us. The problem is when reacting becomes your lifestyle. That's where I lived far too long—always trying to catch up, always solving yesterday's problems, always putting out another fire. I wasn't preparing. I was surviving. And preparation never feels urgent until you need it.

That's one of the greatest lessons my father taught me without ever intending to. Farmers don't wait until harvest to start planting. Ranchers don't wait until the cattle are thirsty before looking for water. Fences are repaired before the cattle get out. Equipment is maintained before it breaks. Preparation is simply part of the lifestyle. Growing up, I watched that every single day. I just didn't understand how much it applied to life outside the ranch.

Somewhere along the way, I stopped living proactively and started reacting instead, and the consequences quietly compounded year after year. Then one morning, during a run, another thought crossed my mind. Don't wait for life to settle. Move within it. That sentence challenged me, because I had spent so much of my life telling myself, "Once things slow down..." "Once work gets easier..." "Once this lawsuit is over..." "Once life settles..." Then I'll start. But life never settled. There was always another responsibility, another problem, another excuse. Waiting had quietly become another form of procrastination.

Then it finally hit me: if I waited for perfect conditions, I'd spend my entire life waiting. That's when I stopped asking life to become easier and started asking myself to become stronger. That one shift changed everything. My circumstances didn't immediately improve—my response did. And our response is one of the few things we'll always have the power to control.

One of the greatest shifts in my thinking came when I stopped asking, "How do I avoid problems?" and instead started asking, "Who do I need to become before the next problem arrives?" Those two questions produce completely different lives. One waits for circumstances to improve. The other prepares regardless of circumstances.

Preparation became one of the greatest gifts running ever gave me. People often assume I'm training for races. I'm really not. I'm preparing for life. If someone called me tomorrow and asked me to run a half marathon, I'd say yes—not because I specifically trained for a half marathon, but because the foundation is already there. The same would be true for a marathon. Preparation gave me options.

Life works exactly the same way. Most people wait until the diagnosis before taking their health seriously. They wait until the marriage is struggling before learning how to communicate, until the bank account is empty before creating a budget, until life hurts enough to finally pay attention. I know, because I did exactly that.

Playing catch-up is exhausting. Preparation is peaceful.

That sentence has become one of my favorite reminders. Preparation doesn't eliminate storms; it changes how you walk through them. Storms are still coming. Disappointment is still coming. Loss is still coming. Life doesn't stop being life because you've become proactive. The difference is that you're no longer constantly surprised by difficulty. You're prepared to respond instead of merely react. That distinction changed everything for me.

One of my favorite thoughts came to me during a run: there is a miracle in the present moment. Not yesterday. Not tomorrow. Right now. That thought stayed with me because I saw how much of my life I had spent somewhere other than the present. I replayed yesterday's failures. I worried about tomorrow's problems. Meanwhile, today quietly disappeared. I missed conversations. I missed moments. I missed opportunities to just be where my feet already were.

The more intentional I became, the more I noticed something remarkable. Peace isn't waiting somewhere in the future. Peace is built by faithfully living today. That's why the Post-it note on my mirror said only two words: Right Now. Not someday. Not eventually. Right now. That little reminder quietly eliminated dozens of excuses. If a difficult conversation needed to happen—right now. If I needed to apologize—right now. If I needed to make a decision—right now. Procrastination had already stolen enough years from my life. I wasn't giving it another day.

From where I stand today, I don't see the restaurant as the end. I don't see the lawsuits as punishment. I don't see the rocking chair as my lowest point. I see all of them differently now.

Preparation.

Preparation for the husband I needed to become. Preparation for the father my daughters deserved. Preparation for the conversations I'm now able to have with people who feel hopeless. Preparation for this book. At the time, none of those seasons felt like preparation. They felt like interruptions. Today I believe something very different. Sometimes what looks like an interruption becomes the road you were meant to travel all along.

That perspective changed the way I view setbacks. Instead of asking, "Why is this happening to me?" I found myself asking, "What is this trying to teach me?" That question continues changing my life—not because I always like the answer, but because growth usually begins the instant we're willing to ask it honestly.

The more proactive I became, the clearer it became that I didn't need another complicated plan. I needed a roadmap. Not someone else's roadmap. Mine. A roadmap built around becoming instead of reacting. A roadmap I could return to every single day. One ordinary decision at a time.

The roadmap didn't appear all at once. I didn't sit down one afternoon with a whiteboard and suddenly organize my life. It happened much more naturally than that. The more I paid attention, the more I noticed patterns. Every challenge I faced seemed to expose the same areas over and over again. When my health suffered, everything else became more difficult. When my thinking became unhealthy, my decisions followed. When my relationships weakened, success felt empty. When I failed to steward my time, money, opportunities, and influence well, everything else became unstable. Life wasn't divided into separate compartments. Everything was connected.

Seeing that simplified my life in a way I never expected. I had spent so long trying to solve individual problems. Now I understood that what I actually needed was to strengthen the foundation.

The more I thought about it, the more another picture came to mind: a house. People spend a tremendous amount of money choosing countertops, paint colors, flooring, furniture, and landscaping. Very few people get excited about pouring the concrete foundation. Nobody drives past a new home and says, "Would you look at that foundation?" Nobody notices it—until something starts cracking. Then it's all anyone talks about.

Life works the same way. We admire the visible things. The promotions. The vacations. The accomplishments. The medals. The finish lines. Very few people pay attention to the foundation quietly holding everything together. That's exactly where I had spent so long neglecting the work. The restaurant wasn't really my problem. The lawsuits weren't really my problem. Alcohol wasn't really my problem. Those things exposed weaknesses in a foundation that had needed attention long before any of them showed up.

Once I understood that, I stopped looking for another shortcut and started returning to the basics. Not glamorous. Not exciting. The basics. The same basics my father had been living every day of his life. The same basics my grandfather quietly modeled. Show up. Do the work. Take care of what's been entrusted to you. Repeat tomorrow.

There's something incredibly freeing about simplicity. I had long believed I needed a more complicated answer. I didn't. I needed a more consistent one. That truth changed how I looked at almost everything. Instead of trying to fix my entire life all at once, I asked one question: "What's the next faithful step?" Not the next ten. Not the next year. The next one. Then I took it. Then another.

The remarkable thing about small decisions is that they rarely feel significant while you're making them. Nobody celebrates another healthy meal. Nobody applauds another early morning. Nobody notices another difficult conversation you chose not to avoid. Those choices rarely feel extraordinary—until enough of them accumulate. Then one day people look at your life and say, "You're so disciplined." Or, "You're lucky." Or, "I wish I had your consistency." What they don't see are the thousands of everyday decisions that quietly built what they're now admiring.

That's why I no longer chase extraordinary days. I chase ordinary faithfulness. Because everyday faithfulness compounds, just like excuses once did. One day at a time. One promise at a time. One decision at a time.

Eventually, something else sank in. Preparation isn't really about avoiding pain. It's about becoming the kind of person who can carry it differently. That became one of the greatest shifts in my thinking. I stopped asking God to remove every difficult season. Instead, I found myself praying, "God, help me become the man this season requires." Those are very different prayers. One asks for escape. The other asks for growth. My life changed when I stopped praying for easier circumstances and started praying for stronger character.

Without realizing it, I had begun laying the foundation for everything that would follow. Not another race. Not another business. A way of living—one that would eventually organize every area of my life around four simple pillars. They weren't revolutionary. They were foundational. And foundations change everything.

One of the things I wish someone had taught me when I was twenty years old is that you don't need a perfect plan before you begin. You only need a direction. For the longest time, I kept looking for someone to hand me the blueprint. A coach. A mentor. A seminar. Another book. Another system. Another shortcut. I kept believing someone else had the missing piece.

The older I get, the more I understand that coaches, mentors, and books are incredibly valuable. I've learned life-changing lessons from every one of them. But eventually there comes a point when you have to stop collecting information and start building your own roadmap. No one else can do that part for you. People can encourage you, challenge you, correct you, even save you years of mistakes. But they can't become you. They can't make your decisions. They can't keep your promises. That's your responsibility.

Oddly enough, I found that incredibly freeing. It meant my future wasn't limited by someone else's imagination. It would be shaped by my willingness to become the man I believed God was calling me to become.

One question became incredibly helpful during that season: What do I want? Simple, but not easy. The second question became even more important: Why do I want it? Because if the "why" isn't strong enough, the "what" won't survive very long. I've watched people set incredible goals that disappeared within weeks. The goals weren't unrealistic. The reason behind them just wasn't strong enough to survive discomfort.

Eventually I added one more question: Who can help me get there? That question changed my perspective. For most of my life I had believed asking for help somehow made me weaker. I couldn't have been more wrong. It takes far more courage to admit you need guidance than it does to pretend you've got everything figured out. Pride isolates. Humility invites growth.

Every meaningful breakthrough in my life has another person's fingerprints on it. My father. My grandparents. My wife. My coaches. My mentors. Friends who loved me enough to tell me the truth. Every one of them helped shape the man I'm still becoming. Understanding that removed another excuse. I no longer had to pretend I could do everything alone. I only needed to remain teachable.

One of the greatest lies I ever believed was that strength meant independence. Today I believe something completely different. Strength means remaining humble enough to keep learning. The day I believe I've arrived, I'll probably stop growing. I hope that day never comes. The more I grow, the more I discover how much I still have to learn. That's actually become one of my favorite parts of life. Every season asks something new of me. Every season introduces another opportunity to become.

The answer isn't perfection. The answer is progress. One small step. Then another. Then another. Direction has become far more important to me than speed. I don't need to know every step five years from now. I need to know only the next faithful one. That's enough. God has never shown me my entire future. He has faithfully shown me enough for today, then enough for tomorrow, then enough for the next step.

When I trace my life backward, almost every meaningful decision happened before I felt completely ready. Opening a restaurant. Joining a coaching group. Quitting drinking. Beginning a daily running streak. Writing this book. None of those decisions came with certainty. They came with faith. Sometimes faith looks remarkably plain. Sometimes it looks like nothing more than taking the next step before you can see the entire staircase.

The more I lived that way, the less overwhelmed I became. Life became simpler. Not easier. Simpler. Tell yourself the truth. Keep today's promise. Take the next step. Repeat tomorrow.

That's how I stopped reacting to life. That's how I began preparing for it. And without realizing it, I was quietly laying the foundation for every chapter that follows.
