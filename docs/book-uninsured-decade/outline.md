# The Uninsured Decade — Book Outline (v1 draft)

Drafted 2026-09-07. Short book, ~80 pages / ~20,000 words. Money-door title for the executive male earner, 50–70, who makes the household financial decisions.

Standing constraints applied: no product or affiliate brand names (point to my4mlife.com); "regenerative therapies," never a locked treatment brand; never expose the proprietary Rx formula; Dr. TJ = architect and Chairman, not the treating clinician (no "my patient," no "I prescribed"); lead credential "Dr. TJ Mundheim, DC, NBCE-certified since 1994" in the disclaimer only; required medical disclaimer on the copyright page. Every cost figure carries an in-text source and must be re-verified before print.

---

## 1. Title candidates

| Title | Subtitle | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| **The Uninsured Decade** | *The one catastrophic risk you cannot buy a policy against — and the premium you can still pay* | Names the gap in his coverage in three words. Insurance vocabulary he already trusts; "decade" makes it finite and priceable. |
| The Last Ten Years | *What a diagnosis at 70 actually costs — and what it costs to lower the odds* | More emotional, less financial. Strong for the spouse handing it over; weaker as a boardroom-table object. |
| Spend-Down | *Medicare pays $0. Here's what happens next.* | "Spend-down" is a real Medicaid term; the insider word signals the book is written for someone who reads statements. Cold, and easily mistaken for an eldercare-law book. |
| The $500,000 Gap | *The risk your portfolio isn't hedged against* | Highest-contrast money hook, best for a cold table at a dinner talk. The number ages badly and reads like a webinar. |
| The Policy You Write Yourself | *Ten hours a month against the only bill nobody underwrites* | Leads with the solution and the premium metaphor. Softer front end — better as a Part III section head than a cover. |

**Recommendation: *The Uninsured Decade*, subtitle *The one catastrophic risk you cannot buy a policy against — and the premium you can still pay.*** It carries the whole thesis, survives the talk title, the calculator page, and the chapter that later folds into book v21, and it does not date. Hold "The Policy You Write Yourself" as the Part III title.

## 3. Chapter outline

**~12 chapters, four parts, ~20,000 words.**

### Part I — The Bill (~6,500 words)

**Ch 1 — The Risk You Didn't Underwrite** (1,800 w)
*Promise:* You have hedged everything else. Fire, liability, disability, key-man, umbrella, term life, long-term disability on the business. There is exactly one catastrophic exposure in your life with no policy behind it, and it is the one that decides who you are at eighty.
*Numbers:* the reader's own coverage inventory; the single line **$0**.
*Story:* open the running couple — Bill, 71, and Cindy. Bill's shoulder vs. Bill's memory.
*Beat:* recognition, then the floor moving. Not fear-mongering — an audit finding.
*Figure:* **Coverage Inventory table** — Risk / Policy / Who pays. Every row filled except the last, which reads "none available."
Drafted opening in §5.

**Ch 2 — What Medicare Actually Covers** (1,500 w)
*Promise:* The most expensive assumption in American retirement planning is that Medicare handles this. It does not. Here is the exact line where coverage stops and your checkbook starts.
*Numbers:* **$0** toward ongoing custodial/memory care; the 100-day skilled-nursing limit and what "skilled" means; Medicaid spend-down eligibility.
*Beat:* cold clarity. This chapter is deliberately unemotional — it is a coverage read.
*Figure:* **What's covered / What isn't** two-column table.

**Ch 3 — The Market That Left** (1,400 w)
*Promise:* You would buy the policy if it existed. For a while it did. Then the carriers ran the numbers, and most of them walked.
*Numbers:* carrier exits from the standalone long-term-care market; in-force premium increases that doubled or more; underwriting declines and the age/health window that closes before most men shop. Hybrid life/LTC riders as the remaining product, and what they actually cap out at.
*Beat:* the door closing. He is not lazy; he is uninsurable at scale.
*Figure:* sidebar — **"If you are going to shop it anyway, here are the three questions to ask."** Honest, non-defensive; buys enormous trust.
*Note:* re-verify every carrier/premium figure with a licensed source before print; no recommendation to buy or drop any product.

**Ch 4 — Ten Years, One Household** (1,800 w)
*Promise:* Walk the arc year by year — the diagnosis at 70, the eight to ten years after, and where the money actually goes.
*Numbers:* stage table from the source material — years 1–3 ~$35–40K; years 4–6 ~$170–180K; years 7–10 ~$290–340K; **10-year total ~$495–560K**; Alzheimer's Association lifetime average **$405,262**, **70% borne by the family**; **$8,019/mo** memory care; **$12,388/yr** caregiver out-of-pocket; **$129,575/yr** private nursing room; Parkinson's **$82.2B/yr**, $10,000+ in excess medical costs in the year *before* diagnosis.
*Beat:* the invoice arriving in installments.
*Figure:* **The Ten-Year Ledger** — the stage table, formatted as a statement.

### Part II — The Premium (~4,500 words)

**Ch 5 — The Second Patient** (1,500 w)
*Promise:* The scenario above is the cheap half. The other half is paid by the person who did not get diagnosed.
*Numbers:* **63%** higher four-year mortality in strained spousal caregivers (Schulz & Beach, JAMA); **59%** high/very-high emotional stress; chronic disease **45% vs 24%**; **72%** miss their own care; **19 billion** unpaid hours in 2025.
*Story:* Cindy at 80 — the decade she spent, the half-million gone, the appointments she skipped.
*Beat:* the hardest beat in the book. This is where the stewardship identity turns. He is not protecting himself; he is protecting her.
*Figure:* none. Let this one run clean.

**Ch 6 — Self-Insurance** (1,400 w)
*Promise:* If no carrier will write the policy, there is one underwriter left. You. The only reserve you can post against this risk is physiology.
*Numbers:* the reframe — actuarial thinking applied to a body; modifiable risk as the only lever with leverage.
*Beat:* the pivot from bill to agency. First appearance of the premium motif in full.
*Figure:* **The Premium** callout box (first of the running series).

**Ch 7 — Ten Hours and Ten Thousand Dollars** (1,600 w)
*Promise:* Here is the actual price of the coverage, in hours and in dollars, with nothing hidden.
*Numbers:* ~**10 hours a month** — two strength sessions a week, the daily walk, the weekly planning hour; ~**$10,000/year all-in** — gym and trainer, grass-finished food delta over the current grocery bill, supplements, gut-repair prescription support where indicated, labs and consults. Set against a **~$500,000** exposure. Express it as a premium-to-face-value ratio, the way he already thinks about term life.
*Beat:* relief. It is cheaper than he feared, and it is denominated in something he controls.
*Figure:* **The Premium Schedule** — line-item annual basket vs. the ten-year ledger from Ch 4.

### Part III — The Policy (~6,500 words)

**Ch 8 — What the Premium Actually Buys** (1,300 w)
*Promise:* Expected-value honesty, stated once and plainly, before any protocol detail: this buys odds, not outcomes. No prevention claim, no cure claim, no reversal claim.
*Numbers:* the language of relative vs. absolute risk in plain English; why "lowers the odds" is the only sentence any honest person can sell you.
*Beat:* the trust chapter. The distrustful reader converts here or nowhere.
*Figure:* **"What this book does not claim"** boxed list — four sentences, blunt.

**Ch 9 — The Coverage Schedule: The Four Ms** (1,300 w)
*Promise:* A policy has a schedule of benefits. So does this one. Mind is the destination; Muscle, Mitigate, and Motivate are the three line items that pay for it.
*Numbers:* the 4M shorthand, one page each, in insurance-document register. *Begin with the end in Mind* introduced here — the triple entendre, briefly.
*Beat:* structure as reassurance.
*Figure:* **Schedule of Benefits** table — Line item / What it buys / What it costs you per month.

**Ch 10 — Mitigate: Stop Paying Claims Against Yourself** (1,700 w)
*Promise:* The largest line item, and the one everyone skips. The gut-brain axis in plain language, sleep, alcohol, environment — the chronic insults that compound into neuroinflammation.
*Numbers:* the canonical gut-brain block — ~90% of serotonin and ~50% of dopamine precursor activity downstream of the gut; the vagal highway; barrier failure and endotoxin as a driver of neuroinflammation.
*Beat:* "eliminate the insulting behavior." Recognition again — he already knows most of these are true of him.
*Figure:* **The Insult Ledger** — a debit column he fills in himself.
*Constraint:* categories and mechanisms only. Prescription gut-barrier support described generically as a physician-written, proprietary compounded prescription tailored to the individual — never a formula, never a product name. Pointer to my4mlife.com.

**Ch 11 — Muscle and the Metabolic Road** (1,600 w)
*Promise:* The road to a dementia diagnosis usually runs through diabetes, hypertension, visceral fat, and lost muscle — and every one of those is a road you are already on or already off.
*Numbers:* lean mass as a predictor of cognitive healthspan; sarcopenia; visceral adiposity as a chronic inflammatory load; protein-first, 30–40g breaking the fast after the fasted morning walk; two compound sessions a week.
*Beat:* competence. This is work he knows how to do.
*Figure:* **Four gauges** — waist, grip/strength proxy, fasting glucose or A1c, sleep hours. Baseline column and a 12-week column.

**Ch 12 — Hormones, the Canaries, and Where Medical Support Fits** (1,600 w)
*Promise:* The early warnings the body files before anything shows on a memory test — and the honest boundary between what you do yourself and what requires a licensed prescriber.
*Numbers:* ED as the canary — the first visible report the vascular system files; the perimenopausal/menopausal transition as the parallel signal on the other side of the marriage; "normal" reference ranges vs. optimal.
*Beat:* permission to say the sentence out loud.
*Figure:* **Canary checklist**, twelve yes/no lines.
*Constraint:* prescription categories described generically — hormone support where indicated, weight-loss medication where indicated, regenerative therapies for those already diagnosed — all "prescribed by independent licensed clinicians," none named as products.

## 4. Recurring devices

1. **The ledger / balance sheet.** Every part closes on a ledger figure: the Coverage Inventory (Ch 1), the Ten-Year Ledger (Ch 4), the Premium Schedule (Ch 7), the Schedule of Benefits (Ch 9), the Insult Ledger (Ch 10), the 30-Day Ledger (Ch 14). Same visual treatment throughout so the reader learns the shape.
2. **"The Premium" callouts.** A recurring boxed unit, one or two per chapter from Ch 6 onward, always the same construction: *The premium: [hours or dollars]. What it buys: [odds, stated honestly]. What it does not buy: [the guarantee].* This is where expected-value honesty gets repeated instead of relegated to one disclaimer.
3. **Bill and Cindy, running.** Extend the existing couple rather than inventing new ones — they already exist in the dinner-talk deck, which keeps book and talk in sync. Told in the James register: specific, unhurried, domestic detail (the tennis league, the car he kept meaning to trade, the calendar on the refrigerator). Bill appears in Chs 1, 4, 5, 13; Cindy carries Ch 5 alone. Composite, disclosed as such in the front matter. A second, shorter thread — a man who took the assessment at 58 — appears once in Ch 13 as the counterfactual.
4. **The one-sentence handoff.** The book is built so a spouse can hand it over with a single line. Chapter 1's opening page is written to be readable in ninety seconds standing in a kitchen, and the back cover carries the same sentence: *"Read the first six pages. If the numbers are wrong, throw it away."*

## 5. Drafted sample

### Who This Book Is For

This is a book about money before it is a book about health. That is deliberate.

It is for the person in the household who reads the statements. Who carries the umbrella policy, the key-man coverage, the term life he has never needed and pays for anyway, because that is what a responsible man does — he buys down risk for people who are not thinking about risk. Usually that is a man in his fifties or sixties. It is not always. If it is you, this book is for you.

You will not find inspiration in here. No transformation stories, no before-and-after photographs, no promises that a supplement will save you. What you will find is an exposure your plan does not cover, priced out with sources, and one honest way to reduce the odds — with the odds stated as odds, never as a guarantee.

**A word to the spouse.** You may be the one who bought this. If so, you already know what happens in the ten years after a diagnosis, because you have watched it happen in another family and you have been unable to get the conversation started in your own. Do not open with your worry. Hand it over and say one sentence: *Read the first six pages. If the numbers are wrong, throw it away.*

The numbers are not wrong. Every figure in this book carries its source and its date. Check them.

**— Dr. TJ**

### Chapter 1 — The Risk You Didn't Underwrite

*(first ~500 words)*

Somewhere in your house there is a folder, or a portal login, that holds every policy you own.

Homeowner's, with the wind-and-hail rider you added after the storm two summers ago. Auto, on three vehicles, one of them your daughter's. An umbrella policy over the top of both, because your attorney told you to and he was right. Term life — twenty-year, maybe a second one layered on it, bought when the kids were small and now mostly a habit. Disability, if you were the sort of earner whose absence would matter, which you were. Key-man on the business. Errors and omissions. Workers' comp. Maybe a buy-sell agreement funded by a policy nobody has looked at in nine years.

You did that. Not your wife, not your accountant — you. Over thirty years you built a lattice of coverage under your family so that nothing that could happen to you would land on them as a bill. That is not paranoia. That is the whole job. Most of the men I have known who did it well would not describe themselves as careful people. They would describe themselves as people who did not want anyone else cleaning up after them.

So let me ask you a question about that folder.

Which policy in it pays for memory care?

Take a second with it. Not the hospital stay — you have that. Not the rehab after the hip — Medicare covers a stretch of that, and we will get to exactly how long. I mean the other thing. The thing that starts as a word you cannot find in a meeting and ends, eight or ten years later, with a bed in a locked wing and someone else spooning food into your mouth at a rate of eight thousand dollars a month, every month, until it stops.

Which policy covers that one?

I already know the answer, because I have asked this question in a lot of rooms now. There is a pause. Then somebody says *Medicare*, half as a statement and half as a question, and everyone else at the table looks relieved for about four seconds, until they see that I am not nodding.

Medicare pays nothing toward it. Not a reduced amount. Not a copay structure with a bad deductible. Zero. It is the single largest uncovered financial exposure in American retirement, it sits in almost every household in this country, and the reason nobody has sold you a solution is that there is barely a solution left to sell.

I want to be careful here, because you have been sold things before and you have a good instinct for when it is happening.

I am not going to tell you that you can prevent dementia. Nobody can tell you that. Anyone who does is either careless or selling something, and by the end of Chapter 8 you will be able to hear the difference in one sentence. What I am going to tell you is that this is a risk, that risk is a thing you already know how to price, and that there is exactly one place left to post a reserve against it.

Not a carrier. Not a rider. You.

Let's start with what it costs when nobody does.
