## 1. Gut-brain neurochemistry (Ch. 10, p. "What your gut is manufacturing")

**As written:** "~90% serotonin, ~50% dopamine, ~90% vagal traffic gut→brain."

**Primary sources:**
- Gershon MD, Tack J, *Gastroenterology*, 2007;132(1):397–414. doi:10.1053/j.gastro.2006.11.002 — enterochromaffin cells account for ~95% of body serotonin; "90%" is the standard rounding.
- Yano JM, et al., *Cell*, 2015;161(2):264–276. doi:10.1016/j.cell.2015.02.047 — mechanistic support (microbiota drives EC-cell serotonin synthesis); does not itself restate the percentage.
- Eisenhofer G, et al., *J Clin Endocrinol Metab*, 1997;82(11):3864–3871. doi:10.1210/jcem.82.11.4339 — mesenteric organs (chiefly gut) account for roughly half of body dopamine turnover; the paper says "substantial," ~50% is the figure downstream reviews cite.
- Berthoud HR, Neuhuber WL, *Auton Neurosci*, 2000;85(1–3):1–17. doi:10.1016/S1566-0702(00)00215-0 — establishes the vagus as predominantly sensory; the ratio the anatomical literature supports is ~80% afferent / 20% efferent, not 90%.

**Status: CORRECTED.** Serotonin ~90% and dopamine ~50% are defensible. The vagal figure is not — replace "about 90% of its traffic runs gut-to-brain" with "roughly 80%."

**Cite:** `[Gershon & Tack, Gastroenterology 2007; Eisenhofer et al., JCEM 1997; Berthoud & Neuhuber, Auton. Neurosci. 2000]`

## 2. Exercise myokines and BDNF (Ch. 11, "Two sessions, and why they are the neuroprotective item")

**Primary sources:**
- Wrann CD, et al., *Cell Metab*, 2013;18(5):649–659. doi:10.1016/j.cmet.2013.09.008 — irisin precursor FNDC5 drives hippocampal BDNF in mice; human irisin-crosses-BBB extrapolation remains debated.
- Moon HY, et al., *Cell Metab*, 2016;24(2):332–340. doi:10.1016/j.cmet.2016.05.025 — cathepsin-B knockout mice lose the exercise-neurogenesis benefit; in monkeys and humans, treadmill exercise raised plasma CTSB, correlating with fitness and hippocampal memory performance — the strongest human-data leg of the three myokines.

**Status: CONFIRMED**, with the softening the book's own note already calls for. Mechanism is solid in rodents; human evidence is correlational (CTSB) or extrapolated (irisin). Keep "in animal models and early human work."

**Cite:** `[Wrann et al., Cell Metab. 2013; Moon et al., Cell Metab. 2016]`

**Sources-list entry:**
- **[Wrann et al., Cell Metab. 2013; Moon et al., Cell Metab. 2016]** — Wrann CD, White JP, Salogiannnis J, et al., *Cell Metabolism* 2013;18(5):649–659, doi:10.1016/j.cmet.2013.09.008 (exercise → PGC-1α/FNDC5/irisin → hippocampal BDNF; mouse). Moon HY, Becke A, Berron D, et al., *Cell Metabolism* 2016;24(2):332–340, doi:10.1016/j.cmet.2016.05.025 (cathepsin B required for exercise-induced neurogenesis in mice; plasma CTSB rise correlates with memory function in monkeys and humans — the human leg of this evidence). *Rodent mechanism, human evidence limited to CTSB correlation; state as "animal models and early human work," not settled human causation.*

## 3. Sarcopenia rate (Ch. 11, "The quiet loss")

**Primary source:** Wilkinson DJ, Piasecki M, Atherton PJ, "The age-related loss of skeletal muscle mass and function: measurement and physiology of muscle fibre atrophy and muscle fibre loss in humans," *Ageing Res Rev*, 2018;47:123–132. doi:10.1016/j.arr.2018.07.005 — muscle mass loss begins incipiently from middle age at roughly 1%/year, with cumulative loss reaching ~50% by the 8th–9th decade in severe cases.

**Status: CONFIRMED**, with the caveat that the paper's own figure is closer to "~1%/year from midlife, accelerating with age" than a flat "1–2% after 50" — restate as a range that widens with age.

## 4. ED as a vascular sentinel (Ch. 12, "For the man reading this")

**Primary sources:**
- Montorsi F, Briganti A, Salonia A, et al., "Erectile dysfunction prevalence, time of onset and association with risk factors in 300 consecutive patients with acute chest pain and angiographically documented coronary artery disease," *Eur Urol*, 2003;44(3):360–365. doi:10.1016/S0302-2838(03)00305-1 — ED preceded CAD symptom onset in a majority of cases; mean interval ≈39 months (range 1–165 months), i.e., roughly 2–3 years on average, not a flat 3–5.
- Artery-size hypothesis reviews (e.g., Montorsi et al., *Am J Cardiol*, 2005) state penile arteries ≈1–2 mm vs. coronary ≈3–4 mm vs. carotid ≈5–7 mm — the diameters as stated in the manuscript are correct.
- Thompson IM, et al., "Erectile Dysfunction and Subsequent Cardiovascular Disease," *JAMA*, 2005;294(23):2996–3002. doi:10.1001/jama.294.23.2996 — Prostate Cancer Prevention Trial placebo arm; incident ED carried HR 1.25 (95% CI 1.02–1.53) for later cardiovascular events, an effect size comparable to smoking or family history. This paper does not itself give a "years of lead time" figure — that comes from Montorsi's mean-interval data.

**Status: CORRECTED.** Artery diameters are accurate as stated. The "3–5 years" lead time is not well supported by the primary Montorsi cohort, whose mean interval is closer to 2–3 years (39 months, wide range). Soften to "commonly two to three years, sometimes longer" or cite the range directly (1–165 months) rather than asserting a fixed 3–5-year window.

**Cite:** `[Montorsi et al., Eur. Urol. 2003; Thompson et al., JAMA 2005]`

**Sources-list entry:**
- **[Montorsi et al., Eur. Urol. 2003; Thompson et al., JAMA 2005]** — Montorsi F, et al., *European Urology* 2003;44(3):360–365, doi:10.1016/S0302-2838(03)00305-1 (ED preceded CAD symptoms in most cases; mean interval ≈39 months, range 1–165 months; artery-size hypothesis: penile ≈1–2 mm vs. coronary ≈3–4 mm). Thompson IM, et al., *JAMA* 2005;294(23):2996–3002, doi:10.1001/jama.294.23.2996 (incident ED, HR 1.25 for subsequent cardiovascular events in a 9,457-man cohort). *The book's "3–5 years" overstates the primary cohort's mean lead time (≈39 months, wide range); restate as "commonly two to three years, and sometimes longer."*

## 5. Alcohol and sleep architecture (Ch. 10, "Now the displacement I promised you")

**Primary source:** Ebrahim IO, Shapiro CM, Williams AJ, Fenwick PB, "Alcohol and Sleep I: Effects on Normal Sleep," *Alcohol Clin Exp Res*, 2013;37(4):539–549. doi:10.1111/acer.12006 — meta-analysis of 27 studies. First-half-of-night slow-wave sleep increases; REM in the first half is dose-dependent, with reduction significant only at high doses; total-night REM percentage is decreased at moderate-to-high doses; second-half sleep is more disrupted as blood alcohol clears. The review reports directional and dose-dependent effects rather than a single percentage — no "20–50% REM cut" figure appears in it.

**Status: CORRECTED — replaces the companion book's unsourced "20–50% REM reduction."** Use the qualitative, dose-dependent finding instead of any single percentage: total-night REM is measurably reduced at moderate-to-high doses, with the disruption concentrated in the second half of the night.

**Cite:** `[Ebrahim et al., Alcohol Clin. Exp. Res. 2013]`

**Sources-list entry:**

Res. 2013]** — Ebrahim IO, Shapiro CM, Williams AJ, Fenwick PB, "Alcohol and Sleep I: Effects on Normal Sleep," *Alcoholism: Clinical and Experimental Research*, 2013;37(4):539–549. doi:10.1111/acer.12006. Meta-analysis of 27 studies: increased first-half slow-wave sleep; REM suppression in the first half is dose-dependent (significant at high doses); total-night REM percentage reduced at moderate-to-high doses; second-half sleep disruption increases as blood alcohol clears. *No single percentage-reduction figure is supported — do not carry forward the companion book's "20–50% REM cut." ## 6. EPA indoor air quality (Ch. 12, "Air and water")
