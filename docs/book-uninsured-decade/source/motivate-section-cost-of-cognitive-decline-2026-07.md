THE MOTIVATE SECTION
The Real Cost of Cognitive Decline: Data, Talking Points & Discussion Prompts
Supplemental content for the MD Specialty Dinner Presentation · July 2026
How to use this: everything below is built for the motivate portion of the talk — the moment you shift from “here’s the science” to “here’s why you can’t wait.” Section 1 is the story you tell. Sections 2–3 are slide-ready numbers. Section 4 gives you audience discussion prompts, and Section 5 is a 90-second closing script. All figures are current (2024–2026) and sourced on the last page.
1. The Scenario: One Diagnosis at 70
A couple retires at 70 with an active life — tennis, travel, grandkids. Then one of them is diagnosed with Alzheimer’s, another dementia, or Parkinson’s. People with Alzheimer’s commonly live 8–10 years after diagnosis — so from 70 to 80, here is what actually happens to that household financially:

Reality check: the Alzheimer’s Association puts the average total lifetime cost of care for one person with dementia at $405,262 (in 2024 dollars) — and 70% of that is borne by the family, through out-of-pocket spending and unpaid caregiving. Our 10-year scenario above lands right on top of the national data.
The part that surprises every room: Medicare does not pay for custodial long-term care. The $8,000-a-month memory care bill, the in-home aide, the assisted living community — that is almost entirely private pay until the couple has spent down to Medicaid eligibility. The healthy spouse is left at 80 having spent a decade caregiving and a half-million dollars of the retirement they built together.
2. Slide-Ready Numbers — Financial
$405,262
Average lifetime cost of care for one person with dementia (2024 dollars) — 70% of it carried by the family. (Alzheimer’s Association, 2026 Facts & Figures)
$818 Billion
What dementia will cost the U.S. in 2026 alone — roughly $143,000 per patient per year when medical costs, unpaid care, and lost quality of life are counted. (USC Schaeffer Center, 2026)
$12,388 / year
Average out-of-pocket spending by a dementia caregiver — on top of the hours themselves. (Alzheimer’s Impact Movement)
$8,019 / month
National median cost of memory care in 2026 (~$6,700/mo in Texas). Assisted living: $6,200/mo. In-home caregiver: $35/hr — $80,000+/yr at full-time hours. Private nursing room: $129,575/yr. (CareScout 2025; SeniorLiving.org 2026)
$0
What Medicare pays toward ongoing custodial/memory care. Families pay privately until assets are spent down. 
$82.2 Billion
Annual U.S. cost of Parkinson’s disease (2024) across ~1.2M Americans — patients rack up $10,000+ in extra medical costs in the year before diagnosis alone, and care partners lost $8.3B in earnings. (Parkinson’s Foundation, 2024 report)
3. Slide-Ready Numbers — The Emotional Toll
Frame it this way: the money is only half the bill. The other half is paid by the spouse and the kids — in stress, health, and years. Dementia has been called “the long goodbye”: the family grieves the person twice, once as the disease takes them and once at the end.
59%
Of dementia caregivers report high or very-high emotional stress. 30–40% develop depression; some studies find clinically significant depressive symptoms in 40–70% of caregivers. (Alzheimer’s Association; Family Caregiver Alliance)
63% Higher Mortality
Elderly spousal caregivers experiencing caregiver strain had a 63% higher 4-year death rate than non-caregivers the same age. Caregiving can literally shorten the healthy spouse’s life. (Schulz & Beach, JAMA)
2x the Chronic Disease
Caregivers report chronic conditions — heart disease, diabetes, arthritis — at nearly twice the rate of non-caregivers (45% vs. 24%), with 23% higher stress hormones and 15% lower antibody response. (Family Caregiver Alliance)
72%
Of caregivers say they don’t get to their own doctor as often as they should; over half miss appointments outright. Among Parkinson’s care partners, 34% canceled their own healthcare visits and 1 in 5 cut their work hours. (FCA; Parkinson’s Foundation)
19 Billion Hours
Unpaid care provided by ~13 million American dementia caregivers in 2025 — worth over $446 billion. For one household, that’s the healthy spouse working an unpaid second full-time job for a decade. (Alzheimer’s Association, 2026)
4. Tie-In & Discussion Prompts for the Room
Bridge from the Bill & Cindy story
The consultation deck already uses Bill (71) and Cindy. Suggested pivot for the dinner talk: 
“We’ve talked about Bill’s shoulder. But let me ask you a harder question. What if it isn’t Bill’s shoulder — what if it’s Bill’s memory? A rotator cuff changes your tennis game. Cognitive decline changes who pays the bills, who drives, who remembers the grandkids’ names — and it hands Cindy a job she never applied for: nurse, accountant, guardian, and eventually, the only one in the marriage who remembers the marriage.”
Questions that get hands moving
“By a show of hands — who here has watched a parent, a sibling, or a friend walk through Alzheimer’s, dementia, or Parkinson’s?” (Nearly every hand goes up. Let the silence sit for a beat.)
“If it happened in your house next year, who becomes the caregiver? Look at the person you came with tonight. That’s who.”
“What’s your plan for a $500,000 problem Medicare won’t touch — hope it doesn’t happen, or do something about it while you still can?”
“We insure our homes against fires that almost never happen. What have you done to protect the thing that runs everything else — your brain?”
“Which would your spouse say is harder: writing the checks, or watching you disappear a little at a time?”
5. Suggested 90-Second Motivate Close
“Let me leave you with two numbers. The first is $405,000 — that’s the average lifetime cost when one person in a marriage develops dementia. Seventy percent of it comes out of the family’s pocket, because Medicare doesn’t pay for memory care. The second number is 63% — that’s how much higher the death rate is for a stressed spouse who becomes the caregiver. Think about that: this diagnosis doesn’t just take one life. It taxes two. Ten years — age 70 to 80 — of watching someone you love fade, while the savings you built together fade with them. That is what ‘wait and see’ costs. You cannot buy back brain cells at 80, and you cannot buy back those ten years. But tonight, at whatever age you are right now, you can still act — you can get evaluated, you can address the decline you’re noticing early, and you can look at options that may support your body’s own ability to repair. The couples who do best are never the ones who waited. They’re the ones who moved while they still had the choice.”
