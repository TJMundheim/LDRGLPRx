# Foreword

In thirty years of practicing estate planning, probate, and guardianship law, I have been across the table from people at almost every stage of life; young parents naming guardians for their children, couples planning carefully for retirement, and adult children trying to help a parent who can no longer manage alone. Repeatedly, one question has quietly determined everything that followed: Does this person have the capacity to make their own decisions?

You should know two things about me before you read another word. Dr. TJ Mundheim is my brother-in-law, and I am a client of My4MLife. Weigh everything that I say here with both of those facts in mind. I would tell a client to do the same.

For a while I served as the Guardianship Auditor for Probate Court Number Two in Tarrant County, Texas, reviewing the accounts of people who could no longer manage their own affairs. That work reminds you what a plan is for. It also shows you the worst that can happen when there is not one. I do not say this to frighten anyone. Guardianship exists for a reason. It can protect a vulnerable person but, by design, it is a last resort. It is time consuming, expensive, and deeply personal business conducted in a courtroom. It can break families apart.

As a rule, we plan well for death, and we plan poorly for decline. Death is a clean legal event. It has a date and a certificate, and the documents do what they were drafted to do. Decline has no date. It arrives as a missed bill, a repeated story, a strange decision about money that everyone explains away. Every document that I draft rests on the assumption that the person signing it understands what they are signing. Lawyers call that “capacity.” When capacity is in question, the options narrow, the cost goes up, and a decision a family could once have made at a kitchen table may end up in front of a judge.

The title page of this book says, “Don’t lose your identity and your dignity while you still have a choice.” Capacity is, in the plainest legal sense, having a choice. Capacity is not an abstract legal concept in my office. It is the difference between signing your own will and having a court decide who will speak for you. It is the difference between choosing who will manage your finances or make your medical decisions if you cannot, and leaving your family to ask a judge, in a public guardianship proceeding, for permission to do what needs to be done. When capacity is intact, we can plan. When it has faded, we are often left to react—lovingly, and with the best of intentions—under constraints that no one would have chosen.

That is why a book about preventing cognitive decline matters to a lawyer—and why I was so glad when my brother-in-law, TJ Mundheim, told me that he was writing one. His book, The Uninsured Decade, names the risk plainly: Cognitive decline is the one catastrophic expense in American retirement that no policy will fully cover. Medicare pays nothing toward memory care. If you are lucky enough to have long term care insurance, it still covers only part of the cost. A decade of care can run hundreds of thousands of dollars.

This risk does not check your net worth first. In a family with significant assets, I have seen it become a fight over who is in charge. In a family with modest means, it can become the question of whether the house survives the cost of care. The numbers change. The conversation I wish we had started five years earlier does not.

I also want to speak to the person I most often find in my office, because she is the reason this book reads the way it does. At least half the time, she is the wife (and I do not mean to exclude non-traditional couples of any kind here, I am just talking about what I see most often in my practice). She keeps the folder. She knows where the documents are, and she helps make the decisions. In many cases she ends up making all of them, because her husband is the one whose mind is failing. When I read TJ’s first draft, I told him it had to be written for her as much as for him, and he rewrote it. If you are the one holding the pen, this book is for you.

TJ writes in this book that every document in the fireproof box—the will, the powers of attorney, the directives—quietly assumes a version of you at seventy-eight who can still be the one in the room whose opinion settles the question. Sometimes that assumption fails. What TJ offers in place of that assumption is a framework he calls the 4Ms. Mind, Muscle, Mitigate, Motivate. The 4Ms are the framework. The MindSpan Score is the way to measure where you stand. I will leave the medicine to the clinicians; what I admire, as a lawyer, is the honesty of the arithmetic. He tells you plainly that the premium he proposes—roughly ten hours a month—does not break even on dementia costs alone, and he shows you the math. His point is that you do not insure a house because you expect it to burn. You buy the elimination of an outcome you cannot come back from. Unlike every policy in the folder, this one pays a dividend while you are alive to spend it: sleep, strength, energy, presence; the years between sixty-eight and seventy-eight that you were saving for, spent the way you meant to spend them.

In November, for National Alzheimer’s Disease Awareness Month, I wrote on LinkedIn that an Alzheimer’s diagnosis raises important questions about financial decisions, healthcare preferences, asset management, and future care, and that reviewing powers of attorney, healthcare directives, wills, trusts, and beneficiary designations early can help ensure a person’s wishes are clear and documented. I believe that, but my work can only go so far. I can prepare a family for the possibility of incapacity. I cannot lower the odds of it. That is the half of the problem this book takes on, and it is why I agreed to write these pages.

Lawyers who do this work tend to talk about prevention in legal terms: execute your documents while you are well; name the people you trust; tell them what you want; and do not wait for a crisis to force the conversation. All of that is essential, and I urge every reader to do it. TJ’s book sends you back to that work, too. In his final chapter he has you sit down with your attorney and check whether your powers of attorney and healthcare directives are current and signed, because, as he puts it, that is “the half with legal deadlines on it.” But legal planning protects your wishes only if we also take seriously the health of the mind that forms those wishes. Protecting cognitive health, for as long as we reasonably can, is another form of planning—perhaps the most fundamental form. It protects the person, not just the paperwork.

Estate planning and brain health are, at heart, the same conversation. Both ask: How do I preserve my independence? How do I remain the author of my own life for as long as possible? How do I spare the people that I love from having to guess (or litigate) what I would have wanted? A will, a power of attorney, and a medical directive are answers on paper. The habits and choices that help sustain memory, clarity, and judgment are answers in daily life. We need both.

There is also a matter of dignity. The clients that I remember most vividly are not only those in crisis. They are the men and women who came in while they were well, who thought ahead, who asked hard questions early, and who left with the quiet confidence that they had put their affairs, and their families, in order.

When TJ shared his purpose for this book with me, what struck me was its hopefulness and its refusal to oversell. He promises no cures and no guarantees. The odds are stated as odds. The numbers carry their sources, and the one number he will not give you is your own: that, he says, is yours to work out. This is not a book to read in fear of what might happen. It is a book to read in time, while there is still time to act, to learn, and to make deliberate choices about how you live. If it prompts you to take one step you have been putting off, whether that step concerns your health or your legal planning, it will have done something genuinely worthwhile.

I will add one more thing, and then I will stop. I am incorporating the 4M pillars into my own life. It is a work in progress. I am not doing that because he is family. I am working on it because I know what the alternative looks like both from my side of the desk and from watching my own father’s decline and eventual death due to Dementia with Lewy Bodies. It was a slow and brutal process that I would spare my children from witnessing, if possible.

So, I commend this book to you with both a professional and a personal word. Professionally, I have seen what is preserved when people plan early and what is lost when they wait too long. Personally, I am proud of TJ, my brother-in-law, a Fort Worth health-span educator with more than thirty years in clinical practice, for taking on a subject that touches every family (including our own) and for treating it with the seriousness and optimism it deserves.

Read this book with your spouse or partner, if you have one, then do both halves. Do the work it describes and call your attorney. If you do not have one, find one. Sign what needs signing while you still can. That, in my experience, is how a good future is protected: not all at once, and not in a crisis, but thoughtfully, in advance, by a person determined to decide for themselves.

# Part I — The Bill {.part}

# Chapter 1 — The Risk You Didn't Underwrite {.after-part}

<!-- voice:money -->

Somewhere in your house there is a folder, or a portal login, that holds every policy you own.

You did that. Not your accountant, not the carrier — you, or the two of you at a kitchen table. Over thirty years you built a lattice of coverage under your family so that nothing that could happen to you would land on them as a bill. And not alone: your advisor, attorney, or CPA helps protect that folder, or you are about to find one who will. Keep them close through every page of this one.

Which policy in it pays for memory care?

Not the hospital stay — you have that. Not the rehab after the hip; Medicare covers a stretch of that, and we will get to how long. I mean the other thing: what starts as a word you cannot find in a meeting and ends, as long as ten years later, with a bed in a locked wing and someone else spooning food into your mouth at eight thousand dollars a month, every month, until it stops.

Medicare pays nothing toward it. Not a reduced amount. Not a copay structure with a bad deductible. Zero. It is the single largest uncovered financial exposure in American retirement, and your advisor has fewer tools to hand you here than on any other line because the market itself thinned out underneath them.

I am not going to tell you that you can prevent dementia. Nobody can, and anyone who says otherwise is either careless or selling something. What I will tell you is that this is a risk, that risk is a thing you already know how to price, and that there is one more place to post a reserve against it.

Not a carrier. Not a rider. You.

## First, the inventory

| Risk | Policy you hold | Who writes the check |
|---|---|---|
| House burns | Homeowner's + rider | Carrier |
| Someone is hurt on your property | Homeowner's + umbrella | Carrier |
| Car wreck, at fault | Auto + umbrella | Carrier |
| You die early | Term life | Carrier |

| **Ongoing custodial and memory care** | **thin, and only if you qualify** | **Mostly you. Then your spouse. Then Medicaid.** |

Every line but one resolves to somebody else's balance sheet. That was the point of thirty years of premiums. The last line does not fully resolve. Where a policy or hybrid rider is available and you qualify, it funds part of the risk — ask your advisor, or the one you are about to find, whether one belongs in your plan. What no contract does is lower the risk. This book is about that job.

Notice the shape of that table. The risks you covered are the low-probability ones — your house probably will not burn. You bought those policies anyway, because the loss, if it landed, would be unrecoverable, and unrecoverable is the test, not likely.

Now look at the uncovered line. A 2025 study in *Nature Medicine* — the largest lifetime-risk analysis of its kind, following more than 15,000 people from age 55 until they either developed dementia or died — puts the lifetime risk of dementia after 55 at **42%**: 35% for men, 48% for women [Fang et al., Nat. Med. 2025]. That is close to a coin flip, and for a woman closer still. That counts all dementia, including mild late-life cases; Chapter 7 deals with the difference. And in a marriage you are not underwriting one life. You are underwriting the question *does this happen to either of us* — and run that arithmetic and the odds lean against you: roughly two in three.

You insured the fire. You did not insure the likelier thing. Not out of carelessness: in every other row of that table, a market showed up with an illustration and a pen and asked you to sign. On the last row, the market thinned out to almost nothing before most of us got there.

Self-pay, then spouse-pay, then impoverishment. That is the actual coverage structure on the largest exposure in your retirement.

One more thing about that folder, because I would be lying to you by omission if I left it out. I am a man, and I write from that chair. ## Bill

<!-- voice:shared -->

Bill is sixty-nine, a composite of a hundred versions of the same man. He sold his share of a mechanical contracting business at sixty-eight, plays doubles on Tuesdays and Thursdays, and has been married to Cindy for forty-four years. Cindy kept the books for that business for twenty-six of them, and the household's for all forty-four. When the advisor calls, the call is for her.

Two years ago he tore his rotator cuff reaching for a low forehand. Repaired, five months of physical therapy, back on the court by spring, a few thousand out of pocket; Medicare and the supplement absorbed the rest, exactly as designed. That is what a covered risk looks like from the inside: expensive, disruptive, and then it ends, and the balance sheet closes back up over the wound.

A rotator cuff changes your tennis game. Cognitive decline changes who pays the bills, who drives, who remembers which grandchild is which — and hands Cindy a job she never applied for: nurse, guardian, sole signature on every decision the two of them used to make together, and eventually the only one in the marriage who remembers the marriage.

The shoulder was insured. The memory is not.

This is a ruin-avoidance trade, not an investment, and I would rather lose you in Chapter 7 than get you there on a number I had to bend.

# Chapter 2 — What Nobody Pays For

<!-- voice:money -->

The most expensive assumption in American retirement planning is that Medicare handles this. It does not. Not partially.

Here is the operative language, from Medicare's own page on nursing home coverage:

> "Medicare doesn't cover custodial care if it's the only care you need."

[Medicare.gov, current]

Everything turns on one word. **Skilled care** requires a licensed professional — wound management, IV therapy, rehabilitation with a documented trajectory of improvement. Medicare covers that. **Custodial care** is help with the activities of daily living: bathing, dressing, toileting, transferring, eating, and supervision to keep a person from wandering out the front door at two in the morning. It does not require a nurse. It requires a person in the room, for as many hours as the disease demands.

That second definition is a nearly exact description of what dementia care *is*, from the middle stage onward. The category of care this disease needs is the category the program specifically excludes. That is not an oversight: Medicare was designed in 1965 as acute medical insurance. It was never built to do this.

Medicare does cover hospitalization, physician visits, imaging and cognitive testing, Part D drugs, hospice at the very end, and up to 100 days of skilled nursing per benefit period. It does not cover memory care, assisted living room and board, in-home aides, adult day programs, or any long-term stay that is "only" custodial.

That 100-day line does the most damage, because people remember the number and forget the conditions. It is *skilled* nursing facility care, gated by a qualifying hospital stay, conditional on a clinician continuing to document that the care is skilled, and carrying substantial daily coinsurance from day 21. In a dementia course it is usually irrelevant: it gets consumed by the hip fracture or the pneumonia, and then the person goes to memory care. No benefit period restarts for "still has dementia."

## What the private-pay meter reads

<!-- voice:money -->

If Medicare is not paying, someone is. National medians, with the year on each figure:

- **Memory care:** $8,019 per month, 2026 — about $96,000 a year. In Texas, $6,684 [SeniorLiving.org 2026].
- **Assisted living:** $6,200 per month, or $74,400 a year, up 5% year over year [CareScout 2025].
- **In-home caregiver:** $35 an hour. At 20 hours a week that is $36,400 a year; at 44 hours a week, $80,080 [CareScout 2025].
- **Nursing home, private room:** $129,575 a year — $355 a day [CareScout 2025].

## Spend-down

<!-- voice:money-legal -->

One government program does pay for long-term custodial care: Medicaid. It is means-tested, which means the way you become eligible is by not having money anymore. The industry word is **spend-down**. Numbers vary by state; 2026 Texas figures are illustrative, but the architecture is the same nearly everywhere [TX Medicaid, 2026 figures]:

- **Countable resources** must come down to roughly $2,000 for an individual applicant, or $3,000 for a couple.
- A **five-year, sixty-month look-back** on asset transfers. Gifts inside that window trigger a penalty period of ineligibility — the provision that punishes families who start planning after the diagnosis instead of before it.
- The at-home spouse is not left with nothing. A **Community Spouse Resource Allowance** protects between $32,532 and $162,660 in assets, plus up to $4,066.50 a month in protected income.

All of that is legal and financial work, not health work, and none of it is do-it-yourself. Medicaid planning, spousal protections, titling, the durable power of attorney and the healthcare directives signed while they can still be signed — that is your estate attorney's and your advisor's craft. The five-year window makes it timing-sensitive: it belongs on their calendar now, not after a diagnosis closes the good options.

## The market that left

<!-- voice:money -->

Around the year 2000, more than a hundred carriers sold standalone long-term-care insurance in the United States. Today fewer than a dozen actively write new standalone policies [Forte, Contingencies 2024]. That is not a market correction. That is an evacuation.

Most of these contracts are guaranteed renewable but not premium-guaranteed: the carrier cannot cancel you, but it can ask your state insurance department to raise the rate on the entire class — and it has, repeatedly. Genworth received a 68.5% average approved increase in a 2026 Nebraska filing; in 2022 it raised rates on more than two thousand policyholders by an average of 97%, with individual increases from 79% to 173%; a 161% request was denied in Massachusetts as "unjust, unfair and inequitable" [S&P Global; state filings, 2022–2026]. John Hancock secured increases across more than ten states in a single 2026 quarter, on blocks already carrying 15% in 2012–15 and 43.8% phased from 2020 [S&P Global; longtermcaredesk.com, 2026]. Stack those and a policyholder at seventy may be paying two or three times the premium signed for at fifty-eight, at exactly the point when the household's income is fixed.

That is the trap in one line. **The event that makes you want the policy is the event that makes you uninsurable for it.**

So the honest count. Only about 10 to 15% of Americans 65 and older hold long-term-care insurance of any kind, against an estimate that roughly 70% of people over 65 will need long-term care of some sort [CRR / HCG Secure, 2026]. Seventy percent will need it. Ten to fifteen percent have it. You are not lazy for being in the uncovered majority; the market moved out from under a generation of buyers.

None of that is an indictment of the people who advise you. It is the terrain they now plan across. When a hundred carriers become a dozen, the professional holding your plan has fewer instruments and harder tradeoffs than the one who sat across from your father — which is exactly why their review matters more now, not less, and why this book's question belongs on that agenda.

I do not sell insurance and I cannot advise you on it. If you are shopping it, your advisor or a licensed agent should answer three questions in writing: is the premium guaranteed or merely guaranteed renewable, and what is this carrier's rate-increase history; what triggers a benefit, who decides, and how long is the elimination period you pay through; and what does the benefit look like in the twelfth year against the projected cost of care, not today's? A policy or a hybrid rider can fund this risk; it cannot lower it, and the work in the rest of this book cannot fund it — some households should own both, so ask your advisor.

# Chapter 3 — Ten Years, One Household

<!-- voice:money -->

Bill is diagnosed at seventy.

It happens the way it usually happens: eighteen months of Cindy noticing, six months of Cindy not saying anything, one bad afternoon when he cannot find his way back from the club he has driven to for eleven years, and then a neurologist's office and a word.

The Alzheimer's Association reports that people 65 and older survive an average of four to eight years after a diagnosis of Alzheimer's, and some as long as twenty [Alz. Assoc. 2026]. Bill's ten years is longer than that average, and I use it on purpose, because a plan has to survive a long course. So the question is not *what does a diagnosis cost* — a diagnosis costs a copay. The question is what the decade costs.

| Stage | What the care actually looks like | Estimated family cost |
|---|---|---|
| **Years 1–3** (age 70–73)<br>Early | Cindy provides nearly all care. Doctor visits, medications, home safety changes, legal and financial planning. Average dementia-caregiver out-of-pocket spending runs about $12,388 a year — *a figure based on 2021 data, and meaningfully higher in today's dollars* [Alz. Impact Movement, 2021 data]. | **~$35,000–$40,000** |
| **Years 4–6** (age 73–76)<br>Middle | Cindy can no longer do it alone. Paid in-home caregiver 20 hours a week at $35/hr (~$36,400/yr) [CareScout 2025], plus an adult day program two days a week (~$9,900/yr, about $95 a day [CareScout 2025]), plus out-of-pocket medical. | **~$170,000–$180,000** |
| **Years 7–10** (age 76–80)<br>Late | 24/7 supervision required. Memory care runs $8,019/mo nationally, about $96,000 a year [SeniorLiving.org 2026]; a private nursing-home room is $129,575 a year [CareScout 2025]. Assume roughly 3.5 years in memory care, priced from about the Texas rate to the national median. A private nursing-home room would run higher. | **~$290,000–$340,000** |
| **Ten-year total** | Cash and care costs falling on the household — before counting the healthy spouse's unpaid hours, lost income, or health decline. | **~$495,000–$560,000** |

A half-million-dollar number invites the suspicion that somebody built it backward from the number they wanted, so here is the check. The Alzheimer's Association puts the average total lifetime cost of care for one person with dementia at **$405,262**, in 2024 dollars, with **70% of it borne by the family** through out-of-pocket spending and unpaid caregiving [Alz. Assoc. 2026]. Our composite lands above that national average, which is what you would expect: a ten-year course with 3.5 years in a facility, against a figure that averages every course, including the short ones. One difference to keep in view: that national figure counts unpaid caregiving, and this ledger does not.

## Why the ledger is shaped like that

<!-- voice:money -->

In years one through three, the household is buying *things*. Medication copays. A grab bar. An attorney's time — Cindy makes the call — to get the powers of attorney signed while Bill still can. A bad year financially, not a catastrophic one — which is exactly why nobody restructures anything, and why the first three years give a false read on the whole decade.

In years four through six, the household starts buying *hours*. This is the phase change. Somewhere in there is a specific week where Cindy admits she cannot leave the house to go to the grocery store, and the household starts paying $35 an hour for someone else to be in the room. Twenty hours a week is one shift a weekday and costs $36,400 a year; every additional hour is another $1,820 a year, forever. Care is the one purchase in American life with essentially no economies of scale.

In years seven through ten, the household is buying *a bed with staff attached*, and the meter reads $80,000 to $130,000 a year until the day it stops. That is when the portfolio conversation happens, and by then it is a liquidation sequence, not a planning conversation.

## Which account it comes out of

<!-- voice:money -->

It does not come out of income. Social Security and whatever pension the household has covers essentially none of a $96,000-a-year care bill on top of ordinary retirement living. The shortfall comes out of the portfolio, on the facility's schedule rather than the market's, which makes Bill and Cindy forced sellers every month for years — the sequence-of-returns risk their advisor explained at sixty-two, arriving at seventy-four at a withdrawal rate far above what a normal retirement plan is built to carry. Then the tax layer: realized gains, IRA withdrawals taxed as ordinary income, higher Medicare surcharges. Then the house, sold in year seven or eight at the moment Cindy has the least capacity to run a sale. None of that shows up in the right-hand column, and all of it turns a $500,000 care cost into something considerably worse.

## The tail on the right

<!-- voice:money -->

Bill and Cindy are a median household on a long course of disease, and this distribution is not symmetric. The low end is bounded: a short course, two or three years, mostly at home. The high end is not. Younger, physically robust patients live longer with the diagnosis, which is the cruelest arithmetic in this subject: the fitter the body, the longer the decade. A course that runs twelve or fourteen years, five of them in a private room at the national median of $129,575 a year, and more in a high-cost metro, does not produce a $560,000 bill. It produces something well north of $800,000, with no ceiling written anywhere.

## The one that isn't on the statement

<!-- voice:health -->

In 2025, roughly 13 million Americans provided more than **19 billion hours** of unpaid dementia care, valued at over **$446 billion** [Alz. Assoc. 2026]. Divide that down to one household and it is Cindy, working an unpaid second full-time job for a decade — on call at three in the morning, with no version where she gives notice. If you priced Cindy's hours at the same $35 an hour the agency charges, the ten-year number above roughly doubles.

This is where most books about this subject would move on to solutions. I am not going to, because the financial case is not the case. The largest cost in this decade is not on the ledger and it is not paid by Bill.

# Chapter 4 — The Second Patient

<!-- voice:health -->

Cindy is seventy-nine when it ends.

She has been the primary caregiver for ten years, the last three driving to a facility every day because she could not stand the thought of him being there without a familiar face in the afternoon. She is the one who learned to give the medications. She is the one who took the car keys, which was the worst day of the marriage.

And she is the second patient in this story. She was never diagnosed with anything, never entered into his chart. Nobody ever wrote her a treatment plan. If you read only one chapter of this book, read this one.

A landmark 1999 JAMA study — Schulz and Beach, the Caregiver Health Effects Study — found that elderly spousal caregivers reporting strain had a **63% higher four-year mortality rate** than non-caregiving spouses of the same age [Schulz & Beach, JAMA 1999]. That study is twenty-seven years old, and I am telling you its age on purpose. Nothing since has overturned it, but hold it as what it is: a robust, old, still-standing finding that the strain of caregiving is associated with dying sooner.

**The diagnosis does not take one life. It taxes two.**

The rest of the picture is consistent. Family caregivers report chronic conditions at nearly twice the rate of non-caregivers, **45% versus 24%**, and roughly **40% of dementia caregivers** show clinically significant depressive symptoms, against 5 to 17% [Family Caregiver Alliance; older, undated figures]. And they stop taking care of themselves, which is the mechanism connecting all of it: in the Parkinson's data, **34% of care partners canceled or missed their own healthcare visits** and more than one in five retired early or cut work hours [Parkinson's Foundation, 2024 data].

## The decade, from her side of it

Year two, Cindy is still calling it *helping*. She drives to the club now because Bill got turned around once, and she has framed it as wanting the time together. The decisions the two of them used to make at the kitchen table, she now makes alone, and signs for both of them. Year four, the calendar on the refrigerator has three colors of ink on it and none of them are hers. She goes to the wedding and leaves before the reception, because three hours away is a project and six is not possible — and the invitations thin out, not because anyone decided anything, but because she has said no eleven times.

Year six, she is not sleeping. He is up at two, and then at four, and she is listening for him even in the hours he is down. She has a cardiology follow-up she moves twice and then lets go. The aide comes four hours a weekday, enough to get to the grocery store and back and not enough to get to a gym, and she has not lifted anything heavier than a laundry basket in four years. Her daughter says *Mom, you have to take care of yourself*, and Cindy says *I know*, which is true, and does nothing about it, which is also true.

Year eight, he is in the facility and she is there every afternoon, and she has come to understand something she cannot say to her children: that the hardest part of this was never the money. It was the year she was grieving a man who was still sitting at the table.

> She did not get the diagnosis, and she is paying for it longer than he did.

## Who this book is actually for

If you are the person who reads the statements, you have spent thirty years buying down risk on behalf of the person across the table. That is what the umbrella policy was. That is what the term life was — you were never going to see a dollar of it. You bought it so that the worst version of your absence did not land on the one you love as a bill.

The uninsured decade is the one exposure where that instinct runs short of instruments. Coverage, where you can get it, funds part of the bill; no contract lowers the odds of the decade itself. And if it goes unaddressed, the outcome is the precise thing you spent three decades insuring against: the worst version of what happens to one of you, landing on the other — not as a check to cash, but as a decade to spend.

And if you are the Cindy in your house — the one who already keeps the folder, and can already see which chair you may end up in — read this chapter twice. The decade lands on whichever of you is left holding the pen, and at two in three for a couple, you do not get to choose which chair. That is the whole argument of this book. Everything after this is arithmetic and logistics.

# Part II — The Premium {.part}

# Chapter 5 — The Dividend {.after-part}

<!-- voice:health -->

Every policy in that folder has one thing in common, and it is the thing nobody says out loud at the closing table.

You hope it never pays.

The premium in this book has a different shape. It has two payouts.

The first is the one you have been reading about for four chapters. It is contingent. It arrives, if it arrives at all, as a decade that did not happen — a ledger that never got written, an aide who was never hired, a Tuesday afternoon that Cindy spent at her book club instead of in a parking garage. You will never be able to prove you collected it. You will only be able to notice, at eighty-two, that the invoice never came.

The second payout is not contingent, and it does not wait.

It starts inside about six weeks. It is paid in sleep that goes unbroken, in the fact that you can pick up a grandchild without doing the calculation first, in an afternoon at four o'clock that does not require a decision about coffee. It is paid in a lab report, a sex life that did not quietly end, a walk up a jetway with a bag over your shoulder.

You do not have to develop dementia to collect it. You do not have to *avoid* dementia to collect it. It pays on the premium itself.

**It is the only policy in the folder that pays before the claim.**

## What you are actually buying

And it is the part I actually care about, so let me say it once and get back to the arithmetic. The goal is not to avoid dementia. It is not even to protect the money, though the money is why we are talking. The goal is the best mind you can have until your last day of life — the whole span, not the tail. Sharp at seventy-eight. Present at eighty-four. Still the one people call when something has to be decided. The long *useful* life, with the decline compressed into as short a window as the body will allow, at the far end, where it belongs.

## The dividend, itemized

**Sleep.** Not eight hours as a virtue — seven or eight that are *consolidated*, so you wake at six and the day starts, rather than waking at six having been up at one-forty and again at four.

**Strength.** The kind that shows up as capacity: two suitcases up a flight of stairs, getting off the floor without a hand on the coffee table. Muscle is the reserve you draw on in every bad week of the next thirty years.

**Energy at four in the afternoon.** The one your spouse notices before you do. The four-o'clock collapse is not aging. It is usually blood sugar, sleep debt, and a lunch that was mostly bread.

**Sex.** Not a lifestyle amenity — the vascular system filing an early report, and the most reliable canary either partner in a marriage gets. It is also, plainly, a payout.

**Presence.** Your grandchildren will not remember whether you were fit. They will remember whether you were *there* — and "there" is a metabolic state before it is a character trait.

**The active retirement decade.** Sixty-eight to seventy-eight is the decade you were saving for, and most people spend it at a level of function they never chose and never noticed choosing.

Not one line on that list requires a diagnosis or a statistic. Every line pays out inside a year.

> ### The Premium
>
> **The premium:** roughly ten hours a month and roughly eight to ten thousand dollars a year, most of it money you are already spending. Chapter 6 prices it out line by line.
>
> **What it buys:** two payouts from one premium. A contingent one — lower odds on the decade in Chapter 3, stated as odds and never as a promise. And a non-contingent one, paid out while you are alive to spend it: sleep, strength, energy, sex, presence, an active retirement decade.
>
> **What it does not buy:** immunity. Nothing in this book prevents, cures, or reverses dementia. Anyone who tells you otherwise is selling something, and Chapter 7 is where I show you the math with nothing hidden.

## What a dividend would have looked like for Bill

Bill was fifty-five once. Thirty pounds heavier than at forty. An A1c that came back at 5.9 that spring, which the nurse called *borderline* and he heard as *fine*. Sleeping badly for years and calling it a personality trait. Off the tennis court because of a knee he never got looked at.

Nobody can tell you what that does to the diagnosis at seventy, and Chapter 7 is where I stop hand-waving. But this I can say with no statistical hedging at all: the thirteen years between fifty-five and sixty-eight would have been materially better years, and those years are contingent on nothing. That is the dividend, paid whether or not the claim is ever filed.

And it is why the line on the cover of *Begin with the End in Mind* belongs in this one too. Don't lose your identity and your dignity while you still have a choice. The ledger is about the money. The dividend is about the choice.

# Chapter 6 — Ten Hours and $675

<!-- voice:health -->

Ask someone in their fifties why they are not doing this and the answer is never the money. It is time — a genuine constraint, not an excuse. So let's find the actual number, because it is not the one most people carry around. That one was set by a magazine article in 1998.

**Two strength sessions a week, forty-five minutes each.** Not five. Two. Compound movements — a push, a pull, a hinge, a squat, a carry — loaded heavily enough that the last two repetitions are genuinely difficult, which is the entire active ingredient and the part almost everybody leaves out. Two sessions done seriously beat five done conversationally.

**The fasted morning walk: twenty minutes, five mornings, before you eat.** A walk taken fasted does something metabolically different from the same walk after breakfast, and it is the single most accessible lever most people have. Outside if you can, for the light. The protein rule attaches here: **you break the fast after the walk, and you break it with thirty to forty grams of lean protein before anything else goes in.** Not the coffee-and-toast that has been the default since 1974.

## The gross number and the net number

Done honestly, the gross number is not ten. The strength sessions are six and a half hours a month, the walks another seven, the planning one — roughly fifteen before a single minute of cooking. The title works because of what those hours displace, so here is that subtraction in the open. The cooking is close to a wash: you are already eating, and four dinners cooked replace four ordered. The walk is mostly a wash: call a third of it new, about two and a half hours a month. The strength sessions are not a wash: six and a half new hours. Planning adds one.

Net new time, honestly counted: **about ten hours a month. Two and a half hours a week** — roughly two percent of your waking hours, less than the average American past fifty-five spends watching television in a single day [BLS, American Time Use Survey 2025]. If your calendar cannot absorb ten hours a month, the calendar is the finding, not the protocol.

## The premium against the face value

<!-- voice:shared -->

- **Premium:** roughly $10,000 a year. **Ten-year premium:** $100,000.
- **Exposure:** the ten-year ledger in Chapter 3 — roughly $495,000 to $560,000 at national-median prices on a ten-year course, with a right tail past $800,000 and no ceiling written anywhere.

Roughly five to one at that ten-year exposure, and better in the tail — exactly the region insurance exists to address. But I will not let that pass as the same ratio as your term policy, which pays full face value if the event occurs. **This premium does not.** It pays nothing on a diagnosis. What it does is reduce the probability that the event occurs at all — by some amount, which is real, and which is smaller than the number you would like it to be. That distinction is the entire subject of the next chapter.

Which is why this basket is not being scored against anything in your folder. A funding instrument and a risk-reduction habit do different jobs on the same line, and neither substitutes for the other. Some households should own both — ask your advisor.

# Chapter 7 — Odds, Stated Honestly

<!-- voice:shared-math -->

I promised in Chapter 1 that I would not tell you this pays for itself. Here is where I keep that promise, with the arithmetic on the page. Four inputs; argue with any of them and the answer moves.

**One. Lifetime risk of dementia after fifty-five: 42%.** From the ARIC cohort study published in *Nature Medicine* in January 2025, following more than 15,000 people from age fifty-five — 42% overall, 35% for men, 48% for women [Fang et al., Nat. Med. 2025].

**Two. For a mixed-sex couple, the probability that at least one of you develops it: about 66%.** Not a separate finding — arithmetic on the first: 1 − (0.65 × 0.52) = 0.662.

**Three. Average lifetime cost of care if it happens: $405,262**, in 2024 dollars, roughly 70% of it borne by the family [Alz. Assoc. 2026].

**Four. Risk reduction from sustained structured lifestyle change: 30%.** The number I have to defend hardest, and I defend it below. It is deliberately lower than the figure you have probably seen quoted.

One caveat travels with the 42% every time it is used. **That 42% is all-cause dementia, including mild late-life cases** — a broader category than "Alzheimer's requiring years of full-time memory care." The $405,262 figure is the average cost among people who develop dementia and require care, not further discounted for the mild cases. So the table below is not "66% of couples face a half-million-dollar decade." It is a risk-weighted expected value, using the broadest risk figure and an unadjusted average cost, which pull in two directions at once.

| | Lifetime risk | Expected cost, unmitigated<br>(risk × $405,262) | Expected savings at **30%** risk reduction | Net against a $100,000 ten-year premium |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Single person** | 42% | $170,210 | **$51,063** | **−$48,937** |
| **Couple** — "does this happen to either of us" | 66.2% | $268,283 | **$80,485** | **−$19,515** |

There it is. A couple paying $10,000 a year for ten years comes out roughly **nineteen thousand five hundred dollars short** on dementia-cost avoidance alone. A single person comes out about **forty-nine thousand short**. It does not break even. I told you in Chapter 1 that it would not.

One note on the couple line, because a careful reader will run it. It assumes your two risks are independent and counts one bill per couple. Count a second bill in the households where both of you develop it, and the expected savings rise to about $100,900, roughly the premium. But two people running this protocol also pay more than the $10,000 here, which is closer to one person's gym and trainer. Run it either way and it is a close call on dementia costs alone, not a return.

Two more limits, both against me. That table counts the whole $405,262, though the family carries about 70% of it; on the family's share, the couple's shortfall is about $43,700. And it charges ten years of premium against a lifetime risk, when the premium runs as long as you keep paying it. Both make the gap wider. This is not an investment case, and those are two more reasons I will not make it one.

## The number you have seen quoted

<!-- voice:health -->

You have probably encountered a friendlier figure: that up to 45% of dementia cases are attributable to modifiable risk factors. It comes from the 2024 Lancet Commission, which identified fourteen — hearing loss, hypertension, smoking, obesity, depression, physical inactivity, diabetes, excess alcohol, head injury, air pollution, social isolation, high LDL, untreated vision loss, and low education [Lancet Commission 2024]. 45% is a **population-attributable fraction**: what share of cases would theoretically not occur if everyone eliminated all fourteen factors across the whole life course, starting in childhood. It is a public-health ceiling under conditions that will never obtain. **It is not a statement that you, personally, starting at fifty-eight, cut your own risk by 45%.** No study shows that.

For the record, the ceiling would put the couple's arithmetic at $268,283 × 0.45 = **$120,727**, which is $20,727 *above* the $100,000 premium. I show it so you know I did not hide it, and I label it plainly: **a population ceiling, not a result any individual household should expect.** It is the version of this arithmetic that most clearly produces a positive number, and I am not going to sell you the positive one. **30% stays the working assumption.**

## The evidence underneath 30%

**The strongest single piece is US POINTER**, published in *JAMA* in July 2025 — 2,111 participants, mean age 68.2, two years, randomized. Both a structured multidomain lifestyle program and a self-guided version improved global cognition, and the structured arm improved significantly more; the benefit held across age, sex, ethnicity, cardiovascular status, and APOE-ε4 genotype [US POINTER, JAMA 2025]. Read what that says and what it does not. It is a protection-from-decline finding over two years, **not** a finding that anybody prevented dementia. No trial has run long enough to show that.

Pooled across meta-analyses, physical activity carries a relative risk for all-cause dementia of about **0.80** in the more active, with leisure-time activity closer to **0.76** [Iso-Markku et al., BJSM 2022; Feter et al., Lancet Public Health 2026] — roughly 20 to 24% from one domain, observational, with all the confounding that implies. Stack a second and third and 30% is a defensible aggregate assembled across evidence types, not a number any single trial has produced. That is the honest state of the field.

## Three things that tip the ledger

<!-- voice:shared -->

**One: you are not buying one risk reduction. You are buying several, simultaneously.** The table prices one line item. The strength work, the metabolic control, the sleep, and the removal of chronic inflammatory load act on the same substrate that produces type 2 diabetes, hypertension, cardiovascular disease, stroke, and sarcopenia — and those conditions are not in a separate column from dementia. They are the road that runs to it. A type 2 diabetes diagnosis at sixty to sixty-nine carries a **hazard ratio of 1.70** for subsequent dementia; at fifty to fifty-nine, **1.72**; before fifty, **1.90** — each against diagnosis at seventy or later. Obesity plus diabetes diagnosed before fifty carries a hazard ratio of **3.05** [Qi et al., PLOS ONE 2024]. Roughly a tripling, from two conditions both addressed by the same ten hours a month. The math above ignores that correlated portfolio, which is one more reason not to read it as the whole trade.

**Two: the right tail.** The $405,262 in that table is an average, and Chapter 3 told you the distribution around it is not symmetric. The low side is bounded. The high side is not. Arithmetic on an average understates the thing you are actually protecting against. The middle is not the risk. The tail is the risk.

Which brings me to the sentence this chapter has been walking toward. **You do not insure the house because you expect it to burn.** The expected value of your homeowner's policy is deeply negative and has been every year for thirty years, and you have never once considered dropping it, because you are not buying expected value. You are buying the elimination of an outcome you cannot come back from. That is ruin avoidance, a completely different trade from an investment — and the uninsured decade is the last unrecoverable loss on your schedule, the one where part of the reserve has to come out of physiology because no carrier can post that part for you.

Read that as an argument for coverage, not against it. Nothing here is a reason to drop or defer anything in your folder: funding the risk and lowering it are two halves of one answer. Some households should own both — ask your advisor.

**Three: the dividend, which the table cannot price at all.** The table treats $100,000 of premium as money spent on nothing but a shift in probability. It leaves out ten years of unbroken sleep, of strength, of a four o'clock that is not a wall, delivered whether or not a claim is ever filed. I have not put a dollar figure on that, because any figure I chose would be invented. You can price your own.

## Where that leaves the trade

> ### The Premium
>
> **The premium:** ten hours a month. Roughly $8,000–$10,000 a year, most of it money already leaving your account.
>
> **What it buys:** an assumed 30% reduction in the odds of the decade in Chapter 3 — which does not break even on that line item alone — plus a reduction across a correlated set of exposures the arithmetic never priced, protection against a tail with no ceiling, and a dividend that does not depend on a diagnosis, which most people start to notice in about six weeks.
>
> **What it does not buy:** immunity, a benefit check, or a guarantee. Nothing in this book prevents, cures, treats, or reverses dementia, Alzheimer's, or Parkinson's. It may lower the odds. That is the only sentence an honest person can sell you, and it is the only one I will.

## What happens at night

Sleep is not rest. In 2013, in mice, a research team at the University of Rochester described a waste-clearance network in the brain — cerebrospinal fluid moving through the spaces between brain cells and flushing out the metabolic debris of the day, including the protein fragments most directly implicated in Alzheimer's pathology [Xie et al., *Science* 2013]. That system is largely dormant while you are awake. It runs during deep, slow-wave sleep, in the first third of the night. The trash goes out at night or it does not go out. The second job of the night is filing: during REM sleep, the day you just lived gets moved from short-term storage into long-term storage. Miss it and the meeting from Tuesday is smudged at the edges by Thursday — which is, incidentally, the symptom most people worry about first.

Now the displacement I promised you in Chapter 6. The evening hour that reorganizes itself when the strength sessions arrive is usually the hour in which a second drink happens, and alcohol is sedating, which is not the same as sleep. It gets you down faster, then the suppression concentrates in the second half of the night, cutting into the REM block while you are unconscious and unable to notice [Ebrahim et al., Alcohol Clin. Exp. Res. 2013]. That is not a moral point. It is an accounting point: you are paying for the drink twice, and the second payment is drawn from the account this whole book is about.

Morning light is the cheapest item on this line. Ten minutes outdoors soon after you wake gives your body clock the anchor your sleep depends on, and it's on your first-week checklist in Chapter 13.

## What your gut is manufacturing

About 90% of the body's serotonin — mood, sleep, appetite — is produced in the gut. Roughly half of your dopamine — motivation, reward, focus — is made there too. And the vagus nerve, the largest cranial nerve you own, is overwhelmingly a one-way street: roughly 80% of its traffic runs gut-to-brain, not brain-to-gut [Gershon & Tack, Gastroenterology 2007; Eisenhofer et al., JCEM 1997; Berthoud & Neuhuber, Auton. Neurosci. 2000]. Your gut is filing a continuous report to your head. The only question is what is in the report.

The failure mode, in one line. The lining of the gut is a single layer of cells — one cell thick — and it is the entire physical boundary between everything you eat and the rest of your bloodstream. Degrade it, and bacterial fragments cross into circulation that were never supposed to be there. The immune system reads that as an invasion and mounts a low-grade inflammatory response, everywhere, all the time. The same inflammatory traffic degrades the barrier protecting the brain. And the brain translates it into the only vocabulary it has: fog, flat mood on a perfectly fine Tuesday, the colleague's name that is gone for three full seconds. You are not necessarily getting old. You may be inflamed.

## Three front doors standing open

Two of the Lancet Commission's fourteen modifiable risk factors are not what anybody expects, and a third door, your teeth, is not on that list but belongs beside them. All three are things a competent household fixes in a month [Lancet Commission 2024; Dominy et al., *Sci. Adv.* 2019].

**Hearing.** Untreated hearing loss is on that list — not hearing aids as a vanity concession, but hearing loss as a load on the brain, which spends its reserve decoding speech rather than holding a conversation. A hearing test and, if indicated, a device.

**Vision.** Untreated vision loss is on the list too. A cataract getting quietly worse for six years is not just an eye problem. It is a light problem, which makes it a sleep problem.

**Teeth.** The bacteria of chronic gum disease have been identified in the brain tissue of Alzheimer's patients [Dominy et al., *Sci. Adv.* 2019]. Gum disease is systemic inflammation with an address. A periodontal probing once a year — not a cleaning, a probing.

## What the blank line looks like

Leave this line blank and nothing happens on Tuesday. That is the whole problem with it. What happens instead is the slow version: fragmented sleep that never gets diagnosed, a body clock with no anchor, a gut barrier under daily insult so the fog gets filed under *fifty-eight*, and three front doors standing open the whole time. None of that produces an event. It produces a slope — and a slope that nobody intercepts is exactly the shape of the arc in Chapter 3. This is a line item, not a project with a completion date. You do not "finish" a premium either.

# Chapter 9 — Muscle: The Reserve You Post in Advance

<!-- voice:health -->

This is the expensive line. Nine of the ten hours are here, and most of the dollars. Four items: two strength sessions a week, a twenty-minute walk before you eat, protein first when you break the fast, and a waist measurement you take seriously. There is no fifth thing.

## Two sessions, and why they are the neuroprotective item

When a muscle contracts under real load, it secretes signaling proteins into the bloodstream. Some cross into the brain and increase the primary growth factor for neurons. In plain terms, when you load a bar and stand up with it, your legs send a chemical message to your head that says *grow* [Wrann et al., Cell Metab. 2013; Moon et al., Cell Metab. 2016]. That is animal-model mechanism plus early human work, not settled human causation — but without the lift the message does not get sent, which is why "staying active" is not a substitute. Golf, a Saturday hike, ten thousand steps: excellent things. They are not load.

## The quiet loss, which is the real exposure

Past fifty, without resistance training, the average person loses roughly one percent of their muscle mass a year, accelerating toward two percent in later decades [Wilkinson et al., Ageing Res. Rev. 2018]. Quietly. Painlessly. Invisibly under good clothes. Run that forward from fifty to seventy and it is a fifth to a third of the lean tissue you are carrying right now.

Muscle is not decoration. It is where glucose goes after a meal, which is most of what stands between you and the metabolic road in the next chapter. It is the scaffolding that decides whether a fall at seventy-eight is an inconvenience or the beginning of the end. And it is cognitive reserve: two people can carry similar amounts of underlying pathology and function very differently, and the differences travel with things like this.

## The walk, and the thirty grams that follow it

Twenty minutes, five mornings, before anything crosses your lips, outdoors for the light. Overnight, insulin is at its low point, and movement in that state pulls preferentially at stored fat. It costs nothing and doubles as your circadian anchor. Then you break the fast — with **thirty to forty grams of lean protein before anything else goes in.** Not because protein is virtuous, but because there is a threshold: below a certain dose, the signal that tells muscle tissue to maintain itself fires weakly and the body shrugs. That threshold rises with age — twenty grams was enough at thirty, thirty to forty is the floor at fifty-five. Five ounces of chicken. Five or six eggs. And then a window: most people do well eating between roughly nine in the morning and six in the evening, because insulin sensitivity is genuinely better earlier in the day and eating at nine at night puts digestion in direct competition with the sleep architecture from the last chapter.

## Visceral fat, and where medication fits

The fat around your organs is not the fat under your skin. Abdominal fat is endocrine tissue: it secretes inflammatory signaling molecules around the clock, drives insulin resistance in both the body and the brain, and in a man it runs an enzyme that converts his own testosterone into estrogen — which is why the loop tightens on itself. The scale can hold perfectly steady while all of that gets worse. Measure the waist.

For some people, a licensed clinician will determine that weight-management medication is appropriate. That class of drug amplifies a satiety hormone your own gut already makes — not a stimulant, not a cheat code — and it belongs inside the rest of this schedule, not in place of it: dropping intake sharply without the protein rule and the strength sessions costs lean tissue you cannot spare. Budget $150 to $300 a month if it is indicated, nothing if it is not.

## What the blank line looks like

Leave this line blank and the ledger writes itself in the middle row of Chapter 3. Muscle comes off at one to two percent a year for twenty years. Glucose handling degrades because the place it was supposed to go is smaller. Visceral fat accumulates and produces its own inflammatory load. Then a fall at seventy-eight, or the pneumonia after the hip — the hundred days of skilled nursing that Chapter 2 told you not to count on gets consumed, and the household comes out needing hours instead of appointments, at $36,400 a year for twenty hours a week and another $1,820 for every hour on top of it, forever [CareScout 2025]. That phase change arrives through the body long before it arrives through the diagnosis.

# Chapter 10 — Mitigate: Stop Paying Claims Against Yourself

<!-- voice:health -->

This line costs essentially no hours, and it is the one nobody funds. That is not a paradox. Mitigate is not a list of things to add. It is a list of things to stop — and subtraction has no calendar entry, no equipment, and nothing to post about.

The framing I use everywhere else: **eliminate the insulting behavior.** Stop hurting yourself first. Then add what works. You have a vocabulary for this already. You do not reduce your loss ratio by buying more coverage. You reduce it by not filing claims against yourself.

## The metabolic road

Nearly every road to a dementia diagnosis runs through somewhere else first, and the somewhere else is a set of numbers you can already read. The Lancet Commission's fourteen modifiable risk factors are, honestly read, about half a cardiometabolic list [Lancet Commission 2024]. And Chapter 7 gave you the hazard ratios — 1.70, 1.72, and 1.90 for type 2 diabetes diagnosed progressively earlier, and 3.05 for obesity together with diabetes before fifty [Qi et al., PLOS ONE 2024]. Roughly a tripling, from two conditions both largely addressed by the nine hours in the last chapter.

So the four gauges here are not exotic, and your physician already draws three of them. **A1c** — Bill's came back at 5.9 at fifty-five and somebody said *borderline*, and he heard *fine*, and nobody ever mentioned it again. Know the number and the direction it has moved over three draws, not one. **LDL and the rest of the lipid panel**, on the Commission's list as of the 2024 update. **Blood pressure**, the single most modifiable item on that list and the one most likely to be sitting untreated at 148 over 90 behind the words *I feel fine*. And **waist**, which nobody's chart records. Four gauges, a baseline column, and a column twelve weeks later.

## The two insults, honestly

**Alcohol.** I am fifty-nine, and I grew up in the same culture you did, where the after-work pour was a reward and the dinner-party wine was a social contract. But you are entitled to the number. Between three and seven drinks a week the evidence is genuinely contested and I will not pretend otherwise. Above seven, and especially in the ten-to-fourteen zone where a lot of successful households quietly live, the picture sharpens: measurable effects on the memory structure of the brain on imaging, on sleep architecture, on hormones, and a cancer risk that climbs in roughly linear fashion. The large 2018 analysis that ended the red-wine story concluded that the level associated with the lowest overall harm is zero [GBD Alcohol Collaborators, *Lancet* 2018].

**Smoking and nicotine.** On the Commission's list, in every version. If this applies to you it is the largest single item on your schedule and it dwarfs everything else in this book.

## Hormones and the two canaries

### For the man reading this

The arteries that produce an erection are roughly one to two millimeters across. The coronary arteries are three to four. The same process that ends in a heart attack — the softening of the single layer of cells lining every vessel you own — narrows the smallest vessels first, because they have the least margin. Which is why erectile difficulty frequently precedes overt cardiovascular symptoms, commonly by two to three years and sometimes longer [Montorsi et al., Eur. Urol. 2003; Thompson et al., JAMA 2005].

That is not an embarrassment. That is a stress test your own body ran and reported the results of, years early, for free. And the brain is on the same chassis — more vascular surface area per gram than any organ you own, the same endothelial function, the same inflammatory load, the same insulin resistance. Men who tell me their erections have softened over two years almost always tell me, in the next breath, about the word they could not find in a meeting. They believe those are two problems. They are one fire in two rooms. If you are reading this section on your husband's behalf rather than your own, you may have seen both rooms before he did.

### For the woman reading this

Your body files a different report, and it is quieter, which is why it gets misread for years. It arrives as a cluster over about eighteen months, and no single piece looks like a medical event. Sleep breaks first — asleep fine, awake at two or three, ninety minutes of lying there. Then the cognitive symptoms, which frighten women more than anything else on the list: word-finding lapses, a name gone for four seconds, walking into a room and losing the reason. Then a shorter fuse and a flatness where enthusiasm used to be. Then the same calories and the same walking, and the weight relocates to the middle. Then intimacy changes and nothing gets said, because there is no script for raising it in an eleven-minute appointment.

And roughly two-thirds of Alzheimer's cases in this country are in women [Alz. Assoc. 2026]. Longer life expectancy explains part of that gap and, on the honest reading, not all of it. The menopausal transition is the leading suspect, and the argument is not settled.

## Air and water

The environmental piece belongs here because it is the same shape: subtraction, and it does not hurt, which is why nobody does it. The EPA's 1989 Report to Congress measured indoor air as two to five times more polluted than outdoor air, and separate research puts the average American's time indoors at roughly eighty-seven percent [EPA 1989; Klepeis et al., JEAEE 2001]; fine particulate matter is one of the Commission's fourteen [Lancet Commission 2024]. A HEPA-plus-carbon filter in the bedroom and a countertop reverse-osmosis unit for the water is the floor. A few hundred dollars each, once. Cheap bets with plausible mechanisms on a risk no policy can lower are exactly the bets your whole career says you should be taking.

## What the blank line looks like

Leave this line blank and you keep filing claims against yourself, quietly, every week. The A1c drifts from 5.9 to 6.4 over three draws that each got called *borderline*. The blood pressure nobody rechecked stays where it was. The pour stays at ten a week. The canary — his or hers — gets explained away as stress, age, or a bad year, and the hormone picture underneath it never gets drawn. The bedroom air and the tap water stay exactly what they were. None of it costs you anything this year, which is precisely why it never gets fixed, and most of those items are on the Commission's list of things that move the arc in Chapter 3. This is the cheapest line on the schedule. Subtraction has no invoice. It only has a bill that arrives later.

# Chapter 11 — Motivate: The Face on the Other Side of the Table

<!-- voice:health -->

Everything so far has been an argument. If arguments worked, this chapter would not exist.

They do not. At six-fifteen on a Tuesday in March, a thing you are doing to avoid a statistic in 2044 loses to a warm bed. Every time. Not a character defect — the correct behavior of a nervous system that discounts the future, and it will beat your ledger in a straight fight for the rest of your life. So the fourth line item is the engineering that keeps the other three funded.

Three load-bearing components. A named person. A sentence about who you are. And fifteen minutes on a Sunday.

## The named person

Here is the question, and it is not the one you expect. Not *why are you doing this.* **Who are you doing this for?**

Name one. Your husband. Your wife. Your daughter. Your son. Your mother, who you are already watching. Or yourself, at seventy-eight, in specific detail — still the one people call when something has to be decided. One person. One face. Someone you can picture in a room.

There is a second reason for the question, which you have already read. Chapter 4 told you who actually pays for the uninsured decade. Not the one who gets the diagnosis — the one who does not: a 63% higher four-year mortality among strained spousal caregivers [Schulz & Beach, *JAMA* 1999], chronic conditions at nearly twice the rate of non-caregivers, a decade of coming last on their own list inside a set of decisions where every call was correct [Family Caregiver Alliance]. So when I ask who you are doing this for, I am not running an exercise on you. I am asking you to notice that you already know the answer, and that it is sitting one chair over.

There is also evidence that having an answer matters on its own. In a long-running cohort of older adults, people scoring high on a validated measure of purpose in life were roughly two and a half times less likely to develop Alzheimer's over the follow-up window than those scoring low, controlling for age, education, depression, and baseline cognition [Boyle et al., *Arch. Gen. Psychiatry* 2010]. I file that under physiology, not inspiration. It behaves epidemiologically like a modifiable risk factor — and it is the only line item that costs nothing.

## The sentence, and fifteen minutes on a Sunday

Once the face is named, write one sentence about who you are. Present tense. Not a goal. The distinction is not semantic. *I am trying to lift twice a week* is a behavior, and behaviors negotiate with you on bad days. *I am someone who lifts* is an identity, and missing it is then dissonant, and your brain hates dissonance enough to route you back.

That is where the line on the cover of *Begin with the End in Mind* actually lands, and it is not a slogan. **Don't lose your identity and your dignity while you still have a choice.** The ledger is about the money. This is about the choice, and the choice is exercised at the level of the sentence you use to describe yourself.

The fifteen minutes is one sitting, usually Sunday: what is on the calendar this week, what is getting cooked, where the two strength sessions actually go, written into the same calendar that runs everything else in your life.

That is the schedule of benefits. Four lines, ten hours, and a premium only you can pay, alongside everything your advisor has built. All that is left is signing it.

# Chapter 12 — Begin with the End in Mind {.after-part}

There is a paperback somewhere in your house with a tan cover and a cracked spine. You read it in 1989, on a plane, probably.
