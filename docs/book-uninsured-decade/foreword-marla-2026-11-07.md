# Foreword

In thirty years of practicing estate planning, probate, and guardianship law, I have been across the table from people at almost every stage of life; young parents naming guardians for their children, couples planning carefully for retirement, and adult children trying to help a parent who can no longer manage alone. Repeatedly, one question has quietly determined everything that followed: Does this person have the capacity to make their own decisions?

You should know two things about me before you read another word. Dr. TJ Mundheim is my brother-in-law, and I am a client of My4MLife. Weigh everything that I say here with both of those facts in mind. I would tell a client to do the same.

For a while I served as the Guardianship Auditor for Probate Court Number Two in Tarrant County, Texas, reviewing the accounts of people who could no longer manage their own affairs. That work reminds you what a plan is for. It also shows you the worst that can happen when there is not one. I do not say this to frighten anyone. Guardianship exists for a reason. It can protect a vulnerable person but, by design, it is a last resort. It is time consuming, expensive, and deeply personal business conducted in a courtroom. It can break families apart.

As a rule, we plan well for death, and we plan poorly for decline. Death is a clean legal event. It has a date and a certificate, and the documents do what they were drafted to do. Decline has no date. It arrives as a missed bill, a repeated story, a strange decision about money that everyone explains away. Every document that I draft rests on the assumption that the person signing it understands what they are signing. Lawyers call that “capacity.” When capacity is in question, the options narrow, the cost goes up, and a decision a family could once have made at a kitchen table may end up in front of a judge.

The title page of this book says, “Don’t lose your identity and your dignity while you still have a choice.” Capacity is, in the plainest legal sense, having a choice. Capacity is not an abstract legal concept in my office. It is the difference between signing your own will and having a court decide who will speak for you. It is the difference between choosing who will manage your finances or make your medical decisions if you cannot, and leaving your family to ask a judge, in a public guardianship proceeding, for permission to do what needs to be done. When capacity is intact, we can plan. When it has faded, we are often left to react—lovingly, and with the best of intentions—under constraints that no one would have chosen.

That is why a book about preventing cognitive decline matters to a lawyer—and why I was so glad when my brother-in-law, Dr. TJ, told me that he was writing one. His book, The Uninsured Decade, names the risk plainly: Cognitive decline is the one catastrophic expense in American retirement that no policy will fully cover. Medicare pays nothing toward memory care. If you are lucky enough to have long term care insurance, it still covers only part of the cost. A decade of care can run hundreds of thousands of dollars.

This risk does not check your net worth first. In a family with significant assets, I have seen it become a fight over who is in charge. In a family with modest means, it can become the question of whether the house survives the cost of care. The numbers change. The conversation I wish we had started five years earlier does not.

I also want to speak to the person I most often find in my office, because she is the reason this book reads the way it does. At least half the time, she is the wife (and I do not mean to exclude non-traditional couples of any kind here, I am just talking about what I see most often in my practice). She keeps the folder. She knows where the documents are, and she helps make the decisions. In many cases she ends up making all of them, because her husband is the one whose mind is failing. When I read Dr. TJ’s first draft, I told him it had to be written for her as much as for him, and he rewrote it. If you are the one holding the pen, this book is for you.

Dr. TJ writes in this book that every document in the fireproof box—the will, the powers of attorney, the directives—quietly assumes a version of you at seventy-eight who can still be the one in the room whose opinion settles the question. Sometimes that assumption fails. What Dr. TJ offers in place of that assumption is a framework he calls the 4Ms. Mind, Muscle, Mitigate, Motivate. The 4Ms are the framework. The MindSpan Score is the way to measure where you stand. I will leave the medicine to the clinicians; what I admire, as a lawyer, is the honesty of the arithmetic. He tells you plainly that the premium he proposes—roughly ten hours a month—does not break even on dementia costs alone, and he shows you the math. His point is that you do not insure a house because you expect it to burn. You buy the elimination of an outcome you cannot come back from. Unlike every policy in the folder, this one pays a dividend while you are alive to spend it: sleep, strength, energy, presence; the years between sixty-eight and seventy-eight that you were saving for, spent the way you meant to spend them.

In November, for National Alzheimer’s Disease Awareness Month, I wrote on LinkedIn that an Alzheimer’s diagnosis raises important questions about financial decisions, healthcare preferences, asset management, and future care, and that reviewing powers of attorney, healthcare directives, wills, trusts, and beneficiary designations early can help ensure a person’s wishes are clear and documented. I believe that, but my work can only go so far. I can prepare a family for the possibility of incapacity. I cannot lower the odds of it. That is the half of the problem this book takes on, and it is why I agreed to write these pages.

Lawyers who do this work tend to talk about prevention in legal terms: execute your documents while you are well; name the people you trust; tell them what you want; and do not wait for a crisis to force the conversation. All of that is essential, and I urge every reader to do it. Dr. TJ’s book sends you back to that work, too. In his final chapter he has you sit down with your attorney and check whether your powers of attorney and healthcare directives are current and signed, because, as he puts it, that is “the half with legal deadlines on it.” But legal planning protects your wishes only if we also take seriously the health of the mind that forms those wishes. Protecting cognitive health, for as long as we reasonably can, is another form of planning—perhaps the most fundamental form. It protects the person, not just the paperwork.

Estate planning and brain health are, at heart, the same conversation. Both ask: How do I preserve my independence? How do I remain the author of my own life for as long as possible? How do I spare the people that I love from having to guess (or litigate) what I would have wanted? A will, a power of attorney, and a medical directive are answers on paper. The habits and choices that help sustain memory, clarity, and judgment are answers in daily life. We need both.

There is also a matter of dignity. The clients that I remember most vividly are not only those in crisis. They are the men and women who came in while they were well, who thought ahead, who asked hard questions early, and who left with the quiet confidence that they had put their affairs, and their families, in order.

When Dr. TJ shared his purpose for this book with me, what struck me was its hopefulness and its refusal to oversell. He promises no cures and no guarantees. The odds are stated as odds. The numbers carry their sources, and the one number he will not give you is your own: that, he says, is yours to work out. This is not a book to read in fear of what might happen. It is a book to read in time, while there is still time to act, to learn, and to make deliberate choices about how you live. If it prompts you to take one step you have been putting off, whether that step concerns your health or your legal planning, it will have done something genuinely worthwhile.

I will add one more thing, and then I will stop. I am incorporating the 4M pillars into my own life. It is a work in progress. I am not doing that because he is family. I am working on it because I know what the alternative looks like both from my side of the desk and from watching my own father’s decline and eventual death due to Dementia with Lewy Bodies. It was a slow and brutal process that I would spare my children from witnessing, if possible.

So, I commend this book to you with both a professional and a personal word. Professionally, I have seen what is preserved when people plan early and what is lost when they wait too long. Personally, I am proud of Dr. TJ, my brother-in-law, a Fort Worth health-span educator with more than thirty years in clinical practice, for taking on a subject that touches every family (including our own) and for treating it with the seriousness and optimism it deserves.

Read this book with your spouse or partner, if you have one, then do both halves. Do the work it describes and call your attorney. If you do not have one, find one. Sign what needs signing while you still can. That, in my experience, is how a good future is protected: not all at once, and not in a crisis, but thoughtfully, in advance, by a person determined to decide for themselves.

**Marla Mundheim**
