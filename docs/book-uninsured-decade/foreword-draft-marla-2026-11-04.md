Before you read another word, you should know two things about me.

Dr. TJ Mundheim is my brother-in-law. And I am a client of My4MLife. Weigh everything I say here with both of those facts in mind. I would tell a client to do the same.

I am writing this anyway because of what I have seen from my side of the conference table.

I have practiced law in Texas for [more than twenty-six] years, most of them in estate planning. I have sat with families who had a great deal to protect and with families who had very little. [I have also served as a guardianship auditor for a Tarrant County probate court, reviewing the accounts of people who could no longer manage their own affairs.] That work teaches you what a plan is for. It also teaches you what happens when there isn't one.

Here is what those years have taught me: we plan well for death, and we plan poorly for decline.

Death is a clean legal event. It has a date and a certificate, and the documents do what they were drafted to do. Decline has no date. It arrives as a missed bill, a repeated story, a strange decision about money that everyone explains away. And every document I draft rests on one assumption: that the person signing it understands what they are signing. Lawyers call that capacity. When capacity is in question, the options narrow, the cost goes up, and a decision a family could once have made at a kitchen table may end up in front of a judge.

The title page of this book says, "Don't lose your identity and your dignity while you still have a choice." I read that line as a lawyer. Capacity is, in the plainest legal sense, having a choice.

This risk does not check your net worth first. In a family with significant assets, I have seen it become a fight over who is in charge. In a family with modest means, it can become the question of whether the house survives the cost of care. The numbers change. The conversation I wish we had started five years earlier does not.

I also want to speak to the person I most often find in my office, because she is the reason this book reads the way it does. At least half the time, she is the wife. She keeps the folder. She knows where the documents are, and she helps make the decisions. In many cases she ends up making all of them, because her husband is the one whose mind is failing. When I read Dr. TJ's first draft, I told him it had to be written for her as much as for him, and he rewrote it. If you are the one holding the pen, this book is for you.

[I recently wrote on LinkedIn that Alzheimer's belongs in the estate planning conversation for every family. Marla: confirm or reword to match your post.] I believe that. But my work can only go so far. I can prepare a family for the possibility of incapacity. I cannot lower the odds of it. That is the half of the problem this book takes on, and it is why I agreed to write these pages.

I am not a physician, and I cannot tell you this program will prevent anything. Neither will Dr. TJ, and that is a large part of why I trust the book. He shows his arithmetic, including the places where it comes up short. He states odds as odds. And he does not ask you to fire anyone. He sends you back to your attorney, your advisor and your CPA, because that work still has to be done, and much of it has deadlines you will not see coming.

I will add one more thing, and then I will stop. I have built most of the 4M pillars into my own life. I did not do that because he is family. I did it because I know what the alternative looks like from my side of the desk. What I do about my own health is my business, as yours should be, so I will leave it there.

Read this book with your spouse or partner, if you have one. Then do both halves. Do the work it describes, and call your attorney. If you do not have one, find one. Sign what needs signing while you still can.

**Marla Morgan Mundheim**
