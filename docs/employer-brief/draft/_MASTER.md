# 1. Cognitive Performance Is Job Performance

## The claim, stated plainly

The thing you are buying when you hire someone is cognitive output. Not hours.
Not attendance. The capacity to think well, repeatedly, under load.

This is not a rhetorical flourish — it is one of the most replicated findings in
the study of work. Decades of meta-analysis have established general cognitive
ability as **among the strongest predictors of job performance we have ever
measured**, across essentially every job studied.

I want to be careful here, because this literature has been oversold for years
and you may have heard inflated versions of it. The classic estimate put the
correlation around 0.51 overall and 0.58 for complex professional, managerial and
technical work. A significant 2022 reanalysis revised those figures downward
under more conservative statistical corrections, and found that structured
interviews may predict performance slightly better.

So I will not tell you cognitive ability is *the* single best predictor. I will
tell you it is **among the best**, that the finding has survived a hostile
reanalysis by serious people, and — most relevant to you — that **its predictive
power is greatest in exactly the complex, senior, judgment-heavy roles that carry
your company.** The more a job requires thinking, the more thinking predicts the
outcome.

That is the whole argument in one sentence: **the higher the value of the role,
the more of it is brain.**

## What makes a brain perform worse this week

Cognitive ability is not fixed. It is a daily output of a biological system, and
that system is being actively degraded in most of your workforce by four things.

### Sleep

This is the most under-appreciated performance variable in American business, and
the data on it is genuinely alarming.

In a landmark study, researchers measured people's cognitive and motor performance
after sustained wakefulness and compared it against the same people dosed with
alcohol. **After 17 to 19 hours awake, performance was equivalent to or worse than
a blood alcohol concentration of 0.05%.** At around 24 hours, it matched **0.10%**
— above the legal limit to drive in every state. Response speeds were up to fifty
percent slower.

Read that again with your own organization in mind. The executive who was up at
5:00 a.m., flew in the afternoon, and is negotiating at 9:00 p.m. is, by any
functional measure of judgment, impaired. You would never let him sign that
contract after three drinks. You let him sign it after nineteen hours awake
without a second thought.

And this is not confined to travel weeks. About twenty-three percent of American
workers meet criteria for insomnia. The measured cost is roughly **eleven days of
lost productivity per affected worker per year**, and the researchers found that
almost none of it was absence. It was presenteeism — the person came in, sat down,
and produced badly.

Fatigue also breaks things. The National Safety Council attributes **thirteen
percent of workplace injuries** to fatigue, and finds workers with sleep problems
carry roughly **1.6 times the injury risk**.

### Metabolic health

Body composition is a brain variable, not just a cardiac one.

Midlife obesity is associated with roughly **double the relative risk of dementia**
later in life. More striking for your purposes: in cognitively normal adults in
midlife — people with no symptoms, people currently on your payroll performing
fine — higher visceral fat is associated with more amyloid pathology and thinner
cortex in the regions Alzheimer's attacks first.

The mechanism is not mysterious. Visceral fat is metabolically active tissue that
drives systemic inflammation and insulin resistance, and the brain is exquisitely
sensitive to both. This is why the same person who cannot lose the belly also
cannot hold a thought at 3 p.m.

Diabetes carries **$12,022 per year in excess medical spending** per affected
person, and the American Diabetes Association's accounting attributes **$35.8
billion** of the national cost to presenteeism specifically. The disease reaches
your P&L twice: once in claims, once in output.

### Strength

Resistance training improves cognition. Meta-analyses in adults over fifty
consistently find improvements in global cognition and memory domains, with
working memory showing among the clearest effects. The mechanism runs through
BDNF — a protein that supports the growth and maintenance of neurons.

I will be straight about the limits: effects on processing speed and attention
have been less consistent across studies. The effect sizes are real but moderate.
Lifting weights is not a nootropic. It is, however, one of very few interventions
that improves cognition, body composition, injury resistance, insulin sensitivity
and mood simultaneously — and your workforce has almost entirely stopped doing it.

### The gut, and why it belongs in a business document

This is the part that sounds least like a corporate conversation, so let me
justify it.

The gut manufactures a large share of the neurochemistry the brain runs on and
communicates with the brain continuously through the vagus nerve, immune
signaling and microbial metabolites. Disruption of that system is associated with
depression, anxiety, impulsivity, and cognitive impairment.

It is also, in plain accounting terms, expensive. Irritable bowel syndrome alone
runs over **$30 billion a year** in the United States. At least a third of
affected employees miss about a day a month, and report **fifteen to twenty-one
percent greater loss of work productivity** than colleagues without it. Employer
spending runs about **$14,000 a year** per employee with a chronic functional GI
disorder, and over **$62,000** for Crohn's disease.

Gut repair is where our protocol begins. Not because it is fashionable, but
because it is upstream of mood, sleep, and inflammation — and because it is one of
the fastest places to give someone a visible win in the first three weeks, which
is what makes them stay.

## The evidence that this is actually fixable

Everything above establishes a problem. This next study establishes that
something can be done about it, and it is the single most important citation in
this brief.

**U.S. POINTER**, published in *JAMA* in 2025, was a two-year randomized trial
across five sites with **2,111 participants** aged 60 to 79 who were sedentary,
eating poorly, and carrying elevated risk for cognitive decline.

Two arms. Both received lifestyle intervention. One was **self-guided** — given
the information and encouraged to act on it. The other was **structured** —
prescribed activity with measurable goals, thirty-eight facilitated peer team
meetings over two years, regular review of health metrics, and goal-setting with
a clinician.

Both groups improved. **The structured group improved significantly more**,
protecting cognition against normal age-related decline for the full two years.

I want to be precise about what that means for you, because it is the difference
between this program and the one you already bought.

**The information was never the missing ingredient. The structure was.**

Handing someone a portal, a pamphlet, or a health questionnaire is the
self-guided arm. It produces the smaller effect. What produced the larger effect
was a prescribed protocol, measurable goals, a clinician in the loop, and — this
is the part I want you to notice — **a peer group that met, repeatedly, for two
years.**

That is a cohort. A group of people doing the same hard thing on the same
schedule, who notice when one of them disappears.

You already have that. It is called your company. It is the one asset a
direct-to-consumer health program can never manufacture and you already own it
outright.

Put those together. A growing share of your workforce — and disproportionately

# 3. Why This Costs You Nothing

## First, the case against my own industry

I would rather you hear this from me than from a board member who reads it later.

The most-cited statistic in corporate wellness is that programs return **$3.27 in
medical savings and $2.73 in absenteeism savings per dollar spent** — from a 2010
Harvard meta-analysis. You have probably been shown that number by a vendor. It is
essentially dead as a causal claim, and its own lead author has publicly walked it
back.

Here is what happened. That meta-analysis pooled observational studies of
self-selected employees at large companies. When researchers ran proper randomized
trials, the effect vanished:

- **The BJ's Wholesale Club trial** (*JAMA*, 2019): 32,974 employees, 160
  worksites, eighteen months. Result: modest improvement in two *self-reported*
  behaviors, and **no significant effect on clinical biometrics, healthcare
  spending, absenteeism, tenure, or job performance.** A three-year follow-up
  found the same nothing, which kills the "you didn't wait long enough" defense.
- **The Illinois Workplace Wellness Study** (*Quarterly Journal of Economics*,
  2019): 4,834 employees, randomized, with incentives from $50 to $350. **No
  causal effect** on spending, health behaviors, biometrics, or productivity at
  12, 24, or 30 months. Their confidence intervals ruled out roughly
  **eighty-four percent** of previously published estimates.

And they identified the mechanism behind the false positive, which is the most
useful finding in the whole literature. Employees who *chose* to join wellness
programs were **already spending about $1,384 less per year** on medical care
before the program existed. Observational studies measured *who signs up*, and
credited the program.

So if you have quietly suspected your wellness vendor's ROI deck was nonsense:
you were right, and there is peer-reviewed evidence you were right.

## What actually survived

Now the part the skeptical coverage usually omits.

What failed in those trials was a **specific product type**: education, health
risk assessments, coaching modules, step challenges, gamification. Neither trial
tested clinical intervention. Nobody was treated for anything.

When you look at programs that *do* clinical work, the picture inverts. RAND's
seven-year study of PepsiCo's program across roughly 67,000 employees separated
the two components and found:

- **Disease management: $3.78 returned per dollar**, driven by a **29% reduction
  in hospital admissions**.
- **Lifestyle management: $0.50 per dollar.**

RAND's broader work for the Department of Labor found roughly **eighty-seven
percent of measurable savings** came from disease management, despite disease
management involving only about thirteen percent of participants.

So the honest synthesis is this:

> **Wellness programs don't fail because wellness doesn't work. They fail because
> employers pay clinical prices for non-clinical products.**

The typical wellness platform charges between **two and ten dollars per employee
per month**. A CoreHealth-class deployment for a thousand-employee company runs
roughly **$45,000 to $65,000 a year**. Eighty-three percent of large firms and
fifty-six percent of small firms already have something like this.

You are, in all likelihood, already paying clinical prices for a non-clinical
product right now.

# A Note from the Author

I spent thirty years in clinical practice working with thousands of patients, and
the pattern that eventually took over my thinking was this: almost nobody arrives
early. People come in once something has already broken — the back, the knee, the
marriage, the memory — and by then the range of what can be done has narrowed
considerably.

I wrote the original edition of *Begin with the End in Mind* for men, because men
are historically terrible at prevention and I wanted to write something that would
actually reach them. But ninety-nine percent of it applies to everyone, and once I
watched what happened to the families around the men who did the work, it was
obvious it had never belonged to one audience.

Then it became obvious it did not belong to individuals either. The thing that
makes this protocol succeed is structure and company — a schedule, a measurement,
and other people doing it alongside you. The best randomized evidence we have says
exactly that.

Companies already have all three. That is why I am writing to you.
