**The longer version, for reference only (175 words, does not fit):**

> **Your gut hears the chew.** Chewing isn't just for your mouth. When you chew real food, your brain sends word down the vagus nerve, the long line between brain and gut, that a meal is on the way. Researchers test this with sham feeding: chew, spit, never swallow. Gut hormones and insulin start moving anyway. Your stomach gets the memo before the food arrives.
>
> Your gut bacteria may notice too. Smaller pieces give them more surface to work on, and lab work suggests the size of the pieces changes what they make. But in one trial, people who chewed more thoroughly for a week saw no change in particle size in their stool. Early days.
>
> And the long view: large studies that followed thousands of people for years found that those who had trouble chewing were more likely to have memory and thinking slip later. That's a link, not proof, and it's about being able to chew, not about counting chews. Still, it's a reminder: the gut and the mind are on the same line.

## 3. The Science, Briefly: new paragraph under "The chew (Chapter 2)"

**Where:** a second paragraph under "### The chew (Chapter 2)". It goes after the paragraph ending "...not a miracle." and before line 932 ("### The space (Chapter 3)").

**New text (147 words):**

> The gut notes in Chapter 2 rest on smaller, earlier studies. When 12 adults chewed real food and spat it out (sham feeding), a marker of vagus-nerve signaling rose; chewing gum didn't do it. At Iowa State, sham feeding shifted insulin and the gut hormone ghrelin in 10 men within minutes. In a Duke trial, 41 volunteers were asked to chew each bite 30 to 40 times for five days; stool particle size didn't change. In the same paper's lab work, smaller food particles changed what gut bacteria made. A Korean study followed 7,568 adults for 10 years; those who found hard foods uncomfortable to chew had about 28 percent higher odds of cognitive impairment. In Singapore, 973 older adults followed for six years had about 43 percent higher odds if chewing was hard. Both rest on self-report, and the authors warn against reading cause and effect.

The bioRxiv preprint is not cited. The lab finding above comes from the published ISME J paper's own in-vitro work (smaller wheat-bran particles gave more short-chain fatty acids), and it's given without a number.

## Needs your judgment

1. **Chapter 2 is 56 words, not 120–170.** The gut-bacteria point appears only in the Science section. If you want the fuller version in Chapter 2, something already there has to shrink by about 12 lines.
2. **"Big studies" vs. the evidence.** The Korean result is modest and borderline: OR 1.28, with a confidence interval that starts at 1.00. Both cohorts rely on people's own reports of chewing trouble. The text calls it "a link, not proof," which I think is the honest ceiling.
3. **Age wording.** The Korean study enrolled middle-aged as well as older adults, so the Science paragraph says "7,568 adults" for that study and "older adults" only for Singapore. No ages are printed.
4. **"Sends word down the vagus nerve."** This is supported as a brain-to-gut signal set off by chewing real food. Chewing gum didn't do it in Teff's study. Nothing here supports a chew count switching on a gut-vagus "loop." That's why the text never ties the vagus to 32.
5. **Sources labels.** The new entries are labeled "The gut." and "The mind." rather than "The chew.", which fits better and saves a line. If you want every label to read "The chew.", the Sources run 1 line over.
