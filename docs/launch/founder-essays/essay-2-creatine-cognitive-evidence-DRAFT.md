## The marketing problem with creatine

Creatine has an image problem. It got branded as a meathead supplement in the 1990s and never recovered. To this day, a 55-year-old man hears "creatine" and pictures a 240-pound bodybuilder in a Gold's Gym, not a randomized controlled trial in vegetarians.

That is a marketing problem, not a science problem. The cognitive literature has been quietly piling up for twenty years, and in the last two years it crossed a threshold that almost nobody in the men's-health space has caught up to.

The premium nootropic industry, which sells $80-a-month proprietary blends with a dozen ingredients you've never heard of, would very much prefer you didn't know any of this.

## What the working-memory data actually shows

The cleanest cognitive trial on creatine ran in 2003. Rae and colleagues, published in *Proceedings of the Royal Society B*. Randomized, placebo-controlled, double-blind. Adults given 5 grams a day of creatine monohydrate for six weeks. Outcome: significantly improved working memory and intelligence test performance vs. placebo.

The subjects were vegetarians, which is the design detail that makes the study elegant. Vegetarians have lower baseline brain creatine stores, so the effect size was larger. Subsequent studies in omnivores show a smaller but real effect.

I have shown this study to more than a hundred patients over the years. The reaction is always the same — "Wait, why has nobody told me this?"

The answer is that there is no margin in telling you. You can buy a tub of micronized creatine monohydrate for $25 that lasts five months. There is no subscription, no proprietary blend, no influencer payout. The science is solid and the economics are terrible — for everyone except the man taking it.

## Sleep-deprived cognitive resilience

The newer finding is even more interesting for the demographic I see in my practice.

In 2024, Gordji-Nejad and colleagues published in *Scientific Reports* — a single 0.35-gram-per-kilogram dose of creatine, given before 21 hours of sleep deprivation, **reversed the cognitive decline normally seen under that condition**. The mechanism is straightforward: under metabolic stress, the brain burns through ATP faster than it can regenerate. Creatine acts as a phosphate buffer, donating to ADP to keep ATP available where it's needed.

If you are a man in his fifties or sixties flying redeyes, sleeping six hours, running a business, watching grandkids, or just doing the daily work of a long life — this is not a small finding. This is "your brain can be biochemically reinforced against the conditions you are already living under."

The premium-nootropic stack with no creatine in it has none of this evidence behind it. Most of those formulas have proprietary blends so dosed-down on the active ingredients that even if the ingredients worked, you would not get a clinical effect.

## The aging brain runs low on phosphocreatine

Brain phosphocreatine concentrations decline with age. This is documented across the imaging literature. The decline tracks loosely with the cognitive aging arc we all see clinically — sharper at 35, foggier at 65, sometimes lost at 80.

Brain creatine uptake is dose-dependent. The Avgerinos 2018 systematic review found cognitive benefit in older adults at 5 grams a day. The Chilibeck 2017 meta-analysis showed creatine plus resistance training improved lean mass and cognitive function in adults over 50.

These are the men I see in my practice every week. The same protocol — 5 grams of creatine a day plus an intelligent training program — addresses three things at once: sarcopenia, metabolic health, and cognitive performance. One supplement. Three downstream wins. No proprietary blend.
