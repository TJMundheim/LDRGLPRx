## PART A — The structural change (REVISED 2026-08-05 per TJ)

> **⚠️ SUPERSEDES the first version of this section.** The original spec bound
> the business case into the front of the employee's book. **TJ killed that:**

> instinct is correct and it is empirically supported — see A2.

### A1. THREE artifacts, not two

The business case and the protocol are **two different books for two different
buyers**, and they must not be bound together.

| Artifact | Audience | Contains the money numbers? | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Men's edition** (shipping today, unchanged) | Consumer men | No | `_MASTER.md`, `--edition mens` |
| **2. Workplace edition** — the employee's book | Enrolled employees (everyone) | **NEVER** | `_MASTER.md`, `--edition workplace` |
| **3. The Employer Brief** — NEW, separate | The buyer only (owner/CEO/HR) | Yes — it's the whole point | New short source, `docs/employer-brief/` |

The Employer Brief is a slim standalone piece (~20–30 pp, or a 12-page
saddle-stitched booklet) that never reaches an employee's hands. It carries
Parts B, C and D of this document. The employee's book carries none of it.

### A2. Why this is right (and it's not just taste)

**The research on this is unusually direct.** Nishii, Lepak & Schneider
(*Personnel Psychology*, 2008; 1,300+ citations) showed that employees make
attributions about *why* management adopted an HR practice, and those
attributions — not the practice itself — drive commitment and satisfaction.
Attributions to employee wellbeing produce commitment; attributions to cost
reduction and exploitation do not. The effects aggregate to the unit level and
show up in organizational citizenship behavior and even customer satisfaction.
https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1744-6570.2008.00121.x

In other words: **the same program produces opposite results depending on what
the employee believes motivated it.** Handing an employee the ROI chapter is
handing them the cost-reduction attribution in writing. It would actively
destroy the retention and culture effect that is the strongest part of the
employer's own business case — the pitch would eat itself.

Three more reasons the split is correct:

1. **The numbers are dehumanizing when read by their subject.** "$16,854 per
   affected adult," "1,685 per employee," "$12,022 in excess medical spend" —
   written *about* a person, these read as *you are a depreciating asset*. The
   whole protocol runs on the accountability target (your wife, your kids,
   your own last decade). Nothing kills that faster than being priced.
2. **Different persuasion jobs.** The employer needs P&L. The employee needs
   "this is yours, and it's about your life." Neither argument helps the other
   audience; each one weakens the other.
3. **Different shelf lives.** The employer numbers go stale annually (KFF,
   Mercer/Aon/WTW all refresh every year). Binding them into the employee's
   book forces a reprint of a 260-page book to update a premium figure. Kept
   separate, the Brief is a cheap annual refresh and the employee's book is
   evergreen.

## PART B — Page One of the EMPLOYER BRIEF: "The Punchline" (draft)

*(Buyer-only. Never appears in the employee's book — see Part A.)*

> ### Everything in this book, on one page.
>
> **What your people's brains are costing you right now**
> - You pay about **$20,000 a year per employee** for family health coverage — and it's rising faster than at any point in 15 years. *(KFF 2025; Mercer/Aon/WTW 2026 projections)*
> - **90% of American health spending** goes to people with chronic and mental-health conditions. *(CDC)*
> - Poor employee health costs US employers roughly **$575 billion a year** and **1.5 billion lost workdays**. *(Integrated Benefits Institute)*
> - Most of that loss isn't absence. It's **presenteeism** — the person is at their desk, and not all of them showed up.
>
> **The part nobody puts on the balance sheet**
> - Measurable cognitive decline begins at **age 45** — not 65. Your most expensive, most senior people are already in it. *(Whitehall II, BMJ)*
> - Nearly **1 in 4 US workers is now 55 or older** — the fastest-growing segment of the workforce for two decades. *(Census Bureau)*
> - After 17–19 hours awake, a person performs like someone **legally too drunk to drive**. *(Dawson & Reid, Nature)*
> - An employee with insomnia loses about **11 days of productivity a year**. Nearly all of it while present. *(Harvard/Kessler)*
> - General cognitive ability is among the **strongest known predictors of job performance** — and the effect is largest in exactly the complex, managerial roles you can least afford to lose. *(Schmidt & Hunter; Sackett et al. 2022)*
>
> **What actually moves it**
> - In 2025, a major randomized trial (**US POINTER**, 2,111 participants) showed a **structured** lifestyle program improved cognition in at-risk older adults — and beat the self-guided version. Structure is the active ingredient. *(JAMA / Alzheimer's Association)*
> - The four levers are unglamorous and well-evidenced: **sleep, metabolic health, strength, and purpose.** This book is the protocol.
>
> **Why the last wellness program failed you — and why this one is built differently**
> - The famous "wellness saves $3 for every $1" claim did not survive randomized testing. Two large trials found **no effect** on spending, biometrics, or absenteeism. *(Song & Baicker, JAMA 2019/2021; Illinois Workplace Wellness Study, QJE 2019)*
> - But what failed was a **product type**: education, health-risk questionnaires, and step challenges sold at $3–10 per employee per month.
> - Clinically anchored programs are the category that survived: disease management returned **$3.78 per $1** while lifestyle-education returned **$0.50**. *(RAND/PepsiCo)*
> - **Wellness programs don't fail because wellness doesn't work. They fail because employers pay clinical prices for non-clinical products.**
>
> **What this costs you**
> - The platform: **nothing.** No per-employee fee, no procurement cycle, no contract minimum.
> - What you provide: **permission, a little time, and a small incentive.** Incentives raise participation by roughly **20 points** — and the research is clear that **small ones do nearly all the work**; above ~$100–200 the returns flatten. *(RAND)*
> - What you get back: a workforce that sleeps, thinks, and decides better — and a **quarterly report showing the score move**, in aggregate, never by name.
>
> **And the part that isn't on the balance sheet at all**
> - Replacing an employee costs **33% to 200% of their salary**. *(Work Institute; Gallup)*
> - This program doesn't stay at work. It goes home — to their weight, their marriage, their energy with their kids, their odds of knowing their grandchildren's names.
> - **People do not forget who gave them that.**

*Design note:* this page must be typographically calm — no boxes, no icons, no
sales-deck styling. It's a page of facts. The restraint is the credibility.

## PART C — "A Letter to the Employer" (the thesis, per TJ)

TJ's framing, verbatim intent: *this book was written for employers as a way to
help employees improve not just job performance but their entire life — and the
carryover into their personal life is exactly why it works at work, because the
resources and support came from you.*

Argument spine for the letter:

1. **You are not being asked to buy a wellness program.** You're being asked to
   open a door you don't have to pay for.
2. **The asset you employ is a brain.** You pay for judgment, memory,
   pattern-recognition, and emotional regulation. Every one of those is
   biological, measurable, and currently declining in your most senior people.
3. **The decline starts at 45, not 65** — mid-career, mid-mortgage,
   mid-leadership. You are watching it right now and calling it stress, or
   burnout, or "he's lost a step."
4. **The carryover IS the mechanism, not a bonus.** A program confined to the
   office fails, because the behaviors that build a brain happen at 6am, at
   dinner, on a Saturday. When it works, it works everywhere — and the employee
   knows precisely where it came from. Research on perceived organizational
   support consistently links this kind of support to lower work-family
   conflict, lower burnout, and lower turnover intention.
5. **That's the retention argument** and it's the one with the firmest
   evidence behind it — firmer than any claims-savings promise anyone has ever
   made you.
6. **The origin story** (absorbing the old disclaimer line): *"I wrote the
   original edition to motivate men, because men are historically terrible at
   prevention. But 99% of it applies to everyone — and once I watched what it
   did to the people around the men who did it, I understood it never belonged
   to just one audience. This edition is for your whole company."*

## PART D — Research appendix (the numbers, with sources)

### D1. Cognitive performance is job performance

- **General cognitive ability = a top-tier predictor of job performance.**
  Schmidt & Hunter (1998) put operational validity at r = .51 overall and
  **r = .58 for high-complexity (professional/managerial/technical) jobs**.
  Sackett et al. (2022) revised the estimate down to ~r = .31 under more
  conservative corrections, and found structured interviews (~.42) may edge it
  out. **Book framing: "among the strongest predictors," never "the single
  strongest"** — the honest version survives a skeptic, and the complexity
  gradient is the point for a leadership audience.
  https://gwern.net/doc/iq/ses/2023-sackett.pdf ·
  https://www.siop.org/tip-article/is-cognitive-ability-the-best-predictor-of-job-performance-new-research-says-its-time-to-think-again/
- **Sleep deprivation ≈ legal intoxication.** 17–19 hours awake produces
  impairment equal to or worse than **BAC 0.05%**; ~24 hours ≈ **BAC 0.10%**;
  response speeds up to 50% slower. Dawson & Reid, *Nature* (1997); replicated
  across multiple groups (17–24h → 0.05–0.10% BAC equivalence).
  https://www.nature.com/articles/40775
- **Structured lifestyle intervention improves cognition — RCT-grade, 2025.**
  US POINTER: 2,111 adults 60–79 at risk, 2 years, 5 sites. Both arms improved;
  the **structured** arm improved significantly more than self-guided. Published
  *JAMA*, announced AAIC 2025. **This is the single most important citation in
  the book** — it validates "structure + accountability," which is exactly what
  a company cohort provides and a pamphlet doesn't.
  https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40720610/ · https://www.alz.org/us-pointer/study-results
- **Resistance training improves cognition.** Meta-analyses in adults 50+:
  improved global cognition and memory domains (SMD ~0.13–0.22 overall;
  working memory SMD 0.44), mechanism via BDNF/synaptic plasticity. Effects on
  processing speed/attention were not consistently significant — state it
  honestly. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12772445/ ·
  https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12289702/
- **Midlife obesity → dementia risk.** Relative risk of dementia ~**2.04**
  (obese) and **1.64** (overweight) in midlife vs normal weight. Midlife
  visceral adiposity associates with higher amyloid pathology and lower
  cortical thickness in AD-signature regions in *cognitively normal* adults.
  Mechanism: IL-6/insulin resistance/systemic inflammation.
  https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4237034/ ·
  https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11272197/
- **Gut-brain axis.** Microbiome modulates serotonin, dopamine, GABA and
  glutamate pathways plus immune/endocrine axes; dysbiosis associates with
  depression, anxiety, impulsivity and cognitive decline. Business-audience
  translation exists in memory `project_gut_brain_axis_canonical` — reuse that
  copy block. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12007925/
- **GI disease is an employer line item.** IBS: >$30B/year US; ≥1/3 of IBS
  employees miss ~1 day/month and report **15–21% greater work-productivity
  loss**. Employer spend runs ~$14,000/yr per employee with a chronic
  functional GI disorder to >$62,000 for Crohn's.
  https://www.ajmc.com/view/apr05-2021ps07-s16 ·
  https://oshihealth.com/gastrointestinal-diseases-in-america-the-costly-impact-on-employers-and-patients/
- **Perceived organizational support → lower work-family conflict, lower
  burnout, lower turnover intention**; support spills over into the family role.
  This is the peer-reviewed backing for TJ's carryover thesis.
  https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3116443/ ·
  https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9361864/
- **Brain health is an emerging benefits category (2025–26)** — benefits-trend
  reports now name brain health, metabolic regulation and stress resilience as
  determinants of sustained performance across longer careers. We are early,
  not fringe. https://www.marshmma.com/us/insights/details/employee-health-and-benefits-trends.html

### D3. Why this program is built differently (the ROI chapter)

- **The famous number is dead as a causal claim.** Baicker, Cutler & Song
  (*Health Affairs* 2010) — $3.27 medical + $2.73 absenteeism per $1 — was a
  meta-analysis of self-selected observational studies. Baicker has publicly
  walked it back. https://www.healthaffairs.org/doi/10.1377/hlthaff.2009.0626
- **The RCTs that killed it.** BJ's Wholesale (Song & Baicker, *JAMA* 2019;
  32,974 employees, 160 worksites): modest self-reported behavior gains
  (exercise +8.3 pp, weight management +13.6 pp), **no effect on biometrics,
  spending, absenteeism, tenure, or job performance** — and still nothing at
  **3 years** (*Health Affairs* 2021). Illinois Workplace Wellness Study
  (Jones/Molitor/Reif, *QJE* 2019; 4,834 employees): **no causal effect** on
  spending, behaviors, biometrics, or productivity; CIs rule out ~84% of prior
  published estimates. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30990549/ ·
  https://www.nber.org/papers/w24229
- **The mechanism of the false positive is selection**: Illinois found wellness
  joiners already spent **$1,384/yr less** on medical care *before* the program
  existed. Observational ROI measures who signs up, not what the program does.
- **What survived: clinical intervention.** RAND/PepsiCo (*Health Affairs*
  2014, ~67,000 employees, 7 years): **disease management $3.78 per $1**
  (driven by a **29% drop in hospital admissions**) vs **lifestyle management
  $0.50 per $1**. RAND's DOL/HHS study found ~87% of savings came from disease
  management despite ~13% of participants.
  https://www.rand.org/news/press/2014/01/06/index1.html
- **Incentives: participation lever, not a savings lever.** ~**+20 percentage
  points**; rewards >$100 → 51% participation vs 36% for smaller; typical
  ~$200/employee; **diminishing returns above that**, and a well-designed
  program hits 59% nearly matching what incentives buy. Loss-framing beats
  equivalent rewards. RAND RB-9842 (2014).
  https://www.rand.org/pubs/research_briefs/RB9842.html
  → **This is the empirical backing for TJ's "small incentives, tremendous
  ROI" instinct — and it says small is not a compromise, it's optimal.**
- **Retention is the firmest ground.** Replacement cost **33% of salary**
  (Work Institute) to **50–200%** (Gallup/SHRM). Gallup: burnout-driven
  turnover + lost productivity ≈ **$322B/yr globally**; disengagement ≈ **$8.9T
  (~9% of global GDP)**. MetLife EBTS: 70% say customizable benefits would
  increase loyalty; 82% say benefits give a greater sense of stability; and in
  the 2026 edition employers name **healthcare-cost control their #1 HR
  objective** for the first time since 2022. ⚠️ trace the SHRM "1 in 3 left
  over wellness benefits" and Aflac "87%" stats to primary before print.
  https://www.metlife.com/workforce-insights/employee-benefit-trends/
- **What the incumbents charge (the wedge, in their own numbers):**
  wellness platforms run **$2–$10+ PEPM**; CoreHealth ≈ **$45K–$65K/yr for
  1,000 employees ($3.75–$5.40 PEPM)**. **83% of large firms / 56% of small
  firms** already offer wellness programs (KFF 2024/25) — meaning the buyer
  almost always already has one, and probably already suspects it isn't
  working. https://pricingnow.com/question/corehealth-pricing/

## PART E — Prevention / screening services layer (TJ raised 2026-08-05)

TJ's question: add ConnectedMind mental-health screening for everyone (results
carried to their own doctor), plus **PGx testing** (how an individual
metabolizes medications — affects mental, physical and cognitive outcomes),
plus other early screening. Open question: **what's the benefit, and who pays —
employer, employee, or their health insurance as a preventive benefit?**

### E1. Why this fits the employer edition specifically

Screening is the **highest-value, lowest-friction thing an employer can hand an
employee**, and it converts the book's argument into a first action. It also
sits on the right side of the ROI literature: what survived the RCTs was
*finding and treating actual conditions*, not educating well people. Screening
is the front door of exactly that. And the one durable positive finding in the
Illinois RCT was **more employees attached to a primary care physician** —
which is precisely the ConnectedMind "take these results to your own doctor"
motion.

### E2. Who pays — the honest read

There are four possible payers, and they are not equally available:

1. **Employee's health insurance (preventive benefit).** Under ACA §2713,
   non-grandfathered plans must cover USPSTF A/B-rated preventive services with
   **no cost sharing** — and **depression screening is on that list** (the
   Supreme Court reaffirmed the A/B requirement in 2025). So a *screening
   instrument* delivered in a covered clinical encounter is often already paid
   for. This is a **selling point, not a revenue line** for us: "your plan
   already covers the follow-up; we're removing the reason people never start it."
   https://www.kff.org/womens-health-policy/preventive-services-covered-by-private-health-plans/
2. **Employee out-of-pocket, HSA/FSA-eligible.** Screening and lab tests are
   generally HSA/FSA/HRA eligible when medically appropriate. **This is the
   realistic payer for PGx and advanced panels** and it fits our existing
   direct-to-employee commerce rail with zero new infrastructure. Labcorp
   OnDemand and Function Health both already run this exact model — precedent
   is established and consumer-familiar.
3. **Employer pays directly.** Possible, but it's the option that **breaks the
   $0-PEPM wedge** — and note that employer-paid biometric screening can pull a
   wellness program into ERISA group-health-plan territory, which is exactly
   the procurement complexity our pitch promises to avoid. **Recommendation:
   keep the employer's only cost "permission, time, and a small incentive."**
   If an employer *wants* to fund screening, take it as an upsell — never as
   the default architecture.
4. **Us, as a loss leader.** The assessment already is one. Don't add paid lab
   cost to it.

**PGx specifically — the reimbursement reality is rough.** There is only one
national Medicare coverage determination (warfarin, 2009); everything else runs
through local coverage determinations, and commercial coverage is highly
variable — MolDX covers all 65 clinically relevant drug-gene pairs, while
**8 of 12 major payer policies reviewed covered 10 or fewer**. Overall PGx
claims reimbursement rate: **~46%**.
https://www.ajmc.com/view/medical-policy-determinations-for-pharmacogenetic-tests-among-us-health-plans ·
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10461057/

**Conclusion: do not build PGx as an insurance-billed service.** Roughly half
of claims don't get paid, and chasing that is a billing operation we don't have
and don't want. **Build it as a cash-pay, HSA/FSA-eligible add-on** priced
transparently — which is how the successful consumer testing companies already
do it, and which fits our existing Stripe rail exactly.

### E3. Recommended structure — a three-tier screening ladder

| Tier | What | Who pays | Role |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Free** | MindSpan 20-question assessment + **ConnectedMind mental-health screen**, results to the employee, "take this to your doctor" | Nobody (our cost, already built) | The door. Drives enrollment, generates the aggregate HR report, attaches people to a PCP. |
| **Covered** | Anything USPSTF A/B — depression screening, diabetes, BP, lipids, obesity counseling | Employee's existing insurance, $0 cost-share | We route and remind. Zero cost to us, high perceived value, strengthens the "we're not selling you anything" posture. |
| **Cash-pay** | **PGx panel**, comprehensive wellness/hormone labs, advanced cognitive screening | Employee, HSA/FSA-eligible | The revenue line. Same rail as our existing consult pricing. |

The PGx clinical argument for the book (and it's a good one): a person can be
"treatment-resistant" on paper simply because they metabolize a drug too fast
or too slow. That's a cognitive, mood, and productivity problem masquerading as
a personality or performance problem — and it's identifiable once.

### E4. Hard constraints on the employer surface

The screening layer must not compromise the trust line the whole vertical rests
on: **the employer sees aggregate data only, never an individual's results —
not the mental-health screen, not a lab, not a score.** Individual results go
to the employee and their own physician. Every screening feature must be built
so that this is structurally true, not merely promised. This is also what keeps
the HR conversation simple.
